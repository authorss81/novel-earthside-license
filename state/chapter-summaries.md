**Chapter 0301 — A Notice In A Hand.** Day 301, a Monday, the forty-sixth day of the Short Month. A lane past two gates, the shut gate from about the seventh hour, then a room over a market to about the ninth. **The woman of about twenty-nine who keeps a public register walks up a lane nobody sent her up, and a barrow of boards is standing against the wall and has been standing there for five days counting the day it was put there as one of the five, and the arrangement for taking them up was for a Thursday, and the Thursday is in three days.** She writes a question in her own hand, in writing, to everybody who will be in the room on the fiftieth of that month, four days off, **unsigned, with no name at the top of it**, and leaves it on the gate wall under a stone off a doorstep, and does not call at a door. **The woman of about fifty at the gate, who has not been asked anything by anybody in nine years, is not asked anything, and says four things out loud to a post and to a barrow of boards and to nobody, and one of them is that she has not read the sheet.** At about the ninth hour a slate comes up in the room over the market with three things on it and the third is four lines long and says the ground on that lane has to be settled before the season turns. **The register-keeper says out loud that she cannot say where the line runs and that she is not going to look at it, and nobody in the room asks her why, and a man of about thirty-one who wants to ask her why more than anybody in the room does not.** Change: the volume's central pressure is on a page and nobody in the room can answer it.

**Chapter 0302 — A Slate With Three Things On It.** Day 302, a Tuesday, the forty-seventh. The room over a market, the sixth hour to the fourth. Three things on a slate: the return for the quarter, the case that came up the river in a cart with a docket on it and has not been opened, and four lines about ground. **A man of about thirty-one who carries things for a living is in the room for two sections with a box of nails and twice interrupts by naming a fact about the building — a wet joist at the north corner and a sill that has never been repaired.** A boundary in this world is settled by two parties agreeing where it runs or by nobody walking it, and there is no third way. **The register-keeper is asked the word in the third place of the Crown's page and says before the question is finished that she is not going to define it, and stops there, and the word is not defined in this chapter.** The room does the return and leaves the other two where they are, and the man of about thirty-one puts his hand on the table and says the room has been standing on the wrong side of a thing since before the thirty-ninth of that month, and says he will not say what the thing is until the deed is read. **Chapter 0303 — The Deed, And A Sheet Attached To It.** Day 303, a Wednesday, the forty-eighth. Two rooms and a stair, the sixth hour to the ninth. **A man of about forty-three who keeps other people's papers takes a pin out of the corner of a deed and separates a sheet that has been pinned to it for as long as he has had the box, which is eleven years.** He reads both out in the local language in daylight with a date in chalk on the door to about nine people, and says what is on them and not what he thinks is on them. The deed is about forty years ago and says where the boundary of what was bought is in the words of a house four hundred miles inland. **The sheet is a clerk's copy of an entry in a public register in a town four hundred miles inland, entered a hundred and forty years before the deed was written, copied about forty years ago, and nobody in this city can go and look at the page it was copied from.** It names nineteen stones; eight of them are different, further down the road and in a different order. **Adrian is in the room once, named once by the woman counting what is read out, reading the clerk's hand over the man's shoulder, and is not asked one question about the boundary.** A woman of about thirty-three asks about the order the stones are in and is told that is the order the page has them in and that nobody knows whose order it is. **The eight names at the foot of the copy are not read, and the chapter is about the not reading, and it does not say why.** Change: both papers are true and the room has been treating one of them as a mistake since it walked in.

**Chapter 0305 — A Tin, And A Question In A Woman's Own Words. The batch's midpoint.** Day 305, a Friday, the fiftieth. **Chapter 0306 — A Ditch, A Channel, And Two Pair Of Boots.** Day 306, a Saturday, the fifty-first. Eleven miles of flats and a channel, the fifth hour to the seventh. **Chapter 0307 — Four Times A Year Since Before The Water.** Day 307, a Sunday, the fifty-second. **Chapter 0308 — Four Hundred And Forty, And The Only Page It Is On.** Day 308, a Monday, the fifty-third. Change: about four hundred and forty people are on a number in one man's head and on no page anybody else holds, and the only correct paper in this city would fill the Crown's empty place with them without anybody deciding to.

**Chapter 0309 — Six Days Left In It, And Nothing After It. The hinge.** Day 309, a Tuesday, the fifty-fourth. **Chapter 0310 — A Word, In A Room, In Daylight, With A Date On The Door. The batch's climax.** Day 310, a Wednesday, the fifty-fifth. A room over a market, the sixth hour to the fourth, and the light comes in over a step at the back with no name on it and stops about the middle of the floor. **A man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall and wrote the ninth line on a wall nine miles up the road in a harder piece of chalk is asked a question about a mark of about a foot and about two inches, and the figures came to the room from a man with a barrow who is not in it.** He is asked to say the word, and **he says it, in one word, in the local language, in daylight, with a date in chalk on the door, and the word is *scar*, and the chapter does not define it and does not say what it means.** He then says four sentences: it is not a path, it is not a watercourse, it is not a furrow, it is not a boundary, the mark is not a pattern, and he is not going to say what it is because he has found out in the last fifty days what a word on a page is for and is not going to find out twice. **He is asked to say it again so the register-keeper can get it right, and does not, and she gets it right, and the chapter says so and does not say how.** The word goes on her second slate in chalk with the date beside it and no name on the line, at his asking, because a line with a name at the top of it is a line somebody can come back to. The chalk that writes it is the piece of chalk nobody was going to use, which has lain on that table since the forty-third day of that month. Nobody in the room asked him about the ninth line. He is frightened and nobody can tell, and at the end he says in a mouth that he has found a way to rub a word out that has nothing to do with a cloth or a wall, and that it is to say it once in a room with a date on the door, and that he has done it about nine times in his own head this afternoon. Change: **there is now a word in this city for a thing about nine hundred paces off a shut gate, and a word in a city is the cheapest thing anybody has ever bought, and the man who bought it does not know what he sold.**

**Chapter 0311 — Nine Places, And A Sheet With No Day On It.** Day 311, a Thursday, the fifty-sixth of the Short Month. A road out of the city, the sixth to the ninth hour, and the hands are on a folded sheet of road with about nine places on it and a lump of chalk carried in one of the folds and nothing else. **Tamsin Quill is on a page for the first time since the seventeenth day of the Short Month, and she is on it working, and she is on a road and not in the room over a market, because the two women of twenty-nine are never in one room.** Her second handle is invented here and in no other chapter: **a folded sheet of road with about nine places on it, folded the same way every time — long way in half, then in half again across, and the corner that came last goes under — and the reason she gives once is that a sheet folded some other way can be taken for a notice, and a notice has a day on it, and hers has not got a day on it and is not going to get one.** She walked nine places in three days asking one question at each, *what is this ground called, and who called it that*, and got eight answers. Seven of the eight are the two names the two documents use, written seven different ways. The eighth is **the nine acres**, and it came from a woman of about thirty-three behind a bread board at a turning where four streets come together, who said that nobody has ever been asked and that it is not a name anybody writes down because there is nowhere to write it. The ninth place has eight houses in it and nobody came to a door and **she did not go back, because going back is how a person turns a question into a pressure.** The ninth line is left empty and not written *no answer*, because an empty line can be filled in and a line that says *no answer* can be argued with. **Adrian is named once, by her, on a road, and is not asked one question, and the consent fracture is not mended and neither of them says a word about why, and the reason is nine years old and it is hers as much as his.** Change: a third name for a piece of ground is on a page in four hundred miles, and it came from a person and not from a document, and the sheet that carries it has no day and no name on it and eight households have asked to keep it.

**Chapter 0312 — The Fifty-Seventh, And A Sheet That Came Down.** Day 312, a Friday, the fifty-seventh of the Short Month. A lane, a shut gate, and **the woman of about fifty at the shut gate is in this chapter and is asked nothing in it and nobody was sent to her.** Her five handles are the same five: a brass key on a tarred cord in the apron pocket, a bar of ash in two brackets, a wooden hook, a piece of chalk, and a date in chalk on the post every morning with nothing rubbed out. The barrow of boards is still against the wall, where it has been since the forty-first of that month counting that day as one of them. The asking the register-keeper put up on the wall on the forty-sixth is still on it, and the room it was written for was on the fiftieth. **The wind took it off the wall at about the second hour of the afternoon and it went into the wet at the foot of it, and she picked it up and read it, having said out loud on the forty-sixth that she had not read it and was not going to, and that reading it would be the beginning of having been asked.** The back of that sheet is blank, and a second sheet in this city has a question on the back of it in another hand, and she has not seen it and is not going to. **She puts it back up and leaves it up and says the two reasons out loud at a post: a paper with nothing at the top of it cannot be taken off a wall on behalf of a person, and there is nobody to take it off for.** The man of about thirty-eight with the barrow comes down the lane at about the fifth hour, does not stop, puts his hand flat on the boards, takes it off, and goes on down. **She says out loud that she has read it and has not been asked and that the two are separate facts and she is keeping them apart on purpose.** Change: the only decision anybody has made about a page on that lane in about a month was made to a post, and it was to leave a thing where it was.

**Chapter 0313 — What A Notary Is For, And What She Is Not Going To Do Today.** Day 313, a Saturday, the fifty-eighth of the Short Month. A room over a market, the seventh hour to the ninth, and **Sera Vail, a notary of fifty, twenty-six years a notary, is in the room and her bag is shut on the table all day.** The register-keeper asks her for **a day** and not a month, and about four people in that room have worked out that the difference between the two asks is the whole of a week. The notary names the cost out loud first, as she does: her name at the top of it, the copy in every room four hundred miles off that has a table and a clerk's habit, and if the days do not stop where the word on it stops then the paper is wrong and she is the wrong; **and the second part, which is that yesterday morning she had a form and this morning she has not, and she has not told anybody that in nine years, and she is telling it because she is about to write a form again.** A man of about forty-three asks the difference between entering a form and making one and is told **twenty-six years and one word**, and that neither is what is being asked, because the day after the sixtieth is the only day a form belongs on and she is not going to make one on a day she has a form for. **She will not spend one of the nine days of writing she has left on the only thing anybody has asked her for this month.** The man of about fifty-seven, with a length of new rope he has not used, says nothing and has been answered at length four days ago and not answered at all this morning. **Adrian is named once by the register-keeper, is reading the clerk's hand on the sheet pinned to the corner of the deed, and is asked nothing.** He says that the copy is the only page in four hundred miles on which anybody has written down the shape of a form and that nobody copied the shape, only the words, and that he is not going to make it an argument because a room where a woman is going to be asked to make one in three days is not a room for his argument. Change: the room has a day to work towards, and the day of the week is a Tuesday, and the notary will be in the room on it and will not say in advance what she wants on the door.

**Chapter 0314 — The Column That Is Not There.** Day 314, a Sunday, the fifty-ninth of the Short Month. A room over a market, the seventh hour to the fifth, and the hands are on a register with nine years in it, a second slate, a box with a deed in it and a sheet pinned to the corner of the deed, and a waxed slate in a case. **The discovery of the chapter is a missing column, and it is found by a person in a room in daylight with a date on the door, and it is about the four days of notice.** The register-keeper opens her book at a page about forty days back on purpose and says she has kept it three columns — what was asked, what came back, and the day — for nine years and has never wanted a fourth and wanted one on Tuesday. **Hettis Roke puts a waxed slate on the table face up and then turns it face down and says the day a sheet went out is not in any of her three columns, and that this is why she cannot check it and he has known it for nineteen years and has never wanted to be able to.** He refuses the room his roll outright, says its shape out loud, and asks that his refusal be entered. The man of about forty-three says that he has the column she wants, in a book, on a shelf, in a room he lets nobody into, **and has never read it back in eleven years, because if he did he would find nine mistakes and then have to decide what to do about nine, and he has never wanted to decide what to do about anything on a Sunday.** Adrian is named once, reading the register over her shoulder, and is asked nothing. The finding itself is the man of about forty-three's: **you are not missing a column for the day the sheet went out, you are missing it because of the four days, and every answer in your nine years is attached to the day you heard it and not the day you asked.** The register-keeper will neither put a fourth column in nor refuse one, and says both out loud. **Chapter 0315 — A Date On A Plain Slate, Read Out Loud.** Day 315, a Monday, **the sixtieth and last day of the Short Month.** A room over a market, before the light to the fifth, and the hands are on a plain slate, a lump of chalk, a cloth, and about nine sheets with nothing at the top of any of them. **A girl of seventeen, on no roll of anything, who has copied every asking anybody has written in this flood and has signed none of them, writes her name at the top of the slate before she writes anything else on it, in the ordinary way, and says the reason out loud: because of the habit, and it is her slate and nobody has ever asked her for it.** The register-keeper, who has not put a name at the top of a sheet for four years, looks at the name and **does not ask for it to be taken off, and never says so out loud to anybody.** The girl copies the last asking, wipes the slate and copies it again, because a thing that is going to be read out in a room has to be read off a copy and not off the paper it came on, and she has never explained that to anybody and nobody has ever asked her. The sixtieth goes on the second slate in chalk and nothing goes under it, and she is asked to read it out, and she looks at the slate for the length of four words deciding whether reading counts as being asked, and decides that it does not, because reading is not answering. **She reads the sixtieth day of the Short Month out loud, and that it is the last one of those, and that there is a Monday on the end of it and the next one is a Tuesday, and she is made to say the second part again and slower, and the register-keeper says she wants the whole room to have it and not just the two of them.** Nobody explained it to her. The man of about forty-three, the only person in that room who could have said something about a name at the top of a page, says nothing at all. She asks the only question she asks in that room — what am I to copy for tomorrow — and **nobody answers, and she is on no roll of anything and there is no address at which a sheet could be put in front of her, and being on no roll of anything is not a qualification and nothing in this month has turned it into one.** Change: the last date this city can write in a form that exists goes on a slate with a girl's name at the top of it, and nobody corrected it.

**Chapter 0316 — A Name, In A Room, In Daylight, With A Number On The Door.** Day 316, a Tuesday, **the first day of a month this city had not got.** A room over a market, the seventh hour to the ninth, and **this is the chapter the whole hinge of Volume 06 was waiting for, and the name goes on a page here for the first time in this manuscript.** The door has no month on it: the register-keeper wrote a count of the days of the flood on it in chalk herself, alone, at the seventh hour, and said the reason out loud before anybody asked — a number a person put on a door is a date until somebody puts a better one on it, and if she is wrong about it then every day after is wrong on the same count, and nobody in four hundred miles can check it. **She gave up a blank space on a door, which is the one thing in this flood nobody has ever had to explain, so that a notary could see what a room looks like when it has nothing.** The notary names the cost again in one sentence, and is told **do it, and I am not going to argue with the cost, because you named it before you were asked.** She rules a line at the top of a sheet and begins. **The man of about fifty-seven stands up off a wall and says one word and refuses her, and not one of his four sentences is about the calendar: a word made in a room is a word somebody can be asked to have made, and he is the man who will be asked, because there is nobody else in that room who could be asked about a boundary and be asked about it honestly.** He says he is not angry and nobody argues with him. She puts her hand flat on the sheet, waits for the light to reach the table, and says, once, in the local language, in daylight, in a room with a number on the door: **the Bare Month.** Nobody asks her a second question about it. He asks how long it is and she declines in front of about nine people and asks for the declining to be entered as hers. The man of about forty-three asks why and she gives a reason that is hers: **she has put a number at the head of a month once, the days did not stop where the number stopped, nobody found that out from the paper, they found it out from the days, and she is not going to guess at a number again in front of about nine people.** What she offers instead is that she will write every day of it that she is asked to write, and that when a clerk writes to her about a date he cannot place she will be able to tell him how many there have been and will not have told him in advance. **The form is five lines with a notary's name under the first, and it is the first page in four hundred miles in about a month with a person's name at the top of it in a room, and the person is a notary of fifty and not a household.** She rubs out the count on the door with a cloth and writes the date in the new word, and the register-keeper writes the same on the second slate. **The man of about fifty-seven does not leave, and at the end asks whether the first day of it is a Tuesday, and is told yes, and says nothing else. He did not know until that morning that the days of the week were still standing, and they are the one instrument in this flood nobody had to make up, and nobody wrote it down.** Change: this city can write a date again, and the month has no number on it, and the only man in the room the new month costs anything to is the man who refused it, and nobody has told him he is wrong. **Adrian is in the room and is in no chapter in any form, and the chapter does not say so.**
