## THE STAGING OF 981, 985 AND 990, THE THREE DAYS ADRIAN VALE IS IN

**ADRIAN VALE, Stage 2, AND ON 981 HE IS AT THE SHEET.** Both his own hands flat on it, the wood under his wrists, from the
fourth hour, with nothing on it. **What he wanted was to see one thing written about himself before it was put to anybody so
that he could read it and know it was on its way. He got no:** the first thing that was ever written down before it was put to
anybody in this manuscript was written about the woman of about sixty-nine with her tin and was about whether there was a fire
for her at the end of that row, and there was nothing in those three lines about him. **No mouth in that room put anything to
him, nobody said his name, nobody in that room was waiting for him to be useful, and his own two hands were of no use to any of
them.** **He is the cause of one thing that happens to somebody else:** she wrote the three lines a hand's width in from the
right edge of that sheet because his own two hands were on the wood at the left of it, and she did not ask him to move them
and he did not move them.

**ADRIAN VALE, Stage 2, AND ON 985 HE IS AT THE SHELF.** Both his own hands flat on the front edge of the shelf at the height
of a person's shoulder, where the second book stands a foot to his own left. **What he wanted was for the word under the day
cut across the head of that second slate to be read out in that room by somebody who was not him. He got it, and it was not
the word he had expected, and nobody in that room improved on it, and the chapter does not say what it means.** That is the
one obtained thing of this volume's nine Adrian days. **No mouth in that room said his name, nobody was waiting for him to be
of any use, and he was of no use to any of them.** **He is the cause of one thing that happens to somebody else:** his own
shoulder is in the way of the light coming in at that window across the shelf, and the keeper works at her own column from the
foot of it up for the rest of the morning instead of from the head of it down, which is the wrong way round for a column of
figures and takes her about a quarter of an hour longer.

**ADRIAN VALE, Stage 2, AND ON 990 HE IS AT THE BENCH WITH THE BACK OF HIS OWN HAND ON THE SHEET.** The back of his own right
hand on that sheet from the fifth hour and his own left hand flat on the wood of the bench a hand's width off from it, and the
register open between them with the column that holds figures showing. **What he wanted was for the seventh stroke to go into
that column, and it is not his to enter and never will be, and no seventh stroke goes into any column of either book on any of
the fifty days of this volume.** **No mouth in that room said his name, nobody was waiting for him to be useful, and his own
two hands were of no use to any of them.** **He is the cause of one thing that happens to somebody else:** at about the tenth
hour his own shoulder is in the way of the wood of that bench where the keeper's own hand wanted to go to lift the topmost
page of her own leaf, and she lays the dry cloth on the head of the second book's leaf instead of on the head of her own.

**THE WOMAN OF ABOUT FIFTY-TWO.** **She has not been back to her own far wall since 971 and is at the far end of that bench
on every day of these ten at which she is in that room**, with her own shoulder to the wood behind the far end of it. On 981
she took no part in the morning at all. On 983 she went across that room at about the seventh hour and stood in front of that
block about four minutes and came back and stood where she had been. On 988 she said one thing out loud across about nine feet
of that room to the stallholder about the stool she has carried up that stair every morning since the water came and has never
sat on, **and he did not stop and did not answer and did not look up.** On 989 she named the cost out loud first in her own
mouth and then put one thing to the man who came up that stair about those six strokes and whether anybody knew whose lines
they were, **and he answered and the chapter prints nothing of what he said.** On 988 and on 989 she goes out at the middle of
the afternoon and along that row and out of that market, and the stool stands where she left it.

**THE MAN OF ABOUT THIRTY-NINE WHO TRADES ON A BOARD, the tradesman.** Comes up at the fifth hour, goes along the face of his
own board with the cloth, and the cloth stops at the foot of the middle column where it stops every morning. **On 984 he goes
at it four times and it stops in the same place four times, and he says out loud that he could shave it off with a blade and
is not going to, because he has a hand for cloth and has not got one for wood, and that there are nineteen rates on that
board worth more to him than the length of his own arm with the cloth on it; and at about the eleventh hour he lays the cloth
down on the face of his own board at the foot of that middle column and leaves it there.** **THE CLOTH IS HIS AND NOT THE
STALLHOLDER'S.** His nineteen rates and the two-finger space at the foot of the middle column were not written in on any of
these ten days, **and nobody in that row said one word about the space to anybody.**

**THE WOMAN OF ABOUT TWENTY-NINE WHO KEEPS A PUBLIC REGISTER, the keeper.** **On 981 she names the cost of her own decision
out loud first in her own mouth and about herself, before anything else that morning, and then takes a pencil out of the
pocket of her dress and writes three lines on a sheet.** **On 982 she says the second decision once in her own mouth and the
page carries the putting-to, the person and the day and carries nothing back, and nobody agrees with it, nobody argues with it,
nobody improves on one word of it and nobody is thanked for it.** **On 983 nothing whatever is done about the block on that
wall and no hand goes near it but the woman of about fifty-two's, and it does not.** **On 985 her own shoulder in the way of
the light costs her about a quarter of an hour on her own column.** **On 987 she comes down that stair at about the eighth hour
and stands at the top of that step and is asked one thing about the row and answers it in the ordinary course.** **On 990 she
lays the dry cloth on the head of the second book's leaf, and it is still there at the going of the light.** About nine people
were in that room on 975 and 983 and **no mouth in it reached any figure by asking anybody anything.**

## THE FOUR PEOPLE WHO ARE IN DAYS 981 TO 990 AND NOT IN THE ROOM

**THE MAN OF ABOUT THIRTY-FOUR WHO KEEPS A STALL TWO STALLS ALONG, the stallholder.** **HIS OWN BOARD IS FACE IN AGAINST THE
WOOD OF HIS OWN TRESTLE WITH THE BARE BACK OF IT TURNED OUT INTO THE ROW BEHIND HIS OWN SHOULDER, AND THE NINETEEN RATES AND
THE TWO-FINGER SPACE BELONG TO THE TRADESMAN TWO STALLS ALONG AND NOT TO HIM; EVERY ONE OF THESE TEN CHAPTERS STAGES THAT
CORRECTLY.** **On 987 he goes at the near foot of his own trestle on dry ground, and about an inch comes off the near side of
that foot and about half an inch off the other, in shavings the length of a finger, and the trestle stands exactly as it stood
and the foot of it is still gone into the flag it went down into.** **His own hand went flat over that inside breast pocket four
times on 987 and the chalk did not come out of it on any of these ten days, and on 988 he came up that stair, put his own thumb
at the head of the middle of his own board, went along the foot of it three times and did not answer the woman of about
fifty-two.**

**THE MAN OF ABOUT THIRTY-ONE WHO CARRIES THINGS FOR A LIVING.** **On 985 he asks the stallholder, out loud across that room,
whether the near corner of the walking space at his own trestle is still standing up on its edge, and is told that it is and
that it will go on standing up on its edge until the trestle goes over.** **On 988 he sets the satchel down on the flags
against the skirting about a foot to the left of where he put it down the day before, which is one of the two paler
satchel-shaped places along that near wall, and the flap of it stays down.** **The satchel was not opened on any of days 981
to 990 and no chapter of them says why he opens nothing he carries.**

## THE THREE MANNER RULES THIS VOLUME'S FIRST BATCH BROKE AND THAT HOLD, SO NOBODY HAS TO LEARN THEM AGAIN

**1. §17.5 — AN ATTRIBUTION DOES NOT RESTATE A PERSON'S WHOLE HANDLE EVERY TIME THAT PERSON SPEAKS.** Measured across days 981
to 990 the count of full-descriptor restatements is **0**, and **no person speaks twice in any chapter of these ten**, so the
rule is never tested on this batch. The second handles are **the keeper, the tradesman, the stallholder, the carrier and the
man of about thirty-eight**, and She and He where a person has been established in the chapter. **A descriptor belongs beside
the age and the trade on first appearance in a chapter and never instead of it.**

**2. PAST TENSE FOR A SCENE'S STATE, PRESENT TENSE FOR A GENERAL TRUTH.** A verb describing the state of a specific located
object on that morning is in the past. A verb describing how a material behaves stays in the present. **Days 981 to 990 were
written in that tense and converted nothing after the fact, and the gnomic present was not touched once.** The statements
about what a day always does and what a wall always holds are of the second kind and are correct.

**AND THE THIRD MANNER RULE, WHICH IS ABOUT THE BOLD SPEECH MARKER AND NOT ABOUT A PERSON.** Every speech in this volume is
printed as a bold speaker marker because §17.7 makes that marker the thing both speech instruments read. **The comma belongs
inside the closing quote and the tag belongs outside it**, so the form is `**"…," ** said the man of about thirty-nine`.
**Measured on days 981 to 990: seven speech paragraphs, seven closers, every closer `," **`, and 0 sites of the wrong form.
Volumes 17 and 18 already print it correctly and are the model.**
