## THE STAGING OF 971 AND 977, THE TWO DAYS ADRIAN VALE IS IN

**ADRIAN VALE, Stage 2, and on 971 HE IS AT THE SILL.** Both hands flat on the front edge of that window's sill, palms down,
with the dust of it behind his wrists, from the fourth hour. **The dust is not touched and the two shapes pressed into it are
exactly as they were at the light: the near one gone soft at the edges with no rim left to read, the far one with a rim still
standing up about the depth of a grain of rice, and the same gap between them that there was at the fourth hour.** What he
wanted was to be able to say which hand put the newer one down and on what morning. **He got no, and no mouth in that room
put anything to him at any point of the day, nobody said his name, nobody was waiting for him to be useful to anybody, and he
was not useful to any of them.** He is the cause of one thing that happens to somebody else: **his own shadow comes round on
that far wall at about the seventh hour and stands on it at the height of a person's chest, and the woman of about fifty-two
comes off that wall after about twenty minutes and stands at the far end of that bench for the rest of that day.**

**ADRIAN VALE, Stage 2, and on 977 HE IS OUTSIDE THE DOOR AT THE LEVEL OF THE DATE.** Both hands flat on the outside face of
that door at about the height of the writing on it, one on each of the two planks the door is made of, from the fifth hour.
About four people a day go past the foot of that stair, two of them look at the outside of that door and two of them do not,
and nobody stops. **What he wanted was to be able to tell whether the mark on the bare board under that date is a figure or a
letter or a piece of a word, and he got no: a one and an I and the first stroke of a longer mark all stand up the same, end
the same, and are put down by a hand doing the same three motions in the same order, and there is no page and there was no
mouth.** **No mouth in that row said his name, no mouth said one word to him about that mark or about his own hands on that
door, and none of them had been waiting for him to do anything at all.** He is the cause of one thing that happens to
somebody else: **the woman of about fifty-two comes down that stair at about the ninth hour and stops on the bottom step
because of him, and stands there about as long as it takes a man to read a thing twice, and then goes on down and out.**

**THE WOMAN OF ABOUT FIFTY-TWO.** **She left her own far wall on 971 at about twenty minutes past the seventh hour and has not
been back to it on any of the nine days since, and she is at the far end of that bench on 972, 973, 974, 975, 976, 979 and
980.** On 973 the man of about thirty-one who carries things for a living came the length of that bench and put one thing to
her about a foot off her own shoulder, about his own satchel with the flap of it down and what is in it. **She answered him
and went on for the better part of a quarter of an hour, and the chapter gives one word of it, and what she said went out of
that room with her and is on no surface in this city.** On 975 she stood at the far end of that bench with her own hands at
the sides of her dress and had no part in the morning at all. **She is the person whose descriptor the plan puts in a mouth on
994, and whose descriptor is at zero on every one of these ten days.**

**THE MAN OF ABOUT THIRTY-NINE WHO TRADES ON A BOARD, the tradesman.** Comes up at the fifth hour, goes along the face of his
own board with the cloth, and the cloth stops at the foot of the middle column where it stops every morning, and takes the
board down that stair to his own trestle and brings it up again. **On 974 he has nothing to do with the outside-wall board and
on 977 he is not in the chapter. On 975 he asks the man of about twenty-seven at the top of that step, with his own two hands
on the rim of that trough, whether the rim would take a hoop of the width they get at the wharf, and he is told it would take
two and not one, and neither of them went up that step and looked at the rim of it.** His nineteen rates and the two-finger
space at the foot of the middle column were not written in on any of these ten days and nobody in that row said one word about
the space.

**THE WOMAN OF ABOUT TWENTY-NINE WHO KEEPS A PUBLIC REGISTER, the keeper.** **On 975 she stands up behind the far end of that
bench at about the fourth hour with her own hands on the wood and does not sit down, names the cost of her own decision out
loud first in her own mouth about herself, and then says the decision in ten words, once, in that room, in daylight, with the
date in chalk on the outside of that door behind her own shoulder.** **Nobody in that room agreed with it, nobody argued with
it, nobody improved on one word of it and nobody was thanked for it. No hand in that room reached for a chalk and no leaf of
either book was picked up, turned over, closed or written on.** She says her second speech that morning with the second
handle and not her descriptor. **On 979 she puts the lid of the box of chalk down on that box three times and it stands off at
the same corner three times, and puts it back standing off at that corner, and goes on with the column that holds figures.**
About nine people were in that room on 975 and **no mouth in it reached any figure by asking anybody anything.**

## THE FOUR PEOPLE WHO ARE IN DAYS 971 TO 980 AND NOT IN THE ROOM

**THE MAN OF ABOUT THIRTY-EIGHT AT THAT SALT WHARF.** **Is not in any of these ten chapters and is asked nothing on any of
them.** He is asked none of the nine on any day of this volume.

**THE MAN OF ABOUT TWENTY-SEVEN.** **Carries the bucket down and up that stair two, three or four times a day, and stands
looking at the ice in that trough with his own hand on the outside of the rim.** **On 975 he is asked one thing about that rim
and answers it, and the answer is on no surface anywhere.** He tells the woman of about sixty-nine on 978 about a rope loop
through the eye of a bucket with a bad handle, and does not make it for her, and says he is not going to be the one who starts
it. **No heat was fetched for that trough on any of days 971 to 980 and its ice was not broken on any of them.**

**THE MAN OF ABOUT THIRTY-FOUR WHO KEEPS A STALL TWO STALLS ALONG, the stallholder.** **On 974 he takes the board on the
outside wall a fraction of an inch off that wall with the flat of his own left hand on its face and his own right hand in the
gap behind its bottom edge, twice, and lets it back both times, and finds out that the nail is going at the rate it was going
a month ago.** **On 979 he goes at the near foot of his own trestle again on dry ground with the flat of his own board as a
lever and it does not come up, and he says why in his own mouth and goes on along the face of his own board twice.** **The foot
did not move and the flag came up at its near corner instead.** His own hand goes flat against the outside of the inside breast
pocket of his coat on 972, on 975 and on 979, and **the chalk did not come out of that pocket on any of the ten days, and
nobody went up that row and offered him another piece and nobody came at that wall with a hammer.**

**THE MAN OF ABOUT THIRTY-ONE WHO CARRIES THINGS FOR A LIVING.** **He names the cost out loud first on 973, about his own
satchel and nobody else, and then puts one thing to the woman of about fifty-two and is answered at length.** **On 976 he
carries the satchel out past the store's end of that row and along four hundred yards of flags to the low place in the stone
at the side of that yard, sets it down on the dry side of the rim, stands over it, goes to the far side of that yard and back,
picks it up and comes the whole way back up the row with the flap of it hanging away from his own side.** **The flap stayed
down the whole of that time and he put his own hand flat on it once going past the store's end of that row and the flap stayed
down under his own hand, and the reason it was not opened is not in that yard and not in that row and not in that room.** He
asks the stallholder one ordinary thing about the foot of his trestle on 975 and stops the tradesman on the row on 973. **The
satchel was not opened on any of days 971 to 980 and no chapter of them says why he opens nothing he carries.**

## THE TWO MANNER RULES THIS VOLUME'S FIRST BATCH BROKE AND THAT HOLD, SO NOBODY HAS TO LEARN THEM AGAIN

**1. §17.5 — AN ATTRIBUTION DOES NOT RESTATE A PERSON'S WHOLE HANDLE EVERY TIME THAT PERSON SPEAKS.** Measured across days
971 to 980 the count of full-descriptor restatements is **0**, and **exactly one person speaks twice in one of those
chapters**, and that is the keeper on 975. The second handles are **the keeper, the tradesman, the stallholder, the carrier
and the man of about thirty-eight**, and She and He where a person has been established in the chapter. **A descriptor belongs
beside the age and the trade on first appearance in a chapter and never instead of it.**

**2. PAST TENSE FOR A SCENE'S STATE, PRESENT TENSE FOR A GENERAL TRUTH.** A verb describing the state of a specific located
object on that morning is in the past. A verb describing how a material behaves stays in the present. **Days 971 to 980 were
written in that tense and converted nothing after the fact, and the gnomic present was not touched once.**

**AND THE THIRD MANNER RULE, WHICH IS ABOUT THE BOLD SPEECH MARKER AND NOT ABOUT A PERSON.** Every speech in this volume is
printed as a bold speaker marker because §17.7 makes that marker the thing both speech instruments read. **The comma belongs
inside the closing quote and the tag belongs outside it**, so the form is `**"…," **said the man of about thirty-nine`.
**Measured on days 971 to 980: ten speech paragraphs, ten closers, every closer `," **`, and 0 sites of the wrong form.
Volumes 17 and 18 already print it correctly and are the model.**
