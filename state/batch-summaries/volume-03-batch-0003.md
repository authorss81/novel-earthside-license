# Volume 03, Batch 0003 — Chapters 121–130: Thirty-Three Days

Ten days. Days 121 to 130, the thirty-first to the fortieth day of the month after this one, and the third ten days of Volume 03. **This is the batch in which the narrowing happens.** A box that has been in a room with a door for five days cannot be opened and everybody has started standing near it, and a woman of about thirty-four works out that the thing this city has actually built in five days is a chair. A foreman of a day crew is not told anything, on purpose, and the reason is written on a slate with a date on it. A girl of seventeen writes her own name at the top of a page for the first time in a hundred and twenty-three days, and a man of thirty-two — the only person in this city who could have read it out to her — says no in about four seconds. Nobody in this city has an instrument for asking nine people whether they will go to a room nine hundred miles away, and the finding is that a hearing is a room and an asking is a kitchen. A page that ends a document is failed at for the sixth time in a room with no key in it, and a pale field offers to make one person the account of it, and a woman of about thirty-four who keeps a stall by the wharf says **no** out loud in about four seconds, and the count of refusals in a book kept at home moves from twenty-three to twenty-four for the second time in fourteen batches. A woman of seventy of a village of nine households is asked, properly, in a kitchen, on a day she did not choose, and says what a person's fourth refusal is, and it is a refusal with nothing under it, and it is held up by luck. **And Tamsin Quill stands in a room for the first time in seventy-nine days and says the thing she has been not saying for eight days, which is not about Adrian.** Nobody was asked, nine people said they would stand, and a hearing is in twenty-four days and it is not in these ten days. **The gains are a chair with no date on it, a name at the top of a page, a refusal, an asking, a kitchen, a tenth shape dated and named, and one line on a public roll. None of them is a power and the nearest thing to a power this batch produced is a sentence by a woman of seventy that is nine words long and has nothing under it.**

## 1. The batch's spine

1. **Day 121, a landing and a room with a door.** **THE BOX HAS BEEN UPSTAIRS FOR FIVE DAYS AND A HEARING IS IN THIRTY-THREE DAYS.** Somebody put a chair on the landing on the second day and nobody took it away. **The finding: a thing nobody is waiting for is a thing everybody is standing near, standing near a thing is a practice, and a practice is a toll — and the toll is that every person who stands near the box adds a line to the only account of it that will exist.** A notary of fifty's fifth asking is written out on a slate and not delivered, and the not-knowing is the entry, and nobody has asked her a sixth time.
2. **Day 122, the near gate and a fork nine miles up a road.** **A FOREMAN HAS NOT BEEN TOLD ANYTHING, AND THE FINDING IS THAT THE NOT-TELLING IS A PAGE.** A word can be carried and it was not carried, and the decision is on a slate with a date on it and no name on it. Then a page in a stone is found to be wrong and cannot be corrected, and the only correction available to anybody in four hundred miles is to ask the household to take it down, and nobody has ever asked that household anything. **Nine inches of stone is not a safe place for anything**, and it is the first thing anybody in this city has written a page about.
3. **Day 123, a room over a market, at the eighth hour.** **A NAME GOES ON SOMETHING.** A girl of seventeen puts her own name at the top of a nineteen-line description of a box, and it is the first time in a hundred and twenty-three days that a person here has put her own name on a page in a room in daylight, and the cost falls on her. **A man of thirty-two is asked to read it out and says no in about four seconds**, and she reads it out herself, badly, and nobody corrects her. The four-word question a notary asked four days earlier gets an answer, about the girl and not about the box.
4. **Day 124, two hundred steps of a river wall on a condemned rail.** **TWO EMPTY PLACES.** The twenty-fifth reading is not taken for the thirty-ninth day. **A man of thirty-one with a right hand that will not close comes down with a gauge in the crook of his arm**, and a man of about thirty-nine asks out loud whether the four can make a rule about a man, and the man of thirty-one says **I do not know** without being asked. A Hearthguard of forty-four stands on the landing and is not asked anything, for the fourteenth day in a row.
5. **Day 125, a room over a market, four hours.** **A HEARING IS A ROOM AND AN ASKING IS A KITCHEN.** There is no instrument for asking nine people whether they will go, because nine answers is a list, and a list is a removal with a column for where the person is. **The two people who could carry an answer anywhere are the two who may not ask**, and both of them work that out about themselves and neither of them worked it out before. A decision that nobody will be asked to ask goes on a slate with a date on it and no name and no list.
6. **Day 126, under a wall above a market, in the wrong weather.** **ONLY ARRIVING, SIXTY-EIGHT DAYS LATE AND EIGHT DAYS OLD.** A boy of nine's answer is **eight days old** and sixty-eight days late, and the sixty-eight is the number everybody knows and the eight is the number nobody is keeping. Two people find out they have both been saying the same two words to other people. The answer to his six words exists, is on a slate, and has nowhere to go. **The last beat is that he is asked five words and says one and says nothing about having been asked.**
7. **Day 127, a room at the back of the Open Hand. THE BATCH'S CLIMAX.** **THE SIXTH ROOM, HELD, AND FAILED, AND THE THIRTY-FIRST THING THE WALL HAS SAID, AND A REFUSAL THAT COST SOMEBODY SOMETHING.** Six attempts, the sixth of them *who wanted it to work*, and the answer is that nobody in this city ever did. A pale field offers to make one person the account of the page for good. **No.** The refusals go from twenty-three to twenty-four, the count at Aurel stays thirteen, and the difference is eleven.
8. **Day 128, a kitchen in a village of nine households.** **WHAT A PERSON'S FOURTH REFUSAL IS.** Asked properly, in a kitchen, in daylight, by a woman who keeps a stall by the wharf. **A person's fourth refusal is a refusal with nothing under it, and it is the strongest refusal this city has, and it is held up by luck**, and the first three were never hers and she has never been asked what they were. She asks for a page that is not a document. Nobody went to a fourth house and nobody stood outside one.
9. **Day 129, a converted salt store, about forty people.** **TAMSIN QUILL, AND THE TENTH SHAPE.** She has not stood in a room in seventy-nine days. She brings the eleven-line table she has carried in a coat for seven years and does not read it out, and says the top of line one once, and says the thing she has been not saying for eight days, which is that keeping a document out of a book for seven years is the single most consequential thing she has done in seven years. A man of forty-one in a very good coat says the one thing nobody else in the room can say. A count is written in a book and not read out. One line goes onto a public roll.
10. **Day 130, a room over a market, the ninth hour.** **TWENTY-FOUR DAYS.** What there is, gone through out loud, in a list of ten days with no names on it, and then the other half of the list: nine things this city has that are not anywhere, and every one of them is a person or a person's sentence. **The last image is a slate with a child's handwriting on the back of it and nothing on the front, and the case is upstairs, and the wall has said nothing.**

## 5. The rules established in this batch, binding on Batch 0004 and later

1. **Standing near a thing is a practice and a practice is a toll.** A thing that has been in a room with a door for five days and cannot be opened is not a thing anybody is waiting for; it is a thing everybody is standing near; and every person who stands near it adds a line to the only account of it that will exist. **The toll of a description is the people in it**, and that is the thirtieth offer's cost arriving late and continuously.
2. **A chair with no date on it is a page nobody can be angry at, and a page with a date on it can be taken down on a date, and a chair is the only thing in this city that is allowed to be nothing.** It is still on the landing outside the room with the door.
3. **A decision not to do something is a page, and this city has got very good at writing pages and has not once written one about itself until the thirty-second day of the month after this one.** Three pages about this city now exist, all in books that cannot travel, all with dates and no names.
4. **A page in a stone cannot be corrected, and the only correction available to anybody in four hundred miles is to ask the household that keeps it to take it down on a date, and nobody in this city has ever asked that household anything.** **Nine inches of stone is not a safe place for anything** and is the first page anybody in this city has written about itself.
5. **A name at the top of a page can be argued with, and a page nobody can be angry at is also a page nobody can be stopped by, and those are the same protection.** The first self-naming in a hundred and twenty-three days was done by a person on no roll, on a local page that cannot travel, and the cost fell on her and not on anybody else, **and the man who could have read it out to her, and who is the only person in this city who can read two systems of writing, said no in about four seconds and she read it out herself, badly, and nobody corrected her.**
6. **A notary's four-word question — *what is this about* — has been answered, by the person it was about, about herself and not about the object, and nobody counted what she said.** The page is about nine people and about the girl who wrote it, and that does not explain the box.
7. **A hearing is a room and an asking is a kitchen, and the only instrument this city has is a room, and a room is the wrong shape for an asking because nine people hearing an answer is eight other people hearing an answer.**
8. **The two people in this city who could carry an answer anywhere are the two people who may not do the asking, and both reasons are the same reason.** A person on no roster asking nine households a question is a person who is asking, and asking is what a chain is made of; and a man standing at the junction of two systems of writing is the person who will say what nine people meant, and a man who says what nine people meant is a description of nine people. **A man of thirty-two has entered that he has not asked one person one question in a hundred and twenty-nine days and wants it entered with the date on it.**
9. **Nothing will be written down about any answer, by anybody, in any book, on any board, in any stone, or in any column, and a girl of seventeen is going round the kitchens one at a time.** It is on a slate with a date on it and no name and no list. **And the cost of it is that if the number is eight on the day, nobody will ever be able to say who was not in the room.**
10. **A sentence becomes a name when two people have said it in one room, and the two words a boy of nine wrote are in about nine rooms in this city and none of those nine rooms has met any of the other eight, and the only thing standing between them and a name is that this city has not put its rooms together.** It is not a Crown name and it is in no book that office keeps, and a man of thirty-two checked in about four seconds on the day it was said.
11. **The answer to a boy's six words exists, is on a slate, is in no book, has not been repeated, and has nowhere to go.** A hearing is a room; a room is gone in a day; a slate goes in a book that travels; a stone nine miles up a road cannot be read; and four ears is not a record and is not nothing either. **It is on the slate because there is nowhere else and not because it is safe.**
12. **The sixth thing they tried is who wanted it to work, and the answer is that nobody in this city ever did, and that is why five rooms and then a sixth have not got anywhere, and that is not because the page is hard.** A description of a thing is made by people who want the thing to be different from what it is. **Six rooms, six attempts, the count of rooms on the page is six, the page stays shut, and the one sentence that came closest was not written down.**
13. **A page with a person at the top of it can be argued with and can be used against the person, and the reason a number with nobody at the top of it is the safest page this city has built is the same reason it cannot be corrected.** Both halves of that sentence are now on the page and they were not both on the page a hundred and twenty-three days ago.
14. **What a person's fourth refusal is: a refusal with nothing under it.** The first three were refusals of a document — a notice, a date, a channel, a term sheet — and the no went into the same thing as the notice. The fourth is a refusal of a person, and there is no document, and the only thing holding it up is a mark in chalk on nine doors that nobody in this city can wash off and nobody can explain. **It is the strongest refusal this city has and it is held up by luck, and a woman of seventy of that village has never in her life been asked what the first three were and has never asked any of the eight other houses either.**
15. **A wall in a room is read by about forty people and is not a column, and a column goes in a case.** The tenth shape has not been read out and is not going to be, and a woman who has carried it in a coat for seven years has entered that **keeping it out of a book has been the single most consequential thing she has done in seven years, and that it is why a woman of seventy had to carry a page down a road in a cart herself.** Both of those were correct. **Nobody improved on either.**
16. **A refusal of an offer is a thing that costs the person who makes it, and the cost is named out loud and entered.** The refusals in the second book a clerk of the Registry keeps at home are **twenty-four**, up by one, the second movement in fourteen batches, and the count at Aurel is thirteen, and the difference is eleven, and it was ten for about a hundred days, and it was nine before the first registered refusal, and it has never been made smaller.
17. **A man of thirty-one with a hand that will not close is the only person in a room who says *I do not know* out loud without being asked, and he said it on the thirty-fourth day of the month after this one about himself, which is the first time in a hundred and twenty-nine days that he has said it about himself.**
