# Premise

## Working identity

- **Title:** *The Earthside License*
- **Genre:** Isekai, game-lit, political fantasy, portal ensemble
- **Target length:** 900 chapters in 18 volumes of approximately 50 chapters
- **Point of view:** Primarily Adrian Vale, with occasional close chapters for the first cohort and named Veyran allies when a relationship or consequence cannot be seen through Adrian
- **Relationship policy:** One slow-burn romantic relationship: Adrian Vale and Tamsin Quill. It begins as professional friction, develops through shared work and conflict, and never replaces the larger political plot. No harem structure.
- **Tone:** Grounded fantasy with ordinary labor, bodily danger, political disagreement, and humane consequences. The System is a readable interface, not a cheat button or an excuse to abandon the world.

## One-sentence premise

A cynical game designer accidentally accepts the first Earthside License, is bodily transported into the real world of Veyra, and must learn that the “quests” granting his growing control over inter-world passage are being written by a faction trying to make history obey a single approved version.

## Logline

Adrian Vale knows how to design a fair game interface, but he has never had to live inside one. When an impossible license pulls him and a small group of real people through a threshold into Veyra, every choice he makes can open or close a door between worlds, rewrite a local law, or turn ordinary refugees into an army. To stop a hidden “version lock” from erasing inconvenient people and histories, Adrian has to give up the one thing he knows how to do well: control the system alone.

## The promise to the reader

1. **The game-like layer is real, but the world is not a game.** Players arrive in their own bodies. Injury, exhaustion, pregnancy, illness, death, and time lost to crossing are real. There is no respawn, save point, or automatic return.
2. **Every power creates a decision.** Adrian can ask the System for a permission, route, or passage, but local witnesses, physical anchors, and the consent of the receiving community determine whether it works.
3. **The players are not blank slates.** Earthside people bring their assumptions, work skills, family obligations, grief, prejudice, and hope. Veyrans are not quest-givers or scenery; they have ordinary routines, political interests, and a history that continues without Adrian.
4. **The central mystery is about authorship.** The System uses a human game grammar because it was built from human memories, but someone is using that grammar to select which histories of Veyra can become real.
5. **Growth changes Adrian’s obligations.** He begins by wanting a safe route home. He ends by helping build a bridge that neither world owns and giving up his ability to be its sole administrator.
6. **The eventual resolution is a stable bridge, not a perfect victory.** The official lie of “players” becomes a negotiated truth: people cross as named, consenting witnesses with duties and protections, not as disposable characters.

## Protagonist in brief

Adrian is a 32-year-old game designer at the fictional studio Morrowglass Interactive. He is good at seeing loopholes, balancing incentives, and explaining systems, but he uses that skill to avoid emotional responsibility. His external want is survival and a route home. His internal need is to accept that no one is a variable in his private design, including the people who depend on him. His central flaw is the belief that if he can name and measure a danger, he can remain separate from it. His deepest fear is becoming an expendable figure in a system that does not care whether he was the author.

## Central mystery

### Surface question

Why does the System know game rules, classes, names, and “quests” that no Earth player could have taught it, and why is it already changing Veyran history before Adrian understands the crossing?

### Hidden truth

The System is the visible portion of an ancient Veyran device, the **First Grammar**. The device was originally a way for several communities to witness and preserve a law during a catastrophe. A later attempt to preserve one official version of the law caused the **First Sundering**, tearing contradictions out of the world.

The Quiet Choir found a fragment of the device and learned to draw on echoes of human culture from the other side. Earlier crossings had already left traces in Veyran myth, and the first Earthside people supplied the familiar patterns the device used to make its safety language understandable. The current interface is therefore a translation and consent layer assembled from a cross-world archive, not a simulation created by the System’s own intelligence. Its game vocabulary is mostly Adrian’s own mental vocabulary after the crossing.

Ivenn Marrow, a Veyran scholar and custodian of the Quiet Choir, deliberately seeded false permissions and attractive “quests.” He wants Adrian’s modern design mind to complete a **Single Witness** version of the First Grammar: one authoritative history, one controlled crossing, and no contradictions that can produce another Sundering. Adrian’s accidental opening gives the Choir a chance to put a living designer inside the machine before the local communities can set terms.

The full mystery is resolved in `outline/ending.md`.

## Final resolution

Adrian rejects both obvious answers: he will not preserve the lie of a harmless game forever, and he will not impose the Choir’s clean single history. He publicly discloses the bridge and its human cost, then helps distribute the license among independent civic and community witnesses. The bridge survives only if Earthside governments, Veyran settlements, player families, and local custodians accept reciprocal duties, inspection, withdrawal rights, and the possibility of closure.

The emotional answer is that Adrian finally understands that a fair system is not one that gives its designer total control. It is one that makes power answerable to the people who live with its consequences.

## Antagonistic question

Every apparent “quest” asks: Who gets to call a person a player, a monster, a resource, or a witness? Adrian’s central antagonist is not simply a stronger creature or a larger army; it is anyone who treats that label as permission to use a life.

## Scope and non-negotiables

- The portal is physical, limited, expensive, and vulnerable to infrastructure. It is not a convenient teleport button.
- The System gives one readable prompt or rule at a time and can be incomplete, edited, or wrong.
- Veyra has ordinary people who do not become lore-dispensers when Adrian leaves.
- Adrian can fail, lose people, close a route, and lose access to abilities. He cannot solve every problem with a hidden class.
- The central Choir antagonist is introduced early and remains the final human antagonist. The final conflict uses the established First Grammar rather than introducing a new cosmic enemy.
- The ending resolves the immediate bridge conflict while leaving the broader question of other possible worlds intentionally open.
