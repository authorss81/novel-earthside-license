# Chapter 0894 — Said Out Loud For Him

The man of about thirty-one who carries things for a living came up the stair at about the sixth hour with a second man a step behind him, and the one by the door stopped just inside it with his hands empty at his sides while the light from the one window found his face.

It was Saturday, the five hundred and seventy-ninth day of the Bare Month. The morning was hard and blue with no cloud, and the frost lay in the shade of the row till the sun came round onto it. The man of about twenty-seven was already at the table leg with the bucket beside him when the stair sounded. The room over the market held full and most of those in it stayed on their feet. The man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall two stalls along sat with his own board on his knees. The man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board held the far end of the bench. The woman of about fifty-two kept the wall with her hands at the sides of her dress. Otto Marsh took the far side of the table his hands tucked behind him, one sleeve of his coat wet to the shoulder and the other sleeve dry. He stayed clear of the bucket's path. The woman of about twenty-nine who keeps a public register bent over the bench with the book open under her hand and the second slate waiting on the shelf behind her.

---

The man of about thirty-one had been in the way of the one by the door twice on the road in the wet, once going out with an empty satchel and once coming back with it full, and he stood beside him now and looked at the floor and not at the room.

**"—"**

He said it once, in his own mouth, into the middle of the floor, and the one by the door stood where he had stopped and did not look away from the window. The keeper kept her hand on the page and did not lift it, and the man of about thirty-four took his hands off his own board and set them flat on his knees. Otto Marsh did not turn round and did not go to meet the one by the door, and gave him no word. Nobody thanked him, nobody agreed with him and nobody argued, and not one of them bettered a word of what he had said.

The room decided nothing. The one by the door stayed by the door with his hands at his sides, and the light moved off his face and onto the bench.

**"He can stand where he is till the hour turns,"** said the woman of about fifty-two, by the wall.

**"He can,"** said the man of about thirty-nine, at the far end of the bench.

Each of the two said one plain thing and neither said more. The man of about twenty-seven carried the bucket to the window and back and set it down with the water flat in it. The woman of about fifty-two did not move from the wall. The one by the door put his back against the door frame and stayed there through the hour with his hands where they were.

---

Toward the tenth hour the shadow of the awning came up off the walking spaces and lay along the step. The one by the door was still inside the door where he had stopped, and the dust on his boots had dried pale where the wet had been, and he had not moved his hands from his sides at any hour of the morning.
