# Chapter 0852 — A Hand Went In And Came Out Empty

The man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall two stalls along had his right hand inside his coat and brought it out again with nothing in it, and the chalk was not in his hand when it came, and he went on with the edge of his own board under his thumb.

Saturday came in still and bright on the five hundred and thirty-seventh day of the Bare Month, with the date gone on to the outside of the door at the foot of the stair again in the morning and the bare piece of board under it standing a finger's width off the wood. The door stood open on its latch. The light came up the steps and lay along the top of them, and on that step the two sockets the iron belongs in stood open and empty with the dust lying undisturbed in the bottom of them, and the iron lay along the step beside them with nothing under it.

The man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board had his board under his arm at the far end of the bench. The woman of about twenty-nine who keeps a public register had the register open in front of her at the near end of it with her hand flat on the page. The window stood open on its hinge and the light off it lay along the middle of the bench. The woman of about fifty-two with her hands at the sides of her dress stood with her shoulder to the wall by the bench, and the man of about twenty-seven came up the stair with the bucket in his hand and the rag over the rim of it. The man of about thirty-one who carries things for a living came up behind him with his satchel under his arm and the flap of it down and stood by the window.

About four people a day went past the door. Some of them looked at the chalk on it and some of them did not, and the ones who did not kept on up the market row, and the ones who did stood a moment on the strip of ground and then went on as well, and not one of them went up the stair.

---

The man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board said it from the far end of the bench, and his hand came onto the face of the board as he said it and stayed there.

**"Somebody is putting that date on the door every morning."**

**"Somebody is."**

**"I could go along the row and ask at each trestle."**

**"You could,"** she said, and squared the page.

She neither told him to go nor told him to stay, and she did not thank him for the offer, and the man of about thirty-nine took his hand off the face of his board and looked out of the one window at the far end of the market and then put the hand back where it had been. The room went on with the light coming up the stairwell and the bucket by the leg of the table.

At about the fifth hour the man of about thirty-four said the one thing his board wanted. The rate at the foot of it had wanted a day against it since the middle of the week and his hand stayed in his coat while he said it, and the keeper turned the leaf back and turned it forward again and put her hand flat on it where it had been.

---

At about the eighth hour the man of about thirty-nine went down the stair with his board under his arm and out under the door and up the market row on his own business, and the room kept the bench and the page and the light going off the wood. At about the ninth hour he came back up and stood at the far end where he had stood and put the flat of his hand on the face of his board, and nobody in the room asked him where he had gone or what he had heard at any trestle he stopped at, and he said nothing about either.

The woman of about fifty-two moved along the bench to make room for the man of about thirty-four's board and said nothing while she did it. The man of about thirty-one went down the stair once with the satchel and came up again with the satchel under his arm and the flap of it down, and nobody asked him what was at the bottom of the stair or why he went. The man of about twenty-seven carried the bucket down and filled it at the trough and brought it up and set it by the leg of the table with the water in it standing a finger's breadth off level.

---

Near the end of the morning the man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall two stalls along got up off the bench and went down the stair to his trestle without his board and without saying why he had left it, and the board stood face in against the leg of the bench where he had had it propped, with the bare back of it turned out into the room and nothing on it but the dust of the flood.
