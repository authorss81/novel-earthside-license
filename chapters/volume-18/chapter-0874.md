# Chapter 0874 — The Same Words Again, And A Third Mouth

The thumb of the man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall two stalls along went along the foot of the board across his knees and stopped on the rate at the bottom of it, and the sentence came out of his mouth before his hand had finished stopping.

It was Sunday, the five hundred and fifty-ninth day of the Bare Month, and the weather had got up again off the flats and the light came in low under the awning at the store's end and never reached past the middle of that bench. The door at the bottom of the stair stood open on its latch, and outside it a day was in chalk, and the bare piece of board that goes under a date on it stood under the chalk as it has stood all this flood. The room was full and most of it was standing.

So the far end of that bench stood in a grey of its own, with the man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board in it at his board, and the near end of it stood in the light with the keeper's register open on the wood in front of her and the page squared against the fold. The man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall two stalls along was where the light stopped, with his own board across his knees and his thumb on the foot of it and the bare back of it turned out into the room. The woman of about fifty-two with her hands at the sides of her dress had the shade of the shelf and did not move out of it. The man of about twenty-seven was out of the room again with the bucket and the rag over its rim. At the one window the man of about thirty-one who carries things for a living stood with the satchel under his arm. The man with the one wet sleeve stood on the side of the table at the far end of the room with his coat over the back of the chair.

---

**"Nothing in this row wants saying twice."**

He put it into the middle of the floor and to nobody in particular, and the hand of the man of about thirty-nine at the far end of the bench did not come off the face of his own board and he did not turn round. The keeper did not turn a leaf. The woman of about fifty-two with her hands at the sides of her dress said nothing at all about it. The man of about thirty-one went down the stair with the satchel under his arm at about the sixth hour and came up at about the seventh hour with the flap of it down, and nobody asked him anything.

**"You said that before,"** said the man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall two stalls along, down at the board on his own knees.

**"You did not hear me say it before,"** said the woman of about twenty-nine who keeps a public register, with her hand on the page.

The room went on. The man of about twenty-seven took the bucket down the stair and up it again and stood it beside the table leg with the water standing flat in it, and the man of about thirty-nine stood at the far end of the bench and looked out of the one window at the far end of the row and said nothing else at any hour of the morning.

---

Near the end of the morning the man of about thirty-nine took his hand off the face of his own board and put the flat of it on the back of it and put the same sentence into the middle of the floor again, word for word, to nobody in particular.

**"Nothing in this row wants saying twice."**

The man of about thirty-four carried on with the rate at the foot of his board and put his thumb back where it had been. The keeper squared the page against the fold and put her hand on it. The woman of about fifty-two did not turn her head. Nobody in the room said they had heard it before, and nobody said a word about it at any hour of the rest of the day.

---

And then the man against the far wall on the side of the table came off the wall, and he said it.

**"Nothing in this row wants saying twice."**

He said it the way the other two had said it and with the same words and standing where he was, and nobody asked him where he had had it from, and nobody asked him one thing about it. The keeper's hand stayed on the page where it was. The man of about thirty-nine stood at the far end of the bench with his own hand on the back of his own board and did not turn round again.

At about the tenth hour the man of about thirty-four lifted his board off his knees, laid it flat down on the bench, and went out down the stair to his own trestle without it, and the light coming in low under the awning lay across the top of the bench and stopped at the edge of the place where he had been sitting, where the dust had been kept off the wood by the back of him all morning and was standing up plain and pale and hard along the outline of where he had gone.
