# Chapter 0862 — The Row Wanted Nothing Off That Door

A hand went flat on the outside of the door at the foot of the stair, under the date, and the chalk of the last stroke came off on the flat of it and left a grey smear across the second line of the day, and the man who put it there looked at the smear and wiped his hand down the side of his coat and went on up the market row.

It was Tuesday, the five hundred and forty-seventh day of the Bare Month. The rain had gone off in the night and left the row wet from end to end, and the flags of the store's end were beginning to come up in patches where the awnings had kept them dry. A day stood in chalk on the outside of the door and under it the bare piece of board stood off the wood at its near end with its top edge clear of the chalk above. Along the row the trestles were up and the boards were on them face in, and the only thing at this end of the market anybody stopped to read was the day chalked on it.

The man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall two stalls along stood at his own trestle with his board propped against the leg of it. The man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board was at the far end of the same row with his board under his arm. The man of about twenty-seven came up the row with his bucket and went past both of them to the step under the board on the wall.

---

Talk went along the row the way talk goes along a row, from one trestle to the next and back again, and what it was about by the third trestle was the door at the foot of the stair.

**"That door has stood open every morning of this flood and the row has never wanted anything off it."**

**"It has never wanted anything off it,"** said the man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board, and squared his board against his hip.

**"It has not,"** said the man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall two stalls along, **"and it is not going to start wanting on a Tuesday, and I would rather it stood open all day than had one thing asked of it."**

The man of about twenty-seven put his bucket down at the foot of the step and turned round and looked along the row at the two of them, and then he said the door at the foot of the stair was the only thing at this end of the market a person who cannot read a board would still stop for, and he said it to the bucket and not to either of them, and he picked the bucket up again and went on to the trough. Nobody took it up after him and nobody improved on it.

About four people a day went past that door. Some of them stopped in front of it long enough to read the day off the chalk and some of them did not stop at all, and the ones who stopped did not stop for long, and not one of them went up the stair.

---

The keeper came down at about the seventh hour to look at the flags at the store's end and stood a while with her hand on the frame of the door and read the date across it. The man of about thirty-four went past her and went on along the row and left his board where it was. At about the ninth hour the man of about thirty-nine put his own board down face in against the leg of his trestle and stood with his hand flat on the back of it and looked at the door the whole length of the row and said nothing. At about the same hour the man of about twenty-seven came up the stair with his bucket, and the woman of about fifty-two with her hands at the sides of her dress stood with her face turned to the light at the near end of that bench and looked at the two of them looking at it from outside.

The room went on. The man of about thirty-four came up about the tenth hour, dry to the shoulder from the row, and did not say where he had been standing or what he had said there, and nobody asked him.

---

The light went off the awning at the store's end about an hour before the light went off the row, and where it had been letting go of the whole length of the awning all morning it began to come down in one sheet at a time instead, and one of those sheets went along the flags and stopped against the bottom of the door at the foot of the stair and stood there in a line a finger wide, and the man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall two stalls along came past it on his way down and stepped over the line and did not look down at it.
