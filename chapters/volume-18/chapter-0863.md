# Chapter 0863 — The Trestle Leg Would Not Stand Square

Both hands went under the foot of the near leg of the trestle at the store's end of the market row and lifted, and the grit which had packed itself into the socket of it came up in one piece about the size of a fist and stood in his palm, and the leg came up half an inch out of the hole it had made and would go back no further than it was.

It was Wednesday, the five hundred and forty-eighth day of the Bare Month, and it was a bright cold morning with the row drying from the store's end back. The trestle stood with its near end out of square by a finger's width, so a board laid flat on it rocked, and a person working at it had to keep a hand on the leg or the whole thing would walk out of its own wet bed across the flags. The trough at the top of the step was running a little water. A board on the wall above a step showed three sets of figures and nothing written under any of them. Farther down the row the man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board came up to his own trestle and set his board face in against the leg of it and stood beside it, and nothing was traded at any trestle at this end of the market for the fourth morning running.

The man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall two stalls along got the leg square twice in the morning, and it went off square both times as soon as he let go of it, and he would not put a stone under the foot of it because a stone under a leg makes the boards on a trestle hum.

---

**"It wants a wedge,"** said the man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall two stalls along, to nobody, with both hands under the leg again.

The man of about twenty-seven came up the row with the bucket in his hand and the rag over the rim of it, stopped, looked down at the leg, and went on past it to the trough. The woman of about twenty-nine who keeps a public register came down to the row at the step under the board on the wall and read the figures on it from the bottom up and did not come over to where the trestle was.

**"You have the whole morning for it,"** she said.

**"The whole morning will not hold it,"** said the man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall two stalls along, and he took his hands off the leg and showed her the palms of them and went on laying a board flat on the trestle with the near end held down by his hip.

---

He laid his boards on the trestle through the whole morning and they rocked every one of them. A board laid flat and let go of would slide about a finger's width towards the near end before it stopped, which is what a board does on a trestle which is not square, and he laid the next one on top of it and it went the same way, and the course went up crooked and stayed crooked. The woman of about twenty-nine who keeps a public register went back up the stair with the register under her arm and did not fetch anything for him, and the man of about twenty-seven went down the stair with his bucket and up again with it and did not stop at the trestle the second time.

At about the ninth hour the man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board walked past the store's end with his board under his arm, looked at the near end of the trestle going out of square as he went, and kept on up the row and said nothing to anybody about it, and nobody said anything to him.

The leg went back into its hole in the end and stood up out of square, and the boards on the trestle were crooked the way they were, and no wedge was fetched and nobody said out loud where one would be kept.

---

By the tenth hour the wet grit round the foot of the leg had been pressed down into a ring by the rocking of it, and the ring stood clear of the wood the whole way round, and on the side of the leg where the weather came off the awning the ring was packed hard, and on the side away from it the grit was still soft and had not taken the shape of anything yet.
