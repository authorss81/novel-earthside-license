# Chapter 0853 — Ice An Inch Deep And Nobody Broke It

Two hands were laid flat on the rim of the trough at the top of the market step with the ice standing an inch deep in it, and they stayed on the rim, and the ice did not give under them.

It was a Sunday morning before the light was on the flags, the five hundred and thirty-eighth day of the Bare Month, and the trough stood at the top of the step with its whole length frozen over and the box of chalk on the stone beside it with its lid standing at the angle it always stood at. Above the step the board on two nails carried its three sets of figures with nothing written under any of them. The date in chalk stood on the outside of the door at the back of the store's end of the market, and the bare piece of board under it had nothing on it whatever.

Adrian Vale stood at the trough with his hands where they were. He wanted the ice out of it before the fourth hour so the bucket could be filled out of it, and he had been at it since the dark with nothing to break the ice with, and his hands on the rim had not gone into the water. A hard night had put the inch in there and the sun had not come over the store's end yet to take it out, and a hand is not a thing that ice comes out of.

---

He took his hands off the rim at about the fourth hour and stood back from it and turned his palms over and looked at them, and the two marks on them were the white patches the cold of the stone had left. He did not go up the stair. He stood at the top of the step with his hands at his sides and watched the row come up.

Over at the far side of the row the man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall two stalls along stood at his own trestle with his board face in against it and the bare back of it out into the row behind his shoulder. The man of about twenty-seven came up the row with the bucket in his right hand and the rag over the rim of it, and he could not fill it.

**"It is hard all along,"** the man of about twenty-seven said, and put his fingers on the ice and took them off again.

**"It will not come out for a hand."**

**"It will come out for the back of a knife,"** said the man of about thirty-four, and did not move from his trestle.

**"Then it can wait for the knife,"** the man of about twenty-seven said, and he said it without turning round to anybody, and he stood a while longer at the trough with his bucket in his hand.

Adrian Vale went up the market row out of the way of it and stood at the store's end with his hands hanging at his sides, and the row came past him over the step and on up the market the way it does.

---

The man of about twenty-seven took his bucket round by the side of the yard to the low place in the stone, where the water stood always in the dip of it, and filled it there, and it was a longer carry than the trough was. He wrung the rag out over the bucket and put it over the rim again, and he went on with the work that belonged to him: he washed the sill of the store's door and the stone at the side of the step, and he did not wash the wall he had washed once and had not washed since.

He carried the bucket up the market row against his hip with a hand at the rim to keep it level, and the water came over the rim and ran down the front of his coat and along the flags in a line going up the row, and he set the bucket down by the leg of the table in the room above with a hand's depth in it.

Adrian Vale went up the stair after him and stood at the near end of the bench with his hands open at his sides, and the man of about twenty-seven took his bucket past him and set it down by the leg of the table without looking at him and without saying anything about the trough at the top of the step.

---

By the tenth hour the water in the low place in the stone at the side of the yard had gone down and left a wet ring round the dip of it, and the ring was the shape of a bucket standing there through a morning, and the edge of the ring was drying at its inner side where the shade of the store had come off the stone.
