# Chapter 0875 — He Stood His Own Board Up In His Row

Two hands went under the board of the man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board and lifted it off the leg of the bench, and he turned it end for end in the air the way a man turns a thing he knows the weight of, and the face of it came up and the bare back of it went down toward the flags.

It was Monday, the five hundred and sixty day of the Bare Month, and the light came straight in under the awning at the store's end and lay along that bench from end to end. Below the room the door stood open on its latch and the chalk on the outside of it carried a day, and below the chalk a bare board about as wide as a hand stood clear of the wood at its near end by a finger's width. The room above the market was full and most of it was standing, and nobody in it had been sent to be in it.

From the bottom of that stair with the door standing open, the light of the morning came up the steps and stopped at the bench. The keeper sat at the near end of the bench with the register open on the wood in front of her and her hand flat down on the page. At the middle of the bench the man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall two stalls along had his board lying across his knees with its bare back toward the room. The woman of about fifty-two with her hands at the sides of her dress took her standing place at the wall side of the bench where the sill put a strip of light on the floor, and the man of about twenty-seven had the bucket at his own feet on the flags, the rag across its rim. The man of about thirty-one who carries things for a living had got himself the one window, and he was carrying the satchel under his arm with its flap hanging away from his side. By the far wall on the side of the table the man with the one wet sleeve stood, and his coat lay over the back of the chair at the far side of the room.

---

At about the fifth hour the man of about thirty-nine carried the board down the stair and out under the door and up his own row, and the top of his trestle had two notches cut in it near the ends where the feet of a board stand, and the wood inside them had gone pale and hard where the weather could not get at it. He stood the board up in those two notches and turned it so the face of it came out into the row, where the trestles stand in a line and the flags go up between them and about four people a day walk past on their own business.

The face of it had rates on it and the rates were in their places, and it went up into that row in the weather the way any other thing in the row goes up in the weather, and it was out.

---

At about the seventh hour he came back up the stair without it and stood at the far end of the bench where he stood every morning, and a while after him the man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall two stalls along came in from the row and put his own board down across his knees and looked along the bench at the far end of it and said nothing about it. At about the eighth hour the man of about thirty-nine put his right hand flat on the wood of the bench at the end of it and said the cost of it out loud first, in his own mouth, into the middle of the floor.

**"—"**

Nobody improved on one word of it and nobody asked him why it had to be him to say it. He put his hand where it had been and said the other thing after it, in his own mouth, with the date in chalk on the outside of the door at the foot of the stair behind him.

**"—"**

That was the whole of what he said about it. The keeper squared the page against the fold and put her hand back onto it, and the woman of about fifty-two with her hands at the sides of her dress did not turn round, and the man of about twenty-seven carried the bucket out of the room and set it on the step and filled it there and brought it up and set it by the leg of the table with the water standing flat in it. Nobody at the bench agreed with him, nobody argued with him and nobody bettered one word of it, nobody asked him afterwards whether he was sure of it, and nobody thanked him for saying it out loud or for anything else.

**"You have put it out,"** said the man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall two stalls along, to the bench and not to him.

**"It is out,"** said the man of about twenty-seven, and put his hand into the bucket and brought it out heaped, and the water went over the rim of it and down the front of his coat and away along the flags behind him, up the row and out of the sight of the room.

---

At about the ninth hour they went past in ones and twos. Some of them stopped long enough to get a thing off the face of that board and stood in front of it a while, and some of them went up the row without stopping at it, and the ones who stopped did not stop long, and the row went on being a row of trestles with boards standing on them. The man of about thirty-nine stood at the far end of the bench up the stair and looked out of the one window at the end of his own row, and the man of about thirty-one went down the stair at about the tenth hour with the satchel under his arm and did not come up again.

By the light's going the weather had come up off the flags of the row into the bottom of the board where it stood, and the water had climbed the two feet of it about the depth of a finger, and the pale wood inside the two notches at the top of the trestle had gone dark and wet and could not be told from the wood either side of it.
