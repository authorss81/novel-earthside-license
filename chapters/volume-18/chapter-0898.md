# Chapter 0898 — At The Near End Of The Bench

The flat of the stallholder's own right hand went up over the outside of his own inside breast pocket at about the sixth hour and stayed there, and the cloth under it showed the square of what was inside without the hand going in.

It was Wednesday, the five hundred and eighty-third day of the Bare Month. The morning was pale and still, with thin cloud over the roofs and the flags dry from end to end. The man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board had come early to the near end of the bench and laid his hands on the wood. The room over the market kept full with the door open on the stair. The man of about thirty-one who carries things for a living kept the window with the satchel under his arm. The woman of about fifty-two held the wall with her hands at the sides of her dress. The man of about twenty-seven tended the bucket by the table leg. The woman of about twenty-nine who keeps a public register managed the book open on the bench with her hand on the page, and the second slate stayed on the shelf behind her. The man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall two stalls along rested with his own board across his knees. Otto Marsh waited on the far side of the table his hands resting behind him, one sleeve of his coat wet to the shoulder and the other sleeve dry. He let the hour go on past him.

---

At about the seventh hour Otto Marsh left the wall and came to the near end of the bench, and he stood beside the man of about thirty-nine where the light from the one window fell on the wood between them, with a date in chalk on the outside of the door at the foot of the stair behind them and the daylight on both their faces. Nobody else in the room looked up. He spoke low, to one man and not to the room, and nobody in the room heard a word of it.

**"—"**

The man of about thirty-nine did not answer it. He kept his hands on the wood of the bench and looked down at them, and after a while he rubbed his thumb once along the grain and left it there. He was thanked by nobody for not answering it, and nobody in the room heard a word of what had been said and nobody asked either of them about it. Otto Marsh went back to the far side of the table and put his hands behind him again and stayed there through the hour.

The hand on the pocket stayed where it was through all of it. The stallholder looked at the foot of his own board where a rate wanted its day, and then at the window, and nothing came out of the pocket at any hour and the hand did not go into it. Nobody asked him for anything and nobody said a word about his hand being where it was.

**"The trough wants a heat before the cold,"** said the man of about twenty-seven, at the table leg.

**"It wants one,"** said the keeper, without lifting her hand.

**"It will want one tomorrow,"** said the man of about thirty-nine, at the near end of the bench.

---

By the tenth hour the light had gone off the row and the bench stood in shadow from end to end. The hand came off the pocket and went back onto the board across his knees, and the square in the cloth smoothed out and was gone.
