# Chapter 0856 — She Said What It Had Cost Her

The woman of about fifty-two with her hands at the sides of her dress set both hands on the wood of the bench to hold herself still, and the wood was cold under them, and she kept them there a moment and then folded them again at her sides.

It was Wednesday, the five hundred and forty-first day of the Bare Month, and the door at the foot of the stair stood open on its latch with the light coming up the steps and stopping at the top of them. The date stood in chalk on the outside of the door, and under it a board about as wide as a hand stood out from the wood. Along the top step the iron lay where it had lain on every morning of the flood, and the two empty sockets stood open in the stone behind it, and the room above the market was full, and nobody in it had been summoned to be in it and nobody had been sent.

The woman of about twenty-nine who keeps a public register had the register open on the bench in front of her with her hand flat on the page and the second slate standing behind it on the shelf behind the bench. The man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall two stalls along sat at the middle of the bench with his own board across his knees and his thumb going along the foot of it where a rate wanted a day. The man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board stood at the far end with his board propped against the leg of it. The man of about twenty-seven had his bucket by the leg of the table. The man of about thirty-one who carries things for a living was by the window with his satchel under his arm.

Some of the room were carrying what they had found out that morning and some of them were carrying something else, and the woman of about fifty-two stood about nine feet off from the man of about thirty-four, with her hands at the sides of her dress, and he went on with his thumb along the foot of his own board and said nothing about it.

---

**"I am the one who stands at the door and looks, and nobody in this room has ever once asked me what I saw there, and I have got so used to being the one who looks that I cannot tell any longer between a morning I stood there and a morning I only heard about, and that is what it has cost me."**

She said it into the middle of the floor and not to anybody, and it went on longer than anything else said in the room that morning. The keeper's hand stayed flat on the page where it had been. The thumb of the man of about thirty-four went on along the foot of his own board and stopped there, and he found that it had stopped on a rate he had been holding at for days without knowing he was holding it, and he left his thumb where it was rather than move it and show that he had moved it. The man of about twenty-seven put his bucket down on the flags and did not pick it up again for a while. The woman of about fifty-two did not move and did not put her hands anywhere but at the sides of her dress, and the man of about thirty-nine kept the flat of his hand on the face of his board and looked at the middle of the bench.

---

The room went on. The man of about twenty-seven took his bucket out to the step and filled it there and brought it up, and set it down by the table leg with the water standing flat in it. The keeper turned a leaf and squared it against the fold and her hand went back onto the page. The man of about thirty-one went down the stair and came up again with the flap of the satchel down under his arm where it had been all morning, and the woman of about fifty-two stood at the side of the bench through the whole of it with her hands at the sides of her dress. Nothing whatever was done about it at any hour of that day, and nobody in the room asked her a second thing about it.

---

At about the ninth hour the man of about thirty-nine turned his board a quarter round on the leg of the bench.

**"There is a crack come in the near end of the trough at the top of the market step."**

Nobody answered him about it, and he put the board back with the face of it where it had been and stood with his own hand on it.

**"The flags are dry enough to walk on from the store's end,"** he said a while after that, into the middle of the floor and to nobody in particular, and he stopped there.

---

At about the tenth hour the woman of about fifty-two let go of the fold of her dress her right hand had been holding in without her knowing it, and the cloth went back flat against her leg and stayed flat, and nobody in the room saw her do it.
