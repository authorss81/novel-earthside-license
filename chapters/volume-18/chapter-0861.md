# Chapter 0861 — The Boards Were Still On The Flags

Adrian Vale put the flat of both hands on the top board of the trestle at the store's end of the market row. The wood was wet through and cold under them, and the course of boards he wanted off the flags lay along the front of the trestle with the water standing in the joint between the top two of them.

It was Monday, the five hundred and forty-sixth day of the Bare Month, and the rain was coming back across the flats from the west and had not come far enough yet to touch the ground. A date stood in chalk on the outside of the door at the foot of the stair at the back of the store's end, and under it the bare piece of board stood off the wood at its near end. Farther along the row a board on two nails above a step showed its three sets of figures, and the trough at the top of the step was running a little water.

He had come up the row before the light and had been at the near end of the trestle ever since, and what he wanted was the course of boards off the flags before the rain came back on to them. He could have got the top of the stack off by himself inside the time it takes water to come down an awning. Then he would have been standing in the middle of a market holding boards under his arm with nowhere to set them down which did not cost him more than the getting did, and he had worked that out before the light was on the awning. He had not done it. He stood with his hands on the flat of the board and let the morning go on past him.

---

At about the fifth hour the man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall two stalls along came up the row with his board under his arm and set it down on the flat of the trestle at the near end, which is where he sets it every morning he comes up. His hand stopped on the edge of it and stayed there a moment, because the top board of the trestle was under Adrian Vale's two hands at that end, and a board laid flat will not lie flat on the one beneath it without shifting it. He took his hand off. He lifted the board out from under his own arm, carried it the length of the trestle, and stood it against the far leg, and the wood there was wet as well and the leg was round, so the board went over twice and stood leaning with the bare back of it to the row. He put the flat of his hand on the top edge of it and left it there and worked at the near end of the row with his other hand, and he did not say anything about it and nobody said anything to him.

**"It will want a wedge at that end,"** said the stallholder, without letting go of it, and nobody answered him.

**"It will want a wedge at both ends of it,"** said the man of about twenty-seven, who had come along the row with the bucket in his right hand and the rag over the rim of it. He filled the bucket at the trough and carried it back, and the water came over the rim and ran down the front of his coat and along the flags behind him in a line going up the row.

Adrian Vale took his hands off the top board and put them at his sides and stood where he was while the bucket went down the stair and came up it again. The water in it stood a finger's breadth off level when it was set down by the leg of the table. Where the course of boards lay along the front of the trestle the water had come up over the near end of the top one and was standing against the side of the one under it, and it went on standing there while he did nothing about it.

---

At about the seventh hour the woman of about twenty-nine who keeps a public register came down the stair as far as the row and stood in the dry under the awning with the register under her arm. She read the figures on the board on the wall from the step, from the bottom of them up and then from the top of them down, and there was nothing under any of them to say what they were the figures of.

**"You have read those a good while,"** said the man of about twenty-seven, coming past with the bucket.

**"There is no telling what they are of,"** she said, and put her thumb against the lowest stroke of the middle set and left it there a moment and took it off again. Before she went back up the stair her eyes came off that wall and went along the flags to the front of the trestle and stayed on the course of boards lying there for the length of a bucket, and she said nothing about what she had looked at.

Adrian Vale had seen her look. The boards were where he had left them and the near end of the top one had gone under about a finger's depth of water in the time she stood on the step, and he did not go down on his knees in front of a whole row to shift a course of boards he had settled in his own head an hour ago not to shift.

At about the ninth hour the man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board came up the row and stopped at the far end of the trestle with his board under his arm. He looked at the boards lying on the flags, and then at the two men standing at the near end of it, one of them with a hand on a board leaning against a round wet leg, and then he went along the row with his board still under his arm and said nothing about them.

Adrian Vale went up the stair and stood at the near end of the bench with his hands open at his sides, and the room went on with the page and the boards and the bucket by the leg of the table.

---

The rain came off the awning at the store's end about the tenth hour and went on the flags past the near end of the trestle in one sheet. The course of boards lying along the front of the trestle took it along their top edges and let it off again at the far ends of them, and where the water had got between the near course and the one under it the near course shifted and went over on its far end and came to rest across the foot of the trestle with its far end in the water and its near end up on the dry stone. The stallholder's hand stayed on the top edge of his own board at the far end of the trestle from the time he set it there until the light was gone. He took it off once to turn a board over with the other hand and put it back on the same edge in the same place, and the board that had come off the flags lay across the foot of the leg where the water had put it and lay there while the light went off the row.
