# Chapter 0881 — One Question Neither Of Them Answered

Two hands went flat onto the face of a board standing up in a row of trestles in the open, and the wood was cold and wet through, and the water which had come off the awning in the night was still standing in the bottom groove of the writing, and the fingers went along the lines from the top of the face to the foot of it and did not stop anywhere in particular.

It was Sunday, the five hundred and sixty-sixth day of the Bare Month. The rain had come off the flats in the night and had not quite finished, and it was coming off the edge of the awning at the store's end in a line thin enough to miss. The flags of the row were dark and standing with water in every seam between them. At the store's end of the row the door at the foot of the stair stood open on its latch, the outside of it carried a day in chalk, and under the chalk a board about as wide as a hand with nothing on it stood clear of the wood at its near end by the width of a finger, and it had stood clear of it like that for the whole flood.

At the trough at the top of the market step the man of about twenty-seven had his bucket down under the far end of it, because the near end was cracked and there was water coming out of the crack in a thread which found the low place in the stone and went off along the flags. Two stalls along, the stallholder had his own board propped face in against the leg of his own trestle with the bare back of it turned out into the walking space behind his shoulder, and he was laying boards on the flat of his trestle with the near end of one of them out of square. The boards standing on the rest of those trestles showed their backs. About four people went past the row on their own business in the course of a morning, and some of them stopped in front of the one face which was standing out and some of them went up the row without stopping at it.

An hour before the light came properly the man of about thirty-nine had stood in front of the board by himself with a rag in his right hand and his own left hand on the near end of his own trestle, working at the standing water on the top of the wood, and he had been in front of it for the better part of a minute without lifting the rag off it. A man can put a thing out in a row and still not have looked at it. He had been looking at the face of it with the rag in his hand and the water going back into the seams of the trestle underneath, and there was nothing in the looking he could have put into words if somebody had asked him what he was doing, and nothing came off it either.

Adrian Vale came along the row at about the fifth hour and put both hands on the face of the board. It was the first time in years the face had been out where a man standing in the walking space could get at it, and what he got off it was a column of rates with the rates in their places and the paint of them hard under the wet and a hand's breadth of the top of the wood where the grain had gone soft. He read it from the top to the foot and then from the foot to the top, and he put his thumb on one of the strokes to see whether the paint came off on it, and it did not.

**"You have not been out in this,"** said the man of about thirty-nine, coming up the row with his own rag in his hand.

He said it into the middle of the walking space, and it was the first thing anybody had said in the row that morning which was about either of the two men standing there. Otto Marsh stood at the near end of it with the flap of his coat open at the throat and did not turn round, and did not answer, and did not say afterwards that he had not heard. The man of about thirty-nine waited a while with the rag in his hand and then took it and wiped the near end of his own trestle instead, and he did not ask it a second time, and there was no second question in that row between anybody that morning.

The man of about thirty-nine wanted his board a hand's width along the top of the trestle, out of the line where the water came off the edge of the awning, and he could not move it with two hands flat on the face of it. He stood and looked at the hands a while and then took the near end of the board up in his left hand, with the rag caught round his fingers, and Adrian Vale's hands went along the face of it with it as it came up, and between them they carried it a hand's width along the top of the trestle and set it down in the notches on the far side of the drip. Neither of them said a word while it was going. The face went through the line of falling water and came out of it wetter than it went in, and the last of the water standing on it ran off the far end of the board and down onto the flags where the row stood.

They went up the stair together at about the seventh hour and came into the room over the market, which was full and most of it was standing. The register was open on the wood at the near end of the bench in front of the keeper, five strokes in the column with a day against each of them and none struck, and the second slate stood on the shelf behind her with nothing on the face of it. The man of about thirty-nine took his place at the far end of the bench. The woman of about fifty-two stood where the light coming across the floor off the one window came to her, with her hands at the sides of her dress. The man of about thirty-one who carries things for a living had the window and the satchel under his arm with the flap of it hanging away from his side. Otto Marsh was against the far wall on the side of the table, one sleeve of his coat wet to the shoulder and the other sleeve dry.

**"There is a dry length of the sill under the window,"** said the keeper, and she did not take her hand off the page.

**"There is,"** said the man of about thirty-nine, and put the rag in his coat and took his own hand off the wood of the end of the bench.

He went at the near end of his own trestle with the rag for a while after that, and the water came off the eave and went past him and did not come on to the hand which was doing the work.

**"You could have had a thing over it and left it there,"** said the stallholder, who had come in behind him carrying his own board on its edge with the bare back of it out.

**"It would be a thing over a face and a face is what it is there for,"** said the man of about thirty-nine, and nobody in the room said one word back to him about it, and the stallholder propped his own board against the leg of the bench with its bare back out and sat down at the middle of it.

The morning went on the way the mornings of this flood go. The man of about twenty-seven carried the bucket down and up twice and the water stood flat in it every time. The keeper turned a leaf, squared it against the fold and left her own hand lying on the page. The man of about thirty-one went down the stair with the satchel under his arm and came up again with it and the flap of it was still down. Otto Marsh came off the wall at about the ninth hour, stood in front of the bench between the near end of it and the window for a while with his hands still behind him, and went back to the wall and stood there again, and asked nobody for anything and thanked nobody for anything at any hour of the morning.

The question the man of about thirty-nine had put into the walking space stayed where he had put it. It came up the stair in front of nobody and it went out of the room in the two mouths which had it and into nothing else, and by the time the light was off the row there was no way in this city of getting back to it at all, and the man of about thirty-nine did not put it into the floor again on any morning after it.

By the time the light had gone off the row the face of the board had dried with a run of clear water down the middle of it, and where the run had gone across the top of the writing the strokes had opened out into one another about the width of a thumb, and the man of about thirty-nine came along the row at about the tenth hour and put the flat of his own hand over the place and left it there while he looked at it, and took the hand off and went back up the stair without saying anything to anybody about it.
