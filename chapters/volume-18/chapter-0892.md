# Chapter 0892 — What Tuesday Left On The Sill

The sill at the one window held a pale shape in the dust where a satchel had stood on it, and the woman of about twenty-nine who keeps a public register wiped her thumb along the edge of the shape and looked at the dust on her skin and wiped it off on the side of the bench.

It was Thursday, the five hundred and seventy-seventh day of the Bare Month. The rain had gone in the night and left the flags with a shine on them from end to end, and a cold wind came down the row from the far end and lifted the edge of the awning and let it fall again. The man of about thirty-one who carries things for a living stood at the sill with the satchel under his arm and the flap of it down, turning it a finger's width on the wood. The room over the market was standing and full. The man of about twenty-seven waited by the leg of the table with the bucket at his feet and the water flat in it and the rag over the rim. Otto Marsh held the far side of the table his hands folded behind him, one sleeve of his coat wet to the shoulder and the other sleeve dry. He gave the sill a wide berth. The man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board rested his hand on the wood at the far end of the bench. The woman of about twenty-nine who keeps a public register sat at the bench with the book open before her and her hand on the page, and the second slate waited on its shelf behind her. The man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall two stalls along nursed his own board flat on his knees with the bare back of it out.

---

The keeper kept the book open on the bench all morning with her hand on the page, and the second slate stood behind her on its shelf with nobody going near it. At about the sixth hour the man of about thirty-one set the satchel down on the sill, shifted it a finger's width with both hands, and took it up again under his arm. The dust showed one shape where it had stood and, under the newer edge, a second shape of the same width and a little longer, set nearer the middle of the wood.

**"The sill wants wiping before the dust sets round what is standing on it,"** said the man of about thirty-four, with his board on his knees.

**"It wants wiping,"** said the keeper, and did not take her hand off the page.

The sentence had been said on Tuesday in the same words by the same mouth, and on Thursday it was said again in the same words and nobody in the room said they had heard it before. The man of about thirty-nine looked up from the far end of the bench and then down again at the wood under his thumb. Otto Marsh stayed against the far wall and said nothing, and the woman of about fifty-two stayed by the wall side of the bench with her hands at the sides of her dress and said nothing.

**"There is lead on the row and every bit of it is on a nail,"** said the man of about thirty-nine, at the far end of the bench, **"and the thread out of the trough wants a heat as well as the lead."**

**"The heat is in nobody's pocket here,"** said the man of about twenty-seven.

Some of the people in the room answered him and some went on with what they had in their hands. The board on the outside wall at the far end of the market had been read across at the fourth hour by the stallholder, three sets of figures at the height where a man walks, and the page on the bench had been read down and only down, and the water in the bucket stood between the two with a skin on it too thin to be read either way. The stallholder went out to the row at about the eighth hour and came back in with damp on his shoulders and said nothing about what he had seen there.

Out in the row the face on its own trestle stood out in the open with the wet gone off it, and the strokes at the top of it stood open where a run of water had gone across them. About four people a day went by on their own business, and some of them stopped in front of the outside board and some of them did not. The store door in the wall at the store's end stood a hand's breadth across from where it had stood, with the opening narrower at the far edge, and nobody put a hand to it.

---

By the time the light went off the row the shine had gone out of the flags from the middle outward, leaving a pale rim along the walking spaces where the wet had lifted the dust. The shape on the sill stood as it had stood, with the newer edge over the older, and the dust at the rim of it had begun to crumble back onto the wood.
