# Chapter 0879 — She Named What It Had Cost Him

A hand went onto the sill of the one window and stopped there where a satchel had been set down that morning, and the palm of it took the shape of the thing lying on the sill, and the dust on the wood inside the shape was pale and standing up loose where the dust along the rest of the sill had gone hard and grey.

It was Friday, the five hundred and sixty-fourth day of the Bare Month, and the sky had gone over at the top and the light in that room had a colour in it like the inside of a shell. The bar was where it always was along the top step of that stair with nothing under it, and below it the door stood open on its latch with a day chalked on the outside of it and a bare board about as wide as a hand under the chalk.

Out in his own row the board of the man of about thirty-nine was standing with its face out in the weather where he had put it on Monday and had not gone back for since, and in the room he stood at the far end of the bench with his own hand flat on the wood of the end of it. The keeper sat at the near end with her register open on the bench wood in front of her, and she kept her hand flat on the page. The stallholder had his board across his knees at the middle of the bench and his own thumb along the foot of it. The man of about twenty-seven had the bucket by the leg of the table with the rag over the rim of it, and Otto Marsh stood against the far wall on the side of the table.

---

The man of about thirty-one came up the stair at about the sixth hour with the satchel under his arm and the flap of it down, and set it on the sill of the one window and took his hand off it. He went down again at about the seventh hour and came up at about the eighth with the same satchel and the flap of it down, and the satchel was not opened at any hour of the morning and no reason was given for it and nobody in the room asked him for one.

At about the ninth hour the woman of about fifty-two said one thing, and it went on longer than anything else said in that room that morning.

**"The man of about thirty-one who carries things for a living has come up the stair every morning of this flood with the flap of the satchel down, and no one in this room has ever put a single question to him about it, and what that has cost him is that this morning he could not have told you whether his hands were carrying it because it wanted carrying or because they do not know how to come up a stair with nothing in them."**

She said it into the middle of the floor and not to anybody. The man of about thirty-nine did not move off the end of that bench and did not take his hand off the wood. The stallholder lifted his own board off his knees, set it on the bench beside him with its bare back up, and sat with his hands on his own knees. The man of about twenty-seven wrung the rag out over the bucket and put it over the rim again and did not look round. The man of about thirty-one put the satchel down on the sill where it had been and left it there and turned round and looked out of the window.

Nothing whatever was done about it at any hour of the day. The keeper turned a leaf and squared it against the fold and put her hand back onto the page, and the stallholder went back to the rate at the foot of that board and stayed on it. She got no answer out of the room, and not one word of it was bettered, and nobody said it was right or what it had been worth, and nobody thanked her for saying it or asked her a second thing about it.

---

**"That trough at the top of the step is cracked and still letting water out of the end of it,"** said the stallholder, at about the tenth hour, to his own board across his knees.

**"It is,"** said the man of about twenty-seven, **"and it is still the only piece of work on the row nobody has done a thing about since Tuesday."**

Nobody answered either of them about the trough. The keeper turned a leaf and squared it against the fold. The man of about thirty-nine stood at the far end of the bench where he had stood all morning, saying nothing at any hour of it.

The man of about thirty-one took the satchel off the sill a little before the light went and went down the stair with it under his arm and did not come back up. Where it had been standing the dust on the sill had come up pale and loose in the shape of it, and along the two long edges of the shape the older dust had been pushed out into two low ridges by the strap and the flap swinging against the wood, and the ridges had begun to set where the dust beside them was already set.
