# Chapter 0869 — She Named What That Page Had Cost Her

The flat of the hand of the woman of about twenty-nine who keeps a public register went down onto the page of the open register and stayed there, and the page was cool under it, and she did not take the hand off again while she said what she said.

It was Tuesday, the five hundred and fifty-fourth day of the Bare Month, and it was dry and cold, and the flags at the store's end had gone hard in the night. A day in chalk and a bare board under it stood on the outside of the door, the board off the wood at its near end. The room stood full and most of it was standing, and nobody in it had been sent to be in it and nobody had been told to come.

Across the knees of the man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall two stalls along lay his own board, flat, with his thumb along the foot of it. At the far end of the same bench, about nine feet off, the man of about thirty-nine stood with his board propped against the leg of it and the flat of his hand on the face of it, and he carried on with his own business while she spoke. The woman of about fifty-two with her hands at the sides of her dress stood up through the whole morning without once sitting down. The man of about thirty-one who carries things for a living was at the one window with the satchel under his arm and the flap of it down. The man with the one wet sleeve stood at the far wall on the side of the table, and the chair at the far side of the room had his coat over the back of it. The man of about twenty-seven had his bucket by the leg of the table.

---

**"Nobody in this room has ever asked me one word about the page I keep in front of me, and I have gone on keeping it every morning just the same, and that is what it has cost me: this morning I could not tell you whether I am keeping it for them or for me."**

She said it into the middle of the floor and not to anybody, and it went on longer than anything else said in the room that morning.

The hand of the man of about thirty-nine stayed on the face of his own board and then, after a while, came off the near edge of it and went to the other edge at the far side and lay there instead. The man of about twenty-seven took his bucket up off the flags and set it on the top of the table and took his hand off the rim. The stallholder put his board down off his knees and laid it flat across them and left his thumb along the foot of it. The man of about thirty-one put his satchel down on the window sill and left it there and turned round and looked out of the window. The man against the far wall did not move at all.

The hand of the woman of about twenty-nine who keeps a public register stayed on the page.

---

Nobody answered it and nobody improved on one word of it and nobody said it was right and nobody said what it had been worth, and nobody thanked her for saying it, and nobody in the room asked her a second thing about it at any hour of the day.

At about the seventh hour the man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall two stalls along said, into the middle of the floor and to nobody, the near end of the trestle at the store's end was still out of square and would want a wedge and nobody had brought one.

**"Nobody has brought one,"** said the man of about twenty-seven, **"and nobody has said where a wedge is kept either."**

Nobody answered either of them about it.

**"You will not get one out of him either,"** said the man of about twenty-seven, and nodded towards the stallholder without looking at him.

The woman of about fifty-two moved along the side of the bench to make room for the man of about twenty-seven to get at the bucket and said nothing while she did it. The keeper went on with the page and turned a leaf at about the eighth hour and squared it against the fold and put her hand back onto it.

At about the ninth hour the man of about thirty-one who carries things for a living went down the stair and came back up with the flap of the satchel down, and nobody asked him anything. Nothing whatever was done about it at any hour of the day.

---

The flags at the store's end had gone hard enough in the night that a man could cross the room in fewer strides than he had been taking all week. The man of about thirty-one who carries things for a living crossed it in fewer strides and did not slow down when he got to the window, and stood there with his satchel under his arm and the flap of it down.
