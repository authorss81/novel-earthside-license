# Chapter 0878 — A Man Said His Own Name Out Loud Once

The flat of a hand came down onto the wood of the bench at the far end of it and lay there, and the grain of the wood stood up under the fingers and was pressed flat where they lay, and the hand stayed where it was while the light came up the bench from the near end of it.

It was Thursday, the five hundred and sixty-third day of the Bare Month, and the weather had gone back to clear and cold with a low sun coming in under the awning at the store's end of that market. The door at the foot of the stair stood open on its latch, and a day stood in chalk on the outside of it, and under the chalk the bare piece of board about as wide as a hand stood off the wood at its near end. The room was full and most of it was standing.

Behind the register on the shelf behind that bench the second slate stood on its edge with nothing on the face of it and no hand went near it. The woman of about twenty-nine who keeps a public register had the register open on the wood in front of her and her hand flat on the page. The bench came to an end a foot short of the window, and the man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall two stalls along was in the middle of it, his board lying flat across his knees with its bare back turned out into the room. The woman of about fifty-two, her hands at the sides of her dress, stood by the bench looking down it and out of the window and not saying anything. The man of about twenty-seven had his bucket down beside the leg of the table, the rag over the rim. The man of about thirty-one who carries things for a living had the window and the satchel under his arm with its flap down, and did not turn round from either of them.

---

The morning went on the way the mornings of that room go. The man of about twenty-seven carried the bucket out of the room twice before the light and back up the stair twice with it, and the water in it came out level every time. The keeper turned a leaf and squared it against the fold and left her hand lying on the page where it had been all morning. The man of about thirty-four ran the flat of his own thumb along the foot of the board across his knees and came to the rate wanting its day and stopped on it and stayed there. At the far end of the bench the man of about thirty-nine stood with the flat of his own right hand on the wood of the end of it and ran his thumb along the edge of the bench and stopped at a knot in the wood and stayed there, about nine feet from the table at the far side of the room, and the man with the one wet sleeve was standing against that table.

**"The near leg of that trestle went out of square again in the night,"** said the man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall two stalls along, who did not lift his thumb off the rate at all when he said it.

**"It is out of square,"** said the man of about twenty-seven, at the leg of the table with his hand down in the bucket. **"It has been out of square all week and the boards on it go on crooked, and nobody has come for a wedge."**

Nobody went along the row for a wedge and nobody said out loud where a wedge would be kept. The man of about twenty-seven went out with his bucket and down the stair, filled it at the trough at the top of the step, and carried it up again and stood it down where it had stood before, and the man of about thirty-four went on with the rate at the foot of his own board and said nothing further about it.

At about the eighth hour the man at the far side of the table said one thing.

**"—"**

He said it once, in the room, in daylight, with a date in chalk on the outside of the door at the foot of the stair behind him, and he said it in his own mouth and to nobody in it. Nobody in the room had said it before him and nobody in the room said it after him. The man of about thirty-nine went on with the knot in the bench wood under his thumb and did not turn round, and the man of about thirty-four went on sitting where he was with his board across his knees, and the woman of about fifty-two kept her eyes on the sill with her hands at the sides of her dress.

---

It was in nobody's hands. The thing that had been said went out of the room in the one mouth it had been said in and into nothing else, and there was no page in this city on which any part of it stood, and by the light's going there was no way in this city of getting back to it at all. The man who had said it stayed against the table where he was and asked nobody for anything and thanked nobody for anything. Nobody in the room put a question to him about it at any hour of the day, nobody in the room said what he was, and nobody in the room said he was the first of anything.

The room went on round it. The man of about twenty-seven filled his bucket at the trough at the top of the step, carried it up the stair and set it down beside the table leg with the water standing flat in it. The man of about thirty-nine went down the stair later in the morning with his hands in his coat and stood at the bottom of it a while looking up the row at his own trestle from the far end, and came back up and took his place at the far end of the bench with his eyes on the one window and the row going past below it. The man of about thirty-four said the rate at the foot of his board wanted a day against it and said nothing else.

At about the ninth hour the wind got up under the door at the foot of the stair and came up the open stairwell and lifted the near corner of the keeper's page and let it go, and lifted it and let it go, and the hand flat on the page did not move, and the corner went on lifting and settling and lifting for as long as the wind was in that stairwell, and the light came up the bench to the far end of it while it did.
