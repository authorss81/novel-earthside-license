# Chapter 0885 — The Place A Barrow Will Not Go Over Twice

The wind had the water off eleven miles of flats up into the air by the fourth hour, and the man of about thirty-eight at that salt wharf got his barrow out of the yard and turned it out of the gate into it, the tally-board standing face up on the frame with its two lines of grey grit lying across it, the knife in his belt and the bed of the barrow empty.

It was Thursday, the five hundred and seventieth day of the Bare Month. The wind came up the flats against the run of them and the water on them did not go down the way water goes, it stood where it was while the wind took the top of it off and threw it back, and by the sixth hour the whole length was going white in patches and the middle of it had a hard clean look on it where the water had been taken off and not put back. The sun was not on any of it.

The two hollows in the flags at the side of the yard at the wharf stood as they had stood, one of them with its crack gone deeper across the skin of it and one of them with pale grit in the bottom of it and nothing broken anywhere in that, and he brought the barrow round the far side of both of them and neither came near his wheel.

He came to the place about the middle of the flats, where the stone has gone under the mud on the low side and the mud has been going for as long as anybody has watched. Water was standing over it a hand's depth and the going underneath was soft to the bottom of it. He went in at it with the barrow and the wheel went down to the axle in one stroke and stopped, and the barrow stood up out of the water on one side with his own two hands on the handles and his weight on the ends of them. The water came up the side of the frame and stood along the seam of it, and the wheel did not come out of it. He got the far end of the frame up with his own shoulder under it and put the edge of the frame against the hard going on the far side and got the wheel out that way, and the barrow came up out of the place and stood on the hard ground, and the hole he had made in the water was still standing there with his own track in it.

He could not go back over it. The going he had made in it would not hold the barrow a second time and he was not going to find out whether it would or not, so he came off the flats by the road which comes in at the wharf end and came back into the market the long way round with the wind behind him and his coat wet through to the shoulder blades. He was at the store's end of the market row at about the ninth hour with the sun coming out for the first time that day, and the store door in the wall there was still a hand's breadth across with the opening that much narrower at the far edge, and the stallholder stood at the near jamb of it with his own board under his arm and the bare back of it against his chest.

---

**"That barrow has been out on the flats since first light and it has not brought a thing back,"** said the stallholder, looking at the empty bed of it.

**"There was nothing to bring,"** said the man of about thirty-eight, **"and I went out to see whether a barrow could go at all."**

The sun came out on the flags at the store's end while he was saying it and the shadow of the awning came back off them.

**"There is a place on them where it will not go over twice,"** said the man of about twenty-seven, who had come down the step with the bucket in his hand, **"and you have found it, for the mud on the axle of that wheel has not come off it."**

**"There is,"** said the man of about thirty-eight, **"and it is in the middle of them, and the stone has gone under it, and it is a wheel to the axle every time and I would not do it twice either."**

The stallholder looked at the mud on the axle a while and then put his own board down against the jamb and took a hand to the wood of it.

**"Then you went the long way round,"** said the stallholder.

**"I went the long way round,"** said the man of about thirty-eight, **"and I would go it again tomorrow, and I have not told anybody else where the place is and I am not going to."**

---

A lump of mud about the size of a man's two fists was standing on the flags of the store's end of the row by the time the light was off, where the near wheel of the barrow had brought it up out of the flats and put it down, and the lump had begun to dry and come away from the stone at the edges of it. Where the man of about thirty-eight had stood holding the handles while the barrow stood up out of the water, his own boots were still standing in the wet flags with a hard white edge round each of them, and the water in the middle of either one had gone and the dust had not come back down to fill it, and the whole of the print was still there in the morning.
