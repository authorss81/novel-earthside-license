# Chapter 0895 — Two Down And One Staying

The one by the door who had stood inside the door on Saturday stood there again at light with his back to the bench and his hands at his sides, and the man of about thirty-one who carries things for a living stood beside him with the satchel under his arm and the flap of it down.

It was Sunday, the five hundred and eightieth day of the Bare Month. The frost had gone and the water was coming back out of the seams of the flags in thin lines, and no wind moved in the market from the fourth hour on. The woman of about twenty-nine who keeps a public register was first at the bench, opening the book on the wood and settling her hand on the page. The room over the market was full. The man of about twenty-seven brought the bucket to the table leg and hung the rag on the rim. Otto Marsh crossed to the far side of the table and laced his fingers at his back, one sleeve of his coat wet to the shoulder and the other sleeve dry. He left the bench end to those sitting. The man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board lowered himself to the far end of the bench. The woman of about fifty-two found the wall and set her hands at the sides of her dress. The man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall two stalls along carried his own board across his knees.

---

The piece of work on the bench did not come out all morning. The keeper held the page against the draught from the door with the edge of her hand, and the leaf lifted and fell back twice and would not lie flat. The stallholder tried the foot of his own board with his thumb, came to the rate wanting its day, and left the thumb there and did nothing more with it. The man of about twenty-seven mended the fold of the rag over the rim of the bucket and the fold slipped and he mended it again.

**"The leaf will not lie flat while the door stands open,"** said the keeper, with her hand on the page.

**"Shut it and the room will be dark before the hour,"** said the man of about thirty-four.

**"Leave it,"** said the man of about thirty-nine, at the far end of the bench.

At about the eighth hour the one by the door pushed off the door frame and went to the head of the stair. The man of about thirty-one went with him with the satchel under his arm, and the man of about twenty-seven set down the bucket and went with the two of them as far as the top step. The three went down a little way together, and then Otto Marsh stayed where he was on the far side of the table with his hands behind him and did not follow. The one by the door went on down with the two men, past the bar lying along the top step, without touching it, and the sound of their boots went down the stairwell and out through the open door at the foot.

Nobody said he was forgiven and nobody said he was condemned, and he was given to no side and to no one man. The keeper squared the leaf against the fold once the door below had swung to. The man of about thirty-nine kept his hand on the wood of the bench and looked at the door and said nothing more.

---

After they had gone the room was quieter by the width of two men. Otto Marsh stood where he had stood all morning, and the light through the one window reached the edge of the table and stopped short of his boots, and he did not move into it.
