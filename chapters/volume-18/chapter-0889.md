# Chapter 0889 — Two Hands On The Newel

Two hands went onto the newel at the foot of the stair and lay there on it, and there was a band of it wet round the wood about the width of a hand where the water of the night had run down and stopped, and Adrian Vale's hands lay on the dry wood above the band, and they stayed where they were while the light came up the steps from the open door at the bottom.

It was Monday, the five hundred and seventy-fourth day of the Bare Month, and it was one of those mornings which starts hard and clear and turns wet in the middle of it. About the fourth hour the wind came round behind the roofs at the far end of the market and brought the first wet snow of this flood with it. The flags of the row were dry and white at the edges where the night had left them, and the boards standing on the trestles had a rim of snow along the tops of them where the wind could not get at it. In the room, the keeper kept the register open on the bench with the flat of her own hand down on the page, and the second slate stood on the shelf behind her. The man of about thirty-four had his own board under his arm. Otto Marsh stood on the far side of the table with both hands behind him where they had been all morning.

---

The snow was on the flags by the seventh hour in grains the size of a pinhead and it was not lying anywhere yet, and it went along the row in the wind in a low line and took the top of the trough at the top of the market step with it.

**"There is wet snow coming on it and there will be more of it before the light goes,"** said the man of about twenty-seven, at the step with the bucket in his hand.

**"There will,"** said the man of about thirty-four, who had stopped on the top step of the stair with one foot on it, **"and it will go into the seams of the flags and stand there till morning."**

**"There is a dry length of the top step for anybody that wants to stand on it,"** said the keeper, and did not take her hand off the page.

The two hands on the newel did not go up. The man who had put them there had wanted to go up the stair and had not gone up, and he was on the bottom step with his hands on the newel for the better part of a morning, and everybody in the room went on past him with their own business, and nobody asked him what he was doing there and nobody was waiting for him to be useful to them.

What he was in the way of, he was in the way of twice. The man of about thirty-four came to the foot of that stair at about the seventh hour with his own board under his arm, and the width of a stair with a man standing on the bottom step of it is the width of a stair with nobody on the bottom step of it and no more, and the man of about thirty-four came up it with his shoulder against the wall of the stairwell and his own board held up off the rail and his face turned away from the hands on the newel. Adrian Vale had to put his hands off the newel and stand flat against the wall to give the width of the step back, and he stood there with his own hands empty at his sides while the other man came up past him, and the width of the step came back to him afterwards and he did not go up on it.

At about the eighth hour the man of about thirty-nine came up out of the row with his own hand flat on the wood of the top of his trestle, got one hand onto the newel at the top step and then put the other one on it as well, and he went up the rest of the stair one tread at a time with a hand on the newel at each side of him and the bar lying along the top step with nothing under it, and he went past it without touching it. Nobody in the room said a word about it, nobody said why and nobody asked him about it, and the stallholder stood at the top of the stair the whole of it with his own board under his arm and then went back down into the row and left the stair alone.

Out in the row the wet snow had begun to lie along the top of the board standing on its own trestle, in the groove of the wood along the top edge of the face, and it was not brushed off and nobody went along the row and brushed it off. The man of about twenty-seven filled his bucket at the far end of the trough with the snow coming down into the water off the edge of the awning and brought it up and stood it by the leg of the table with the water standing flat in it.

The flat of the stallholder's own right hand went up over the outside of his own inside breast pocket at about the ninth hour and lay there, and it lay there for the better part of an hour. Nothing came out of that pocket at any hour of the morning and the hand did not go into it either. Nobody in the room asked him for anything and nobody said a word about his hand being where it was, and the hand came off the pocket and went back onto his own board across his knees and the two of them went on being there.

---

The wet snow had gone off the flags again by the middle of the afternoon and what was left of it stood in the seams of them and in the top of the trough and along the two feet of the board standing out in the row. By the time the light went off, the damp in the flags had begun to come up out of them in the white, and the seam of the stones under the boots of the man of about thirty-four, who had stood at the top of that stair at about the ninth hour while a man went up it, was still clear of it and had begun to set.
