# Chapter 0888 — A Folded Sheet Of Road And One Thing

A folded sheet of road came up the stair in a woman's hand with the fold of it soft at the corners from being carried and the print of it dark along the crease, and she did not open it on the stair and she did not open it in the room either.

It was Sunday, the five hundred and seventy-third day of the Bare Month. The frost had gone off the flags in the night and the water was coming back out of the seams of them in lines, thin ones and long ones, and not a breath of wind was in the market from the fourth hour onwards. Everybody in the room over the market was on their feet and the middle of the bench was the only part of it anybody was using to sit on. The keeper had the register open on the wood of the bench with her own hand flat down on the page, and the second slate stood on the shelf behind the bench. The man of about thirty-four had his board flat on his knees with the bare back of it turned out. The man of about twenty-seven had the bucket beside the leg of the table and the rag hanging off one side of it. Otto Marsh stood on the far side of the table, and he did not look at the woman with the sheet when she came in.

---

**"I have a sheet of road with about nine places on it,"** said Tamsin Quill, standing inside the door at the near end of that bench, **"and I am going to be working my way along it instead of in this city, and that is the whole of what I have come up the stair for."**

She said it once, in her own mouth, to the middle of the floor. She did not say it again at any hour of the day, and nobody in the room asked her a second thing about it, and nobody said it was right and nobody said it was wrong and nobody thanked her for coming up the stair to say it. The keeper turned a leaf, ran her hand down the edge of it and left the hand lying on the page where it had been. The stallholder ran his thumb along the foot of his own board, came to the rate which wanted its day and stayed there. The man of about thirty-nine had his own hand down on the wood of the end of that bench and said nothing about what she had said, and Otto Marsh did not turn round from the far side of the table and said nothing about it either.

Then the room went on with the ordinary hour it had been having all morning, which is what a room does with a thing it is not going to take up.

**"Is there anybody in this room that has a piece of lead in a pocket this morning,"** said the man of about twenty-seven, at the leg of the table.

**"Not in mine,"** said the keeper.

The woman with the folded sheet turned her head a little at the sound of it, and did not open the sheet, and said nothing.

**"There is lead on this row and every bit of it is on a nail,"** said the man of about thirty-nine, at the far end of the bench, **"and there is a heat wanting as well as the lead and neither of them is on a nail."**

**"There is a heat wanting,"** said the man of about twenty-seven, **"and the man who would want to see the crack closed before the winter is at the far end of the row and not in this room."**

Some of the people in the room answered him and some went on with what they had in their hands, and the question did not get past the room. The woman with the folded sheet stood by the door the whole hour with the sheet in her hand and the fold of it not opened, and she did not answer the question about the lead and she was not asked one about the sheet. At about the eighth hour she put her hand flat on the door frame on the way past it and went down the stair and along the row, and nobody in the room watched her go and nobody said a word about where she was going. She was not asked one thing by anybody in that room at any hour after she had said her one thing, and she was not on the page in front of the keeper, and the column of figures went on holding what it had held all morning with the hand flat on it.

---

The water came up out of the seams of the flags in enough lines, by the time the light was off the row, to show where the walking spaces were. There was a ring of stone at the foot of the stair about the width of a hand which the water had stopped short of and had not gone round, and the middle of it was as dry and as pale as it had been before the water came back up out of the seams, and the pale edge of the ring had begun to set.
