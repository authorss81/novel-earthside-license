# Chapter 0873 — The Crack In The Near End Of That Trough

The man of about twenty-seven put his hand down into the bucket up to the wrist and brought it out full, and the water went over the rim and down the front of his coat, and in the near end of the trough at the top of the market step the crack in the wood was dry, and by the time he had the bucket on the stone the crack was running.

It was Saturday, the five hundred and fifty-eighth day of the Bare Month, and it was flat and cold with no wind in it. The flags at the store's end had gone hard again in the night and the standing water in the low place at the side of the yard had a skin on it. The door at the bottom of that stair stood open on its latch with a day chalked on the outside of it and the bare piece of board standing under the chalk, and above the door along the top step the bar lay where it lay on every morning of the flood, with the two sockets it belongs in open behind it and dust lying in the bottom of them.

The morning ran the way Friday's had run. The keeper had the register open on that bench with her hand flat on the page and turned a leaf and squared it against the fold. At the far end of the bench the man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board kept his own hand where it always was, and the light off the sill came across the middle of the bench and stopped short of where he stood. The woman of about fifty-two with her hands at the sides of her dress kept to her place at the wall side of the bench and did not sit down, and the man of about thirty-one who carries things for a living had the one window and the satchel under his arm, its flap hanging clear. The man with the one wet sleeve stood against the far wall on the side of the table in the place he had taken on Saturday, and he was in the room the way a bucket is in a room, and nobody in the room said one word to him at any hour of the morning. and nobody in the room said one word to him at any hour of the morning.

---

**"That trough has been cracked since Tuesday,"** said the man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall two stalls along, standing out at the step at about the sixth hour with his board under his arm, **"and the crack is the width of a finger now."**

**"It is,"** said the man of about twenty-seven, filling the bucket out of the far end of it, **"and it wants a piece of lead and a heat to close it, and I have not got a heat, and nobody in this room has got one either."**

Nobody said out loud where a piece of lead would be kept, and nobody went along the row for one. The man of about twenty-seven brought the bucket up the stair and stood it by the leg of the table with the water standing flat in it, and at about the seventh hour he took it down again and filled it at the other end of the same trough, and the water came through the crack the whole of the morning in a thread falling on to the stone of the step and found the low place in it and went away down the flags.

The trough was the one piece of work on the row that would not come out, and the man it belonged to went on with it exactly as it stood, because the only thing anybody can do to a crack in a wet trough is close it, and he had nothing to close it with.

At about the eighth hour the man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board came down the row with his board under his arm and stopped at the step and put the board flat on the stone and looked into the near end of the trough from above it. He could not see anything at the bottom of it from where he stood, and he got down onto one knee in the wet to see, and he stayed there a while with his hand on the rim, and he came up with the knee of his trousers wet through to the skin and stood on the step a moment with the water standing in the print of it on the stone. He took his board up off the stone and went on up the row and said nothing to anybody about what he had gone down to look at.

---

**"It will come out of the end of it before long,"** said the man of about thirty-four, back at the middle of the bench, with his thumb on the rate at the foot of his own board.

**"It will come out of the end of it,"** said the man of about twenty-seven, and wrung the rag out over the bucket and put the rag over the rim again and carried on with the washing of his own. The sill of the store's door and the stone at the side of the step he washed, and the wall above it he left alone, and the bucket went down and up twice more before the light went off the row.

By the tenth hour the thread of water coming through the crack had cut a line in the dust of the stone at the top of the step about the length of a hand, and the dust in the line was dark and packed and the dust on either side of it was pale and standing up off the stone, and where the line came out of the wood it had begun to widen at the top.
