# Chapter 0891 — Two Answers To One Ordinary Question

The bucket came up the stair in the hands of the man of about twenty-seven with the rag folded over the rim of it dripping onto the treads, and he set it down beside the leg of the table and stood back from it with his sleeves pushed up past the wrists.

It was Wednesday, the five hundred and seventy-sixth day of the Bare Month. A thin rain had been on the row since before light, fine enough to hang on the edge of the awning without falling, and the flags under it were dark from end to end. The room over the market was full and most of it was standing. The man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board sat at the far end of the bench easing his thumb along the wood. Otto Marsh kept to the far side of the table with both hands clasped at his back, one sleeve of his coat wet to the shoulder and the other sleeve dry. His eyes went no further than the table. The woman of about twenty-nine who keeps a public register worked with the book open on the bench wood and the flat of her hand holding the page, while the second slate rested on the shelf behind the bench with the face of it clean. The man of about thirty-one who carries things for a living leaned at the window with the satchel under his arm and the flap of it down. The man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall two stalls along balanced his own board across his knees with the bare back of it turned out. The woman of about fifty-two waited by the wall side of the bench with her hands at the sides of her dress.

---

The morning went on in the ordinary hour it keeps when the rain is too thin to send anybody out of it. The keeper turned a leaf and squared it against the fold. The stallholder ran his thumb along the foot of his own board and stopped where a rate wanted its day and left the thumb there. The man of about thirty-one shifted the satchel higher under his arm and said nothing about where he had been.

**"Will the thread out of the near end of the trough hold till morning if nobody fetches a heat for it,"** said the man of about twenty-seven, at the leg of the table with the bucket at his feet.

**"It will hold till morning and no longer,"** said the keeper, without lifting her hand off the page.

**"It will hold through the night and go in the cold after light,"** said the man of about thirty-nine, at the far end of the bench.

The keeper put her hand back flat on the page where it had been and gave nothing to either of them with her face. The man of about thirty-nine kept his thumb on the wood, eased it a finger's width along the grain, and looked down the bench toward the window and not toward the keeper. The man of about twenty-seven picked the rag off the rim of the bucket, wrung it out over the water, and laid it back along the rim with the ends hanging even. Nobody thanked either of them, nobody said either of them was right, and not one of the two bettered a word of what the other had said.

Otto Marsh did not turn round from the far side of the table and said nothing at any hour of the morning. He had his hands behind him where they had been since light, and when the bucket went down the stair and up it again he moved a half step to give the width of the floor to the man carrying it and then moved back to the wall. The woman of about fifty-two looked at the shelf and then at the window and said no word about the thread or the heat.

Out in the row the face standing out on its own trestle went darker in the thin rain and stayed where it was. The man of about thirty-nine did not go down to it at any hour before the middle of the day. About four people a day went past the foot of the stair on their own business while the light was on the flags, and some of them stopped under the awning to shake the wet off their shoulders and some of them did not.

At about the ninth hour the man of about thirty-one went down the stair with the satchel under his arm and came up it again before the tenth, and not one mouth in the room asked him where he had been or what he had gone down for. The stallholder took his thumb off the foot of his own board and put both hands flat on his knees. The keeper ran her hand down the edge of the leaf and left it lying on the page.

---

The drip off the edge of the awning thickened toward the middle of the afternoon and began to fall in drops wide enough to mark the flags. Where the drops struck the stone under the line of the face standing out, each left a dark ring which spread to meet the next, till the whole length under the trestle was one wet band with a paler edge along the dry stone.
