# Chapter 0882 — Somebody Would Have To Go Past The End

A boot came in over the flags from the door with the water standing in the print it left, and the print went along the floor in a line of seven or eight and stopped at the near leg of the bench, and the man of about thirty-one who carries things for a living stood there with the satchel under his arm and put his hand up to the back of his own neck and came away from it with his hand wet.

It was Monday, the five hundred and sixty-seventh day of the Bare Month. The sky had pulled up a little after the rain and the air had gone to the hard cold which comes behind a day of water, and the light came in under the awning at the store's end and put the shadow of the boards standing in the row across the walking spaces of it. The room over the market was full, and most of what was in it was on its feet. Across the knees of the man of about thirty-four lay his own board with the bare back of it turned out into the room and his own thumb along the foot of it, where a rate wanted a day. The man of about twenty-seven was out of the room with the bucket and the rag thrown over the rim of it. Otto Marsh had the far side of the table to himself, standing with his face turned into the room.

---

The hour when it came was the hour after the water, when a room is doing nothing and everybody in it would rather be doing something.

**"Somebody has to go past the end of the row for any of that,"** said the stallholder, **"and none of us at this end of it goes."**

**"There is the heat as well,"** said the man of about twenty-seven, with his hand down in the bucket, **"and the heat is not past the end of the row. The heat is in this room and there is nobody here with one."**

He brought the bucket up heaped and the water went over the rim and down the front of his coat and he set it down and put his hand in it again.

**"There is nobody here with a lead either,"** said the stallholder, **"and you have said it since the middle of the week."**

**"I have,"** said the man of about twenty-seven, and brought the bucket up full to the rim and set it down on the stone.

The talk went on the same way for the better part of an hour, and it was the same three things said in a slightly different order every time round. A row of trestles is short at both ends of it. The stallholder said that, and the man of about twenty-seven said it again in his own mouth, and the keeper said it a third time with her hand flat on the page, and the woman of about fifty-two stood with her hands at the sides of her dress and said nothing at any hour of the morning. The man of about thirty-nine said one thing about it and it was short.

**"It can want it,"** said the man of about thirty-nine, at the far end of the bench, **"and wanting it is not the same as a man going for it."**

The stallholder put his own hand flat on the top of the board across his knees and left it there, and the water stood flat in the bucket while he did it. At the far end of the bench the man of about thirty-nine kept a hand on the wood and did not take part in the hour, and the keeper went over the same lines of her column twice and got the same out of them both times, and the man of about twenty-seven filled his bucket again at the far end of the trough and brought it up and stood it where it had stood.

Nobody said it was right and nobody said it was wrong, and the hour ended the way the hour began, with everybody's own business still standing in front of them where they had left it. The man of about thirty-one stood at the window the whole of it with the satchel held up under his arm and the flap of it hanging away from his side, and at about the ninth hour he went down the stair with it and was back up at about the tenth hour with the flap of it down, and the room went on round his going the way a room goes on round a bucket being carried out.

He was the only person in it who went anywhere that day. Not one mouth in it asked him where he had been or what he had gone down for, and he did not say, and he put the satchel down on the sill of the one window and left it there and took his hand off it.

---

The patch of flags at the leg of the bench where he had stood went on drying from the middle out, so that by the time the light was off the row there was a ring round the edge of it which had taken the colour of the floor and a centre which had not. The rain which had come off the flats in the night had come out of the row and the prints he had brought in at the sixth hour had gone to a pale rim standing about the depth of a thumb in the dust which the wet had lifted, and the water had gone out of the middle of them.
