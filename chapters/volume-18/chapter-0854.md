# Chapter 0854 — The Skin Across The Hollow Had Cracked

A hand went down onto the rim of the hollow at the side of the yard with the four fingers laid over the edge of it, and the fingers went into the grit come up into a skin across the hollow, and the skin took the weight of them and did not break.

It was Monday at the wharf at the near end of eleven miles of flats, on the five hundred and thirty-ninth day of the Bare Month, and a north-easterly had got up in the night and had not gone down again. The hollow stood at the side of the yard where the flags dip, and the skin across it had been hard since a barrow had gone down into it and had come up out of it again. The low place in the stone along the wall of the store was dry, and the awning over the boards had been dripping off its edge all the morning and had not made a puddle.

The man of about thirty-eight at that salt wharf took his fingers out of the skin and looked at the grit on them and rubbed it off his coat. The skin was not one piece any more. There was a crack in it running from the rim of the hollow at the near side to the middle of the hollow and stopping there, and the grit under the crack was dark and damp where the grit round the edge of the hollow had gone pale and hard, and the water the yard takes in the wet season was finding the crack and getting in under the skin.

He stood a while with both hands on the handles of his barrow and looked down at it, and then he took the barrow up onto the dry crown and would not bring the near wheel over the hollow again. He took the stack of boards up on his shoulder one at a time and set it down along the crown with the ends squared, and he took the tally-board off the top row and put it flat on the boards face up with the two lines of grey grit lying across it, and he did not turn it over, and the knife in his belt stayed where it was.

---

At the far end of the market the board on two nails above the step carried its three sets of figures with nothing written under any of them, and it was the first thing a person going along the row in the morning had in front of them. They went past it all morning in ones and twos, and some of them stopped long enough to get a day off it and stood a while in front of the figures before going on, and some of them did not stop at all and went up the row with their eyes on the trestles on either side of them.

The woman of about twenty-nine who keeps a public register came down the row about the middle of the day and stood under the board and read the figures from the bottom up and then from the top down, and there was nothing under them to say what they were the figures of.

The man of about thirty-eight at that salt wharf came up the row behind his barrow with a light load on it and the near wheel running in the wet grit along the flags, and he stopped at the step under the board and set the barrow on its legs.

**"You have read that a good while."**

**"There is no telling what it is of,"** she said, with her hand still up on the last of the figures.

**"It is what it has always been of."**

**"That is not the same as telling,"** she said, and dropped her hand, and went back up the row with the register under her arm.

Nobody in the row asked him where his load came from and nobody asked him what he had done with it, and nobody said anything about the board on the nails, and over at his own trestle the man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall two stalls along watched him go by from behind his own board and said nothing.

---

He went back down the flats at about the ninth hour with the barrow empty and the tally-board under his arm, and he came in off the flats on the dry crown again and went round the hollow the long way with the wheel on the dry stone, and the grit come up out of the hollow and stuck along the two rims came off again on the dry flags at the side of the low place in the stone and lay there in two long smears, and by the tenth hour the edges of those two smears had gone into the dry flags and the middle of them had not.
