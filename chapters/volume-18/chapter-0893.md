# Chapter 0893 — Twice In Different Words

Adrian Vale sat at the near end of the bench with a page in his own hand spread on his knees, and nobody else in the room could read a word of it, and he kept the flat of his left hand on the edge of it to keep it from curling.

It was Friday, the five hundred and seventy-eighth day of the Bare Month. The morning came up pale with no wind, and the flags of the row were damp in the seams and dry on the crowns. The woman of about fifty-two stood by the wall with her hands at the sides of her dress, early among those standing. The room over the market stood full through the hour. The man of about twenty-seven set the bucket down by the table leg and left the rag folded on the rim. The man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall two stalls along settled his own board across his knees. The woman of about twenty-nine who keeps a public register opened the book on the wood and laid her hand on the page, with the second slate behind her on the shelf. The man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board took the far end of the bench and fitted the knot in the wood under his thumb. The man of about thirty-one who carries things for a living took the window with the satchel under his arm. Otto Marsh remained on the far side of the table his hands set behind him, one sleeve of his coat wet to the shoulder and the other sleeve dry. He stayed out of the draught from the door.

---

At about the seventh hour Adrian Vale asked a question in the room, and he had asked it on every morning of the flood, and nobody answered it, and the reason was nobody else ever heard him ask it. Nobody in the room remarked on it. The keeper turned a leaf and squared it against the fold and put her hand back on the page. The man of about twenty-seven lifted the bucket and carried it down the stair, and had to go wide at the bottom step to clear the page on Adrian Vale's knees, and a little water went over the rim onto the stone. Adrian Vale drew his feet back to give the width of the floor and did not look up from the page.

He had wanted to have said one question twice, and he did not. Nobody thanked him, nobody told him he was right, nobody in the room was waiting for him to be useful and he was not useful to them.

The ordinary hour went on round him. The stallholder shifted his board on his knees and the wood knocked once against the leg of the bench. The woman of about fifty-two looked at the shelf and said nothing. Otto Marsh stayed where he was with his hands behind him.

**"Will the flags be dry enough by the tenth hour to carry a load across without laying boards,"** said the man of about thirty-one, at the window.

**"Not before light goes,"** said the keeper, with her hand on the page.

**"No, not till the light is off the row,"** said the man of about thirty-nine, at the far end of the bench.

Neither of the two bettered one word of what the other had said, and nobody in the room said either of them was right and nobody thanked either of them. The man of about thirty-one shifted the satchel under his arm and looked out of the window at the flags. The keeper ran her thumb down the edge of the leaf. The man of about thirty-nine rubbed his thumb once along the grain of the bench and left it there.

---

When the light went off the row the damp in the seams of the flags came up white at the edges and the crowns stayed dark. The page in Adrian Vale's hands curled at the corner where his hand had left it, and he smoothed it down once with the heel of his palm and folded it inward before he stood.
