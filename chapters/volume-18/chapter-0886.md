# Chapter 0886 — One Plain Block Along The Far Wall

The woman of about fifty-two took the near corner of the cloth on the table between two fingers and drew it flat where it had come away from the wood of the edge, and the cloth went along the top of the table with a dry sound and stopped, and she put her hands back at the sides of her dress and did not touch it again at any hour of the morning.

It was Friday, the five hundred and seventy-first day of the Bare Month, and the night had gone hard and blue with no cloud in it anywhere, and by the fourth hour the shadows of the boards standing in the row were coming in under the awning at the store's end and lying across the walking spaces and up against the step. The wind had gone into the north and died there, and not a leaf was moving on any of the trestles. Nobody was sitting in a chair in that room and nobody had a place on the bench free at the near end of it. The stallholder had his own board lying flat across his knees with the bare back of it out and his thumb along the foot of it. The keeper had the register open in front of her at the near end of the bench, and the second slate stood where it stood on the shelf behind her. Otto Marsh was on the far side of the table with his hands behind him.

On the wall along the far side of the room there was one thing written, and it stood by itself in a block, and there was nothing on the face of it and no mark of any kind on any part of it. Nobody in the room said any part of it out loud and nobody in the room said any part of it a second time. It was not written on the bare piece of board standing under the chalk on the outside of the door at the foot of the stair, and it had nothing to do with that board.

> A guest who says his own name in a room has given nobody anything, and a guest who does not say it has refused
> nobody anything, and a room that wants the second of those is a room that would rather have a person it can
> find than a person, and there is no cheaper way to want one.

No mouth in the room said the thing on that wall was right and no mouth said it was wrong.

---

Nothing whatever was done about it at any hour of the day. The man of about thirty-nine stood at the far end of the bench with a knot in the wood under the pad of his own right thumb, and he went on pressing it and going along the grain away from it and coming back to it, and no mouth in the room asked him what he made of the thing on the wall, and he was not asked it and he did not offer it. The keeper turned a leaf, squared it against the fold and put her hand back onto the page, and the second slate behind her was not touched at any hour, and no hand went near the shelf but hers and hers did not go near it either.

**"There is a thing on the wall you have all read,"** said the stallholder, at the middle of the bench, to nobody in particular.

**"We have read it,"** said the keeper.

**"I have read it,"** said the man of about thirty-nine, at the far end of the bench with his thumb still on the knot, **"and I have not got anything to say about it, and that is not a thing I am saying against anybody."**

The morning went on the way mornings in that room go. The man of about twenty-seven took the bucket down the stair and brought it up again filled at the far end of the trough, and stood it by the table leg with the water standing flat in it. The thread coming out of the crack in the near end of the trough went along the stone of the step and down into the low place and away off along the flags of the row, and it was going the whole morning, and nothing whatever was done about it. The man of about thirty-one who carries things for a living had the one window with the satchel under his arm and the flap of it hanging, and he looked out of it at the boards standing on the trestles and at the one face among them and turned about and looked at the flags inside the window instead. The man of about thirty-four went out to his own trestle about the ninth hour and came in again with the flat of his own hand still on the face of his own board and left the hand there. Otto Marsh did not turn round from the far side of the table at any hour of the morning and asked nobody for anything and thanked nobody for anything and said nothing at all.

The shadows of the row had gone off the walking spaces and up onto the step by about the tenth hour and were standing along the awning, and the light came in under it and lay along the bench from end to end. The man of about thirty-nine took his own hand off the wood of the end of it, turned the hand over and looked at the print the knot had left in the pad of his thumb, and rubbed his thumb once along the grain of the bench. The print did not come off it. He shut his hand and put it behind him where he had had it all morning.
