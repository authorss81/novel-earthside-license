# Chapter 0248 — Dust In A Pan

A beam with one pan on it has stood in the middle of the yard of the store on the upper road since the seventy-third day of the Long Month on a block of stone about the length of a man's own foot, and on the ninety-third day of that month the man of about fifty-two who digs came up that road at about the ninth hour and found a finger of the dust off eleven miles of flats lying on the top of what was in the pan, and he put his own coat over that pan and did not turn the pan out. The wind has been at that wall since the seventy-fourth day of that month and the beam has stood in the open for every day of it, and the far pan came off it on the seventy-third day of that month in front of about nine people and nobody has taken the wood of it.

---

**And he made that beam on the fifty-eighth day of that month and carried it up nine miles and left it in that yard on the seventy-third day of that month and has not carried it home, and he had not been in that yard in twenty days, and a quarter of a sack that is one piece has been in that pan since the sixty-seventh day of that month and went hard on the sixty-sixth.**

"You made it."

"**I made it, and I left it here on the seventy-third day of that month, and the far pan came off it on that morning in front of about nine people, and I have not been in this yard in twenty days and everything in it has happened without me in it, and I came up this morning to put my coat over that pan and not for anything else.**"

"You have not looked in it."

"**I have not looked in it and I am not going to, because a pan in the open that has been standing in the wind off those flats since the seventy-fourth day of that month has got the day on the top of it, and if I turn that pan out and weigh what is under the day then I am measuring a yard instead of a sack, and about four people in this city have worked out that a man who weighs what is in an open pan on the ninety-third day of a month is a man who has made the yard a column.**"

"Is there a word for what is in it."

"**There is a word for it and the word is a quarter of a sack that cannot be divided, and nobody has worked out that the one pan left on it is the one I put the lump in, and the other one went to pieces on the boards in front of everybody.**"

---

**And the woman of about thirty-four in that store came out of the back and stood in the doorway of that yard and did not come out onto the boards, and the chest is behind her with eleven sacks along the wall of the back room and a groove in the floor under them with a crust of salt in the bottom of it.**

"You have put a coat on my yard."

"**I have put my own coat on a pan I made, in a yard I do not work in, on a beam I left here on the seventy-third day of that month. It is not your yard and I am not asking you for anything, and if you want it off I will take it off, and I will not think anything of it, and I would like all three of those said out loud in this yard this morning before anybody starts putting them in the other order.**"

"Somebody will put them in the other order."

"**Somebody will. About four people in this city will say that a man has covered a wage in a yard, and nobody will say that a man has covered a thing he made and left there twenty days ago, and I have covered a thing I made, and the reason I covered it is that there is about a finger of the dust off eleven miles of flats on the top of it and I would rather have that on a coat than on a sack.**"

"You have not said what the coat is for."

"**I have said what the coat is for twice in nine minutes and you have asked me what it is for twice, and about four people in this yard have worked out that a man who answers a question twice is a man who wants to be asked, and I have answered twice because the first answer was not believed and the second one was not either.**"

---

**And he put the coat over that pan by the two corners so that the middle of it stood about a hand's width off the salt, and the wind that has been at that wall since the seventy-fourth day of that month went under the hem of it and the hem lifted and came down and lifted again.**

"That will not stay."

"**That will not stay, and it is not supposed to stay. A cover on a pan is not a lid, and a lid has a date on it, and a cover has a man under it who is not going to be there at night, and I have not claimed that this beam is a kept thing, and I have said out loud in this yard that I am not going to carry it home, and nobody has worked out that nobody can read it, and I have not worked out which of those two it is either and I did not come up nine miles to work it out.**"

"You could take it in tonight."

"**I could take the pan in tonight and turn it out in a room and weigh what is in it, and that would be a figure with a day on it that anybody in four hundred miles could check, and the day would be the ninety-third day of that month, and I would be the man who put the first figure into that yard in twenty days, and I have not got a use for being that man on the ninety-third day of that month.**"

---

**And the pan on that beam is a length of wood like any other length of wood in this city, and every length of wood anybody in this city has been holding came off a rack, and about four people in this city worked that out inside three days and nobody has ever said it out loud in the yard where the beam is standing.**

"**And I have put my coat on it, and a man who puts a coat on a thing has admitted the thing is worth covering, and I have not decided yet whether I would rather it was worth covering or whether it was a quarter of a sack.**"

He said it to the boards of that yard and then he put both hands back on the two corners of the coat and stood there, and the wind went under the hem of it and lifted it about an inch and let it down.

"**And I have not weighed that beam since the fifty-eighth day of that month and I have not turned that pan out since the sixty-seventh, and about four people in this city have worked out that a man who owns an instrument and does not use it has decided something about the instrument, and nobody has worked out that I have decided something about the yard.**"

"You could make another one."

"**I could make another pan, and I would have to take four lengths out of two frames that are on somebody's rota, and about nine people in this city have worked out that a man who replaces the part of a thing is a man who has taken the thing over, and a few of them have worked out that a man who leaves a thing broken for twenty days is a man who has decided the thing is finished. I have decided neither and I have got a coat on it.**"

"That pan is wood."

"**That pan is wood and the two pieces of the far one were wood, and I have not said out loud in this yard which frames they came off and I am not going to today, because the day I say it in this yard is the day that two men who are on a rota have been told by a yard that their frames are in a beam, and about four people in this city have worked out that a beam with a pan on it is a column about a hole and nobody has worked out that it is a column about a rack.**"

---

**And the man of about fifty-two who digs stood in the middle of that yard at about the tenth hour with both hands on the two corners of his own coat and did not take them off, and about four people in that yard had worked out in three days that every length of wood anybody in this city is holding came off a rack, and the rest had worked out that the pan on that beam is one of those lengths, and nobody had put the two facts next to each other in a yard.**

"You have not taken it off."

"**I have not taken it off and I am not going to take it off, and the reason I came up this morning is that I made this beam on the fifty-eighth day of that month and I left it in this yard on the seventy-third day of that month and I have not been in this yard in twenty days, and nobody has worked out that he came back because he could not live with there being dust on it.**"

"It is only dust."

"**It is only dust off eleven miles of flats and there are about four hundred and forty racks on those flats and about four hundred and forty people work them, and this pan is the only piece of those frames in this yard that anybody can see, and about nine people in this city have worked out that a quarter of a sack cannot be divided and nobody has worked out that a coat cannot either, and I have got a coat on one of them and it is not staying.**"

---

**And the woman of about thirty-four in that store came out of the back again at about the eleventh hour and looked at that coat on that pan for about as long as it takes to fill a pail, and the beam was crooked on its block of stone and had been crooked since the fifty-eighth day of that month, and the coat had been on it for two hours and had not blown off.**

"You have made it crooked."

"**I made it crooked on purpose on the fifty-eighth day of that month because a beam that sits level on a stone has to have had the stone levelled, and there is nobody on the Reach who levels a stone for a beam that weighs nothing until it is in it, and nobody has worked out that the reason is the only part of it anybody is ever going to know.**"

"Would it weigh more straight."

"**It would weigh the same, and I could not tell you how much it weighs, and I have not weighed it since the fifty-eighth day of that month, and there is no figure on it anywhere in four hundred miles, and about nine people in this city have worked out that the man who made that beam does not know what it weighs, and a few of them in this city have worked out that a man who does not know what his own beam weighs has not used it to weigh anything, and I have used it three times.**"

"Three times for what."

"**For a ninth of a sack, for a quarter of a sack, and for the piece that is in that pan now, and nobody has worked out that three is the number of things in this yard that cannot be divided, and that is not a fact about the yard.**"

---

**And the sack of salt on the boards of that yard was eight feet from the beam on the ninety-third day of that month and about four feet from it on the ninety-fifth, and nobody in that yard moved it, and the man of about fifty-two who digs looked at the distance between the two things and said one thing about it and then went up the road.**

"They are further apart than they were."

"**They are, and nobody has moved either of them, and about four people in this city have worked out that two things in a yard drift apart on their own when the yard is used, and nobody has worked out that they drift together when the yard is not used, and there is no third thing that happens, and I have got a coat on one of them and it has not moved and that is the whole of what I did today.**"

"You could have put the sack on the pan."

"**I could have carried a bag I did not pay for off boards I do not work in and put it on a pan I made, and about nine people in this city have worked out that two things in a yard put together is a figure about a yard, and nobody has worked out that a figure about a yard is the only thing anybody would be able to check in this yard in three months, and I have decided that I would rather this yard had nothing anybody could check than one thing.**"

"So there is nothing to check."

"**There is a board on that wall with the figures of the sixty-ninth day of that month on it, and a cloth on a trough, and a coat on a pan, and a sack on some boards, and none of those four can be added to any of the others, and about four people in this city have worked out that four things that cannot be added up are a column, and nobody has worked out that there is no column here, there are four things.**"

---

**And a beam in the middle of that yard on a block of stone about the length of a man's own foot, with a coat held over the pan at two corners and the hem of it going up and coming down on the dust off eleven miles of flats.**

She went back inside and shut the door behind her to about the width of a hand, and the sack was still on the boards about four feet from the beam and nobody in that yard had picked it up, and the man of about fifty-two who digs did not take his coat off and did not go up the road until about the eleventh hour, and the wind had not got under the hem of it once in two hours.

---

**And the hem of a man's coat going up and coming down on the dust off eleven miles of flats, and not once under.**
