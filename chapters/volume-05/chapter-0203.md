# Chapter 0203 — The Forty-Eighth Day

The bell on the rail at the bottom of the Redroot cut went twice at about the seventh hour of the morning on the forty-eighth day of the Long Month, and a boy of eight rang it both times, and the board on the rail had the forty-eighth washed onto it at the top of the forty-fifth and the forty-fourth and the forty-first, and there was no rota on it and there was not going to be one.

It is a Monday. The queue's day is a Tuesday. There is no queue today and the store up the road past this city is open, and about four hundred and forty people are on eleven miles of flats with about nine racks that can still be worked, and seven days ago the water stopped coming down a channel because a household on this cut gave nine days of a week back unasked, and about nine people on this flat worked out for themselves which of them they were for about two hours and then said it out loud.

---

**And this is the day a woman of about thirty-eight who keeps that gate said in a kitchen on the twenty-fourth day of the Long Month that she would be angry about it on, and it is the forty-eighth, and about nine people in this city had it in a book and about four of them had said the date out loud in the last three days to somebody who did not need it said.**

Nobody went.

That is the whole of the first half of the forty-eighth day of the Long Month and it took about four minutes to establish, because about nine people had come up the road in the morning with a reason and a date, and a woman of about fifty-nine who keeps that house and a boy of eight came out onto the step and said the two sentences below, and about nine people stood on a road they had walked eleven miles for and did not go any further.

"**The day came.**"

"**It came at the seventh hour this morning, the same as every day, and nobody has gone up there, and I am not going to and I am not going to send the boy.**"

"**Somebody could go.**"

"**About nine of you could, and about four of you have come eleven miles to say that to me, and I have been in this house since the twenty-fourth day of the Long Month and I am the one who has to be in it for the rest of a year, and that is not a thing I am saying to be sad about. It is a thing I am saying because you are all going to go home and tell people that nobody went, and I would like the telling to have this in it and not something else.**"

"**And the second reason.**"

"**The second reason is that she has a boy of eight and I have a boy of eight and about four adults on that cut have said to about four of the other adults in the last three days that somebody ought to go, and if I send a child up that bank with a message then from today onward everybody in this city knows that this house checks on that gate, and after that every single thing she does for a year is a thing a household is watching. I am not going to do that to her to get nine of you home happy on a Monday.**"

---

**And a woman of about thirty-nine who keeps a public register, who came up the road on the same errand and for the same reason, wrote the two sentences down on a slate in the road outside that house in about four minutes, and read them back once and did not read them back twice, and then she said one more thing and put the slate down.**

"**I have written it. The day came and nobody went and she is the one who is in the house. I am going to take this slate to the room over the market and put it on the table where about two hundred and forty people saw the last one, and I am not going to improve it, and I am not going to read it out, and if anybody in this city wants to say that somebody ought to have gone, they can say it to a board and not to her.**"

"**You could have gone.**"

"**I could have, and I have not, and I have not been asked whether a Monday suited me and I was not going to be, and I want that entered in the same hand and at the same time as the other two things, and I want it entered by somebody who is not me.**"

The woman of about fifty-nine who keeps that house wrote it herself, on the step, in her own hand, and gave the slate back.

---

**And the household at the bottom of that cut is nine people poorer of a week's work than it was on the twenty-fourth day of the Long Month, and about four of them asked the woman of about fifty-nine who keeps it a question about a sack at about the eighth hour of the morning, and she gave them the only answer there is and it took her about four minutes.**

"**A sack came on the twenty-fifth. There is one due and I have not asked and about four of us have not asked. The days went back on the forty-first. Nobody in four hundred miles has worked out whether a thing that was given in exchange for days that have been given back is a thing that still comes, and the two of those sentences have been sitting in my kitchen since Monday morning and I have said them out loud to about four people this morning, which is the first time I have said either of them.**"

"**He gave the days and then you gave the days and that is twice he has been on the wrong end of it.**"

"**It is. And I am not going to be the one who says that sentence in a room with him in it, and the reason is not manners. A sack is a thing you ask for. And the day I walk eleven miles up a road and stand in a yard and ask a man of about thirty-four for a sack, about four hundred and forty people on those flats are going to hear about it inside four days, and about nine of them have never been on a rota, and the man I would be asking has about nine people on his who have. And I have known that for about six days and I have not said it to anyone, and I am saying it to you now, on my own step, with a date on it, and you may write it down.**"

"**Write it down where.**"

"**I do not care. I have never once in nine years been on a page and I am not going to start on a Monday morning because a man came eleven miles with a date in his head.**"

A woman of about thirty-nine who keeps a public register wrote it down on the second slate and read it back once and did not read it back twice, and it is in a book in this city in the words of a person who keeps a house.

---

**And a woman of about thirty-eight who keeps that gate came down to the cut at about the ninth hour of the morning to work the channel beside about four people from the store she had said yes to the day before, and she had the board in her left hand and she did not put it down once, and she did not say one word to any of them about the forty-eighth day of the Long Month, or about the twenty-fourth, or about a year.**

She worked. She is good at it and she has been doing it for nine years and about four people in this cut have said so since the twenty-fourth day of the Long Month and she has never once said so back.

A boy of eight was on the bank with nine feet of rope across his knees and eleven knots in it, and he had a blister and did not stop for it, and he was waiting to be told whether the bell was still going to be there on the fifty-first day of the Long Month, and nobody on that bank told him and about four of them had been asked and had said the same thing, which was that it would be there on the fifty-first.

---

**And a man of about thirty-one with a hand that will not close came up that road at about the second hour of the afternoon with about four other people behind him, and he had walked eleven miles for the same reason as the rest of them and he stood at the top of the cut and looked down at about nine people working a channel with no water in it, and he said the only sentence anybody said on that road all day that anybody is going to remember.**

"**Two things have happened on this cut in six days. A household gave nine days of a week back, and a woman who keeps a gate has been angry for a year, and about nine people in this city have only been able to talk about one of them, and it is the one that does not cost anybody anything this week.**"

"**That is not right.**"

"**I do not know that it is not right. That is the sentence and it is five words and I have been the only person in about nine rooms in three weeks who says *I do not know* out loud without being asked, and I am saying the other five words today and nobody asked me for those either, and I would like the two of them written down on the same line so that whoever reads that book in about four months knows that a man came eleven miles on a Monday to say it.**"

Nobody wrote them on the same line. A woman of about thirty-nine who keeps a public register wrote them on two lines, one above the other, and about four people in that road have said that she did that on purpose, and she has entered that she did.

---

**And at about the eleventh hour a man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall came up the road with about nine people behind him, and he had been told the same thing about that Monday by about four people on it, and he stood about nine feet back from the cut and said the sentence of the day, and it is five words and it is not about her.**

"**Nobody went. Write it down.**"

"**Write down what.**"

"**That is the finding and I have got about four of them in the last three days and they are all the same shape. A keeper said on the twenty-fourth that there would not be a second part and no reason, and about nine people in this city have been waiting since then for a day to arrive so that they could find out whether a thing with no second part is a thing that ends or a thing that goes on. The forty-eighth arrived. Nobody went. And that is not an answer, and I am not going to stand on a bank and tell anybody it is, and I am not going to go up there either, and I have said in about four rooms since the thirty-seventh day of the Long Month that nobody is going to go and get her and I am not going to start today.**"

---

**And a man of about thirty-two stood on the bank above that cut on the Monday morning and did not come down to it, which is the second Monday in a row and the second time in seven days, and about four people in that group worked out who he was and about nine of them did not say anything, and he said one sentence to a man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall and neither of them improved on it afterwards.**

"**You are not going either.**"

"**I am not going either. I have been asked in five days whether a day suits me twice and I said no twice and one of them was a Wednesday and one of them is this, and I am not going to be the man who walks up nine feet and asks a woman whether the thing she said on a date is still the thing she says on the date, because I have never in my life been able to ask anybody a question that has one answer and I am not going to learn it on that bank.**"

"**You would not have got one.**"

"**I would not have got one and she would not have given one and I would have spent a year of being angry at myself about it, and that is a thing I can do on my own and I have about three hundred days of it.**"

And it is entered that the man of about thirty-two said three hundred days, and that a year is a year, and that the number is his and not hers and that nobody in this city is going to be the one who ends it.

---

**And a board with a date washed onto the top of it, three dates under that, no rota on it, and a wet cloth hanging off the rail beside it, and nobody's hand on the cloth.**
