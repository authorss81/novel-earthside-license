# Chapter 0211 — The Last Tuesday

The queue formed along the wall of the store on the upper road at about the sixth hour of the morning on the fifty-sixth day of the Long Month. Rain had come in off the flats overnight and stopped about an hour before, and the ground under the queue was going hard in patches where about a hundred and forty people had stood on it. About nine of them had walked nine miles on foot to be in it. The ninth and last line of the posting was chalk on that wall about nine feet above the back of the last person standing under it, in a different hand from the other eight lines.

---

**And a woman of about thirty-four in that store, who is on no list in it, came out of the back with a slate in her hand and said the date out loud in the yard in front of about nine people. She had the cold end of the slate against her palm and kept changing it to the other hand. About four of the people in that yard had walked nine miles to hear a woman say a date, and one of them has said since that he did not know he was going to do that until he was doing it.**

"**The fifty-sixth day of the Long Month. It is a Tuesday and it is the last Tuesday a wage can come out of this chest, and I wrote that on the slate on the Saturday morning of the forty-sixth day of the Long Month and hung it on the chest so that nobody would have to ask, and I have said this date about four times since the forty-sixth day of the Long Month and this is the fifth time and I am saying it into a yard instead of into a back room, and I would like somebody to write down that I chose that.**"

"And the man at the front."

"**That is a man of about twenty-seven who washes the store's own board and writes the day's figures on it, and he has been at the front of that queue since the seventh week of this flood, and about four people in this city could name that hand and about nine of them could name him and he is on no roster of anything including this store's.**"

---

**And the board was down off the wall when Adrian Vale came up the road on foot at about the seventh hour, and the trough under it had water gone grey in it and a wet cloth folded over the rim. The board is washed every morning and it has the day's figures on it in that hand, and the posting is on the same wall about a foot to the left of it. Nobody had said this out loud before this morning and about four people in that yard have said it since.**

The figures on the board said that about nine people were paid yesterday and about a hundred and forty were not, and Adrian Vale read them out loud the way a man reads a number off a wall, and the man who washes the board wrung the cloth out over the trough and answered him.

"**Nine.**"

"**That is yesterday. That is what the board says about a day, and it is correct about that day, and I write the new figures on it at the seventh hour when I know what the day was, and until then it is about the day before, and it has been about the day before every morning since the seventh week of this flood.**"

"And what does it say about this morning."

"**It says what a day says. That is the whole of what a board with a day on it can say, and there is a page on the same wall nine feet up with a day at the top of it and a price under every line and no figure under any of the prices, and I have been looking at the two of them for ten days and I am not able to tell you which of the two I would rather have written.**"

Nobody in that yard answered him. The water in the trough went on moving for a while after he stopped moving the cloth. It is entered that about four people wanted to say something and had not worked out what, and that the man who washes the board went back to the water and said nothing else about it.

Adrian Vale stood by the door of that store for about nine minutes. He did not go into the queue and he did not go into the store, and there was a place at the back of it that would have held him. Then he went down the nine miles on foot. About four people in that yard have said since that a man walked nine miles on a day the queue was working and did not stand in it, and that about nine of them have not worked out what they think of that.

---

**And a man of forty-two came into that yard at about the seventh hour with his hands in his pockets and went to the back of the queue, which is where he has stood on a Friday and on a Monday and on a Tuesday. He had come nine miles and the rain had not finished drying on him. About four people in that yard noticed and about nine of them had stopped noticing, and it is entered that the noticing has gone down rather than up.**

The woman of about thirty-four in the store said the date one more time, to him, on its own, and he listened to it.

"**Say it once more.**"

"**The fifty-sixth day of the Long Month, and it is a Tuesday, and it is the last Tuesday a wage comes out of that chest, and about a hundred and forty people in this yard are going to be paid this morning and I am one of the four who write it down and I have said the date about five times today.**"

He did not say thank you. He stood with his hands still in his pockets until she had finished, and then he took them out and put them back. It is entered that he did not say it, and that about four people in that yard had been waiting for it.

---

**And then he said the number, once, in a yard, in daylight, in front of about nine people, and nobody improved on it and nobody joined it to anything, and it is a different debt and it is not a part of this morning.**

"**A hundred and twenty-six days is what the thirty-nine men under the near gate of the Lock of Salt have gone unpaid. The first of March was the sixtieth day of the new month. They are on no line of the page on that wall, they have not been on it since the Saturday it went up, and this morning I am taking a wage out of that chest for about a hundred and forty people, and those thirty-nine men are not one of them. Nobody in that yard is going to tell them, because I have worked out in about ten days that there is no telling of them that is not an argument, and I have lost that argument about four times and I am not going to have it a fifth time in a yard.**"

"Then open the chest."

"**I am going to open the chest.**"

---

**And he opened it at about the eighth hour in the middle of that yard, in daylight, in front of about nine people who walked nine miles to be in it. It is the settlement's chest and it is in the back of the store, and he carried it out himself and put it down on the boards in the middle of the yard, and the boards took the weight with a noise. About four people in that yard have said since that they did not know until it was down that he had ever moved it.**

There was salt in it. Not a wage, not in money and not in anything anybody there could take away with them — salt, in sacks, in a chest, and about four of the sacks had been opened and re-tied and the knots on those four were not the knots on the other ones. The salt had gone hard at the bottom of the sacks where it had been sitting longest, and the cold coming off it had a smell off the flats in it.

Nobody in that yard said a word for about a minute. A man at the front of the queue put his hand flat on the boards in front of him and left it there.

"**That is what a wage is made of, and that is all of it, and I did not know that until I had it out on the boards where about nine people I cannot name could see it, and I want it entered that I did not know, and I want it entered that I chose to open it in a yard and not in a back room, and I am not able to give you a good reason and the reason I have got is that it is the settlement's and not mine and I have held it in my own hands for about ten days and I cannot do that with salt.**"

Nobody in that yard asked him what was going to happen. It is entered that nobody did, and that about four of them have said since that they had decided before the lid was off that they were not going to ask him that in a yard, and that one of those four was a woman of about thirty-four who keeps a stall by a wharf, who had come nine miles on foot that morning and had not been to that store since the forty-fourth day of the Long Month.

---

**And a man of about twenty-nine from the settlement's workroom was in that queue with a slate he has carried since the forty-sixth day of the Long Month and has not put down, and he had put nine of the eleven lines of the posting into a page in a room over a market on the forty-fourth day of the Long Month. He held the slate under his arm the way a man holds something he has already been told he should not be carrying. He asked one question in the yard at about the eighth hour and about four people heard it.**

"Is that the wage."

"**I am not going to answer that in a yard.**"

"**Then answer it in a book.**"

"**I have got a book in a workroom nine miles down that road that goes nowhere and I have written in it since the thirty-eighth day of the Long Month that a wage is a thing somebody promised, and that a thing somebody promised has a day on it. What came out of that chest this morning was in it before I was born and it was in it on the fortieth day of this flood, and I am not able to say out loud in a yard that either of those is the wage. I am not going to find out which by asking a man who has just opened a chest in front of about nine people.**"

Nobody in that yard asked him a second question. The man of about twenty-nine from the settlement's workroom went back down the road on foot at about the ninth hour and did not put the slate down. The question he asked is the same one he asked in a yard on the forty-sixth day of the Long Month about the prices, and about four people in that yard have said since that a man can ask a question about a price twice since the forty-sixth day of the Long Month and be asking two different things.

---

**And about the ninth hour, when the yard had emptied down to about nine people and the sacks were still on the boards, a woman of about twenty-four who is on no roll of anything stood in front of the open chest and put her hand flat on the edge of it, and the iron took the heat out of her hand in about the time it takes to say what she said. It took her about nine seconds to get to and about four people have said since that they had expected something better.**

"**A wage came out of this chest this morning. About a hundred and forty people have been paid. That is a fact and it is on about nine boards and it is in a book and it is correct. And a page on that wall has a price under every line and no figure under any of the prices, and it has been up ten days, and I have read it ten times and I have not once been able to say what one line of it is worth, and I have not once been able to say who is on it.**"

"Write that down and it is a finding."

"**It is not a finding. It is two things I do not know sitting next to each other, and I have been writing them down separately since the second day of this flood and I am not going to join them up on a Tuesday morning with the chest open, because the day I join them up is the day this city has a column.**"

She wrote them down separately, one under the other with a finger's width of slate between them, and she did not join them up. It is entered that about four people in that yard watched her do it, and that nobody asked her to read either of them back.

---

**And at about the fourth hour of the afternoon a man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall came up the road and stood in front of the open chest with his hands in his sleeves, and he read the ninth line on the wall above it without looking up, because he does not need to look up at a page he wrote. He said two sentences about the chest and then went and stood at the back of that yard and said nothing else all afternoon, and his hands stayed in his sleeves the whole time.**

"**That is not a wage and I am not going to pretend it is one today. I have been the man who puts sentences into documents in this city since the thirty-seventh day of the Long Month and I am not going to be the man who says a chest of salt is a wage, and if it is a wage then a man of about fifty-two who digs is going to tell me what a day of it weighs, and he will, and I will not be able to answer him.**"

"It is his salt."

"**It is the settlement's salt and the settlement's salt came off the settlement's racks, and there are about four hundred and forty people on eleven miles of flats who put nine measures a day into that same water and have never been on a rota and are not on a line of that page, and none of them was in this yard this morning and none of them will be told anything about a chest, and I am not going to stand here and work out what that is. I have worked it out four times in ten days and I do not have a fifth answer.**"

---

**And the sacks stayed on the boards of that yard until dark, and the light went off the roof of the store and came back up off the wet ground and went out again, and about nine people came and looked at the sacks and did not touch them. The woman of about thirty-four in the store did not put a cloth over them and did not move them, and it is entered that about four people in that yard have said since that a cloth over a chest in a yard is a sentence and that she did not say one.**

**And a chest stood open on the boards of a yard nine miles up the road with salt in it, and about nine people had seen, and nobody had said a word about what a day of it is worth.**
