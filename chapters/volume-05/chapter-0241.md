# Chapter 0241 — Nine Feet Round It

A sack of salt that came off a man's chest on the eighty-fifth day of the Long Month was still on the boards of the yard of the store on the upper road at about the sixth hour of the morning on the eighty-sixth day of that month, and the woman of about thirty-four in that store went round it four times between the back of that building and the front door inside a quarter of an hour. The cold came off eleven miles of flats in a flat way under a sky the colour of a wet board, and the wind has been at that wall since the seventy-fourth day of that month, and it had got under the near corner of the sacking and lifted it about the width of two fingers. Under that corner there was a hand's breadth of boards a different colour from the boards either side of it, and the woman of about thirty-four in that store put the heel of her boot on the sacking and pushed the corner down about an inch and left it.

---

**And the man of about twenty-seven who washes that store's board came out of the door at about the seventh hour with the trough under his arm and stopped four feet short of going round the sack.**

"You are going to move it."

"**I am going to go round it, and I have not decided to move it, and nobody in this yard has. I would like that said before anything else this morning, because a man who goes round a thing four times in a quarter of an hour has decided something and does not know he has decided it, and I have watched myself do it out there and I have not got a name for it.**"

"Have you decided anything since the sack came down."

"**I have decided one thing and it is about a wall and not about a sack. I have decided that I am not throwing a bucket of water up that wall, and I have been washing a board at the foot of that wall every morning since the seventh week of this flood, and the difference between a board and a wall is about nine inches and the difference between those two is the ninth line.**"

---

**And the round took nine feet where four would have done it, and the boards between that door and that sack are the boards that everybody in that building walks on, and a foot of them going pale inside a morning is not a mark anybody put there.**

"Four feet is a walk."

"**Four feet is a walk and nine feet is a walk with a thing standing in the middle of it, and both of them put a person at the same door. This yard has had two ways to that door since the sack came down and it had one before, and I am not going to tell you which of the two I would rather everybody took, because I have never once chosen a line in this yard and I am not starting at the seventh hour on the eighty-sixth day of the Long Month.**"

He put the trough down under the wall. About two fingers of the water in it went over the rim and put a dark line on the boards at the foot of it, and by the time he had lifted the trough and hung it back the dark line had gone the colour of the boards either side of it, and he stood looking at that for a moment longer than the job took.

---

**And the board on that wall has not been written on since the seventieth day of the Long Month, and the figures on it are the figures of the sixty-ninth day of that month, which was seventeen days ago, and the board is about a foot from the page above it and has been since the forty-sixth day of that month.**

"Your board is wrong."

"**My board is correct and seventeen days old, and those are two different things. A board that is correct and silent is a board that can be checked by anybody who walks up nine miles to look at it, and I have not written on it since the seventieth day of that month because a board with nothing on it says that nothing happened on a day, and about four people in this city have worked out that is the same as the day, and nobody in that yard has worked out the other thing.**"

"You could write the date and nothing else."

"**I could put the eighty-sixth day of the Long Month at the top of that board and nothing under it, and that would be a page that says a day happened and nothing else. About four people in this yard would read that as a day with no wage on it, and the rest of that yard would read it as a day when the sack was in the way, and I am not going to be the man who puts either of those two on a board at the seventh hour of the morning.**"

---

**And by the eighth hour there was a line in that yard about the width of a boot going round that sack, and it was not a mark anybody had made, and it is not going to be a mark anybody can read.**

"You are watching dirt."

"**I am watching a line, and a line is a thing this city has a word for and I have got a use for that word this morning and I am not going to use it. A line somebody chose is a line. A line nobody chose is a day, and I have washed that board every morning since the seventh week of this flood and I have never once left a day standing that was not there, and this morning there is a day going round a sack in the dirt and I did not put it there and neither did the man who carried it up here.**"

"Who did."

"**Nobody. That is the whole of it, and it is the reason I am standing at this wall instead of washing that board, because a track in a yard has no date on it and no hand on it and about four people in this city will be walking on it inside a year without knowing that it is there.**"

He crouched and put two fingers on the line where it turned the corner of the sack. The grit under them was darker where the wind had packed it and paler where a boot had gone, and he took his hand away without saying anything about either of those two things, and then he stood up and put his thumb and his forefinger together and shook the grit off into the yard.

---

**And the woman of about thirty-four in that store came out into that yard with her own wage under her arm, where it has been since the fifty-sixth day of that month, and stood at the corner of the boards where the line turned and put her own boot on the same corner twice.**

"That is going to be there longer than that sack is."

"**It is going to be there longer than that sack is, and nobody in this yard is going to know the day it started, and I have been on the other side of that wall every morning since the seventh week of this flood putting a day on a board that a man could read, and this morning the first day I have ever put on the ground in this city went down without me, and about nine of the people in that yard have not looked at the ground at all.**"

"Then put a date on it."

"**You are asking me to do the thing I have done every morning for eleven weeks to a board, and I have never done it to the ground. If I did it to the ground it would be a date about nine feet long, and a date about nine feet long is not a date. I would be putting the eighty-first of nothing on the dirt so that in a year somebody could be angry at a sack.**"

"And what will they be angry at."

"**At the sack, and behind the sack at the man who carried it, and behind the man at the queue, and behind the queue at about four people in this city who can all tell you what happened in this yard and not one of whom can tell you what day.**"

---

**And the man of about twenty-seven took the trough in to the back room at about the ninth hour because the water in it had gone the colour of the boards, and the woman of about thirty-four in that store told him to leave it where it was, and he left it, and then she had to fill a pail herself.**

"You could take it in."

"**I could take it in and I am not going to take it in, because I took it out to the wall this morning and put it down by the sack and it has not moved, and a trough is a thing I own the position of, and I do not own the position of a trough in the middle of a walking line.**"

"Then I will fill it myself."

"**You will fill it yourself and you will have to walk round that sack to do it, and I have just watched you do that four times this morning, and I am not going to be the reason you do it a fifth.**"

She went and got the pail and carried it to the trough and back with the sack in the way, and the pail was heavy and she set it down twice on the way and he did not offer to take it and she did not ask him, and the water went over the side of the pail onto the boards about a hand's width from the line and made a patch there about the size of two hands.

"**I have been in this yard four hours and I have put nothing down in it that was not already there when I came, and that is the first morning of this flood where that is true, and I do not know what to do with a morning like this one.**"

"That is a fourth mark."

"**That is a fourth mark and I did not put it there, and neither did you, and the fifth one will be the boot of whoever comes through this yard next, and about four people in this city have worked out that a yard collects marks the way a floor does and nobody has worked out that a floor is the one you can sweep.**"

---

**And a man of about twenty-nine from the settlement's workroom came through the middle of that yard at about the ninth hour with a slate under his arm and the wrong side up, and about nine people in that yard knew that slate and three of them had been carrying something for the man who wrote it.**

"That is the third time he has come through here since the seventieth day of that month."

"**It is, and he has not stopped at that trough and he has not stopped at that wall and he has not stopped at that sack, and about four people in this city wrote a thing down on a slate on the seventieth day of that month about a name they were not going to say, and he has carried that slate for twenty-one days with the wrong side up and has not put it down once.**"

"He is not on anything either."

"**He is not on anything and neither am I and there is a man standing about nine feet from that gate with a pail at his feet who is not on anything, and three people in a yard going round a sack is not a rota, and I would like it entered that it is not a rota.**"

---

**And Adrian Vale came in at the eighth hour with the trough full and had to set it down in the open and go round that sack to bring it the last nine feet, and he did the whole of it without stopping and without looking down at his own feet.**

"You went round it."

"**I went round it. There is a trough and there is a sack and the trough is heavier than the sack, and I am not going to break a thing a man carried nine miles in order to save four feet of a yard. About nine of the people in that yard this morning have worked out that a man who goes round a thing is a man who has accepted it, and I have not accepted it and I have not refused it, and the difference between those two is about nine feet and I have got it exactly.**"

"You could say that to the man."

"**The man is nine miles down that road and he has come up it twice since the eighty-sixth day of that month and I do not know that he has come up it twice, and I am not going to walk after somebody to tell him what I think about his own sack. He put it down on the eighty-fifth day of that month and I have been in this city two hundred and forty-one days and I have learned exactly one thing about sacks and it is that they are heavier when they are not open.**"

He set the trough down against the wall about four feet from the sack. His hands were wet to the wrist and the water in the trough was the colour of the water in the channel at the bottom of the Redroot cut, which is the colour of nothing at all, and it put a ring on those boards the width of the trough, and the ring and the boot-line and the nine feet were three marks in one yard inside two hours.

---

**And the man of about twenty-seven got the cloth out of the trough at about the ninth hour and wrung it once and stopped, and then he wrung it a second time and hung it on the rim where it hangs every morning, and he did not throw a bucket of water at anything.**

"You have not done the wall."

"**I have not done the wall and I am not going to do the wall, and I have been at the foot of that wall every morning since the seventh week of this flood and this is the first morning I have got away from it. About four people in this city have worked out that a man who stops doing a thing he has done every morning has decided something, and the rest of this city has worked out that he is tired, and I have not got a use for either of those two and I have got about a bucket of water and a cloth and no morning left.**"

"Will you do it tomorrow."

"**I do not know. I have said that about a board nineteen times since the seventieth day of that month and every one of those times I knew by the fourth hour of the morning. This morning it is the ninth hour and I still do not know, and I have gone from a thing I can predict to a thing I cannot, and I do not think that is a step up.**"

"You did not know about the ninth line either."

"**I did not know about the ninth line and I have not asked anybody about it, and about nine people in this city have been in this yard in the last ten days and not one of them has asked me whether it works, and I have not asked them, and that is two rooms of people not asking each other a question in a yard where the answer is written at the ninth foot of a wall.**"

---

**And the woman of about thirty-four in that store stood in the doorway of that yard for about as long as it takes to fill a pail and did not go out onto the nine feet, and the boards under the sack were the colour they had been before the sack came down, and the trough was against the wall about four feet from it with a dark ring round its base the width of the trough.**

"You are looking at the ground."

"**I am looking at the ground and I have been looking at it since the sixth hour, and there are four things on it now and about nine feet of it that nobody has walked on, and about four of those four things are mine.**"

"Which four."

"**The ring round that trough, the wet patch where the pail went over this morning, the heel of my own boot on that sacking, and the line your boots have made going round it. That is four, and I have not put a date on one of them, and I have been in this building since the seventh week of this flood and I have never once seen a mark come up in a yard that I could read the way I can read a board.**"

"Could you read a groove."

"**I could read a groove the way I read a board, and I would rather not, and there is one in the floor of that back room four inches deep at the end the sacks go down at and there is a crust of salt in the bottom of it along its whole length, and nobody in this building can say which sack that came out of, including me, and I have stopped wanting to.**"

---

**And a line about the width of a boot going round a sack in the dirt, with the boards inside it not walked on at all.**
