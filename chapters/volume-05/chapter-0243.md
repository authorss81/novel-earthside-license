# Chapter 0243 — What The Gap Filled With

A gap about the width of a person had been left on the boards of a stall by a wharf since the eighty-first day of the Long Month, and on the eighty-eighth day of that month it had a finger of grit in the bottom of it, and nobody had swept it, and the grit had come in off eleven miles of flats on a wind that has not stopped since the seventy-fourth day of that month. The gap is between the boards of that stall and the front of them. A bag of salt has been sitting on those boards since the fifty-sixth day of that month, and it is a wage out of the settlement's chest, and the woman of about thirty-four who keeps that stall has not put a hand on it since the day it came out of that chest, and the boards of that stall are swept at the end of every market day by a girl of seventeen who is on no roll of anything in this city and who cannot be fetched.

---

**And the girl of seventeen came along the front of those boards at about the sixth hour of the evening with a broom and stopped at the gap, and about four people in that market had seen her stop there the four times she had come at it since the eighty-first day of that month, and the rest of that market had not been in it at six hour on any day at all.**

"That is two sweeps behind."

"**That is two sweeps behind, and it is not two sweeps of dust, it is a bag of salt standing in a place where a broom does not go, and if I sweep that gap this evening I have to move that bag, and if I move that bag I am a person who has moved somebody's wage to clean underneath it.**"

"You could sweep round it."

"**I have swept round it four times since the eighty-first day of that month and it has got worse every one of those four times, and the reason it has got worse is that a broom takes a board clean in about three strokes and takes a board next to a thing nobody has touched in seven days in about nine, and the nine is where the grit comes from.**"

She put the head of the broom on the boards at the near end of the gap and pushed it the length of the gap. The grit did not go with it. It went sideways into the seam at the edge of the bag and stopped there, and about half of it stayed where it was, and she looked at what she had done for a while and then she swept the rest of that stall and did not come back to it.

---

**And a man came along the front of that stall at about the sixth hour with a basket on his arm and put his near foot straight down into the gap and put his other foot out flat to stop himself going over, and about nine people in that market saw a man put his foot in a place where there was nothing to be, and the rest of that market had been in it long enough to know what it was.**

"**And I have swept this stall at the end of every market day for about four years and I have never once had to decide about a gap, and about four people in this market have worked out that a girl of seventeen who decides about gaps is a girl who is going to be asked about them, and nobody has worked out that nobody has asked me about this one and I have told the whole of it to a girl of seventeen and a bag of salt.**"

"You could have held him."

"**I could have held him and I did not, and I was about four feet off him with a broom in my hand, and I have thought about that for about the length of a market day and here is what I have got. If I hold him up then I am the reason a man with a basket got across a gap, and he will remember the gap and not the crossing, and in about nine years that gap will be a thing a man puts his foot in.**"

"He would have fallen."

"**He did not fall, and if he had fallen I would have got there, and the reason I did not get there is that a girl of seventeen is not a person a market comes to with a hand out, and if I start doing it I will be doing it four times a market day and there are about four stalls in this row where somebody's foot is going to go into something.**"

He got himself upright, laughed at nothing in particular, and put his basket on the boards at the near end of that stall, and the bag of salt was about a foot from his elbow and he did not touch it and did not ask what it was.

---

**And the woman of about thirty-four who keeps that stall had been standing at the back of her own boards through all of it with her arms crossed, and she had not said one word until the girl had put the broom back in its corner against the wall.**

"You will not do it again."

"**I will do it on the eighty-ninth day of that month and the ninetieth, and I will not get that board any cleaner than I have got it, and about four people in this market have worked out that a girl of seventeen who does not get a board cleaner is a girl who is not trying, and nobody else in this market has worked out that she is trying and that there is a bag on those boards. Both of those are about me and neither of them is about her.**"

"He will ask me why."

"**He will ask me why and I will tell him the same thing every time and it will not get less true, and the second time a person tells somebody a thing that is true they hear the first time in it, and I have not got a way of stopping that.**"

She put her hand flat on the top of the bag where the sacking had gone stiff, and she took it off again, and the place her hand had been was the colour of the bag and the rest of the bag was the colour of the market, and she had done that three times since the fifty-sixth day of that month and she had never once done it on a market day.

---

**And about four people in that market came past that gap on their way out and went round it, and going round it took them the width of a person in a market that is four streets from a room over a market, and the rest of that market went round it without knowing there was anything to go round.**

"That is a hole in your boards."

"**That is a gap I have had since the eighty-first day of that month and it is a gap about the width of a person and I have not put anything in it. You are the fourth person this evening to walk round it and the first one to say it out loud, and a hole in boards gets a board put across it, and a gap in boards gets nothing.**"

"Then nothing will ever go in it."

"**Something will go in it. Not a thing anybody chooses. The wind off those flats has been at this row since the seventy-fourth day of that month and it does not know what a wage is, and nobody has worked out that in about nine days it will be the only thing in this row that is being filled.**"

---

**And the woman of about thirty-four who keeps that stall put her hand on the bag at about the seventh hour and then took it off, and about four people in that market had been waiting seven days for her to do that, and the rest had worked out in advance that she would do it and be told not to, and she was not told.**

"You have touched it."

"**I have touched it. I have had my hand on the top of that bag four times in seven days and I have not moved it and I have not opened it, and nobody has worked out that the second one costs more than the first.**"

"Does it want opening."

"**It wants opening the way the boards want sweeping, and the reason I am not going to open it is not that I do not know what is in it. I know what is in it. It is a quarter of a sack out of a chest that has not been opened since the fifty-sixth day of that month, and it went hard on the sixty-sixth day of that month, and I have not broken it, and I am not going to, and nobody has worked out that a person who has decided not to know is a person who has to keep the not-knowing in a place where the dust can get at it.**"

"Then the gap is the dust's."

"**The gap is the only ground in this row that a broom does not reach, and nobody has worked out that I have not been keeping it, and I have been standing four feet off it twice a day for seven days.**"

---

**And a woman of about fifty-six who keeps a shop four streets down from that market stopped at the front of that stall at about the seventh hour with a basket on her arm and looked at the gap and then at the bag and then said a thing that about four people in that row heard and nobody repeated.**

"Two days of wind on that cloth and I have not shaken it out."

"**Two days of wind on that cloth since the seventy-fourth day of that month and I have not shaken it out and I am not going to, and nobody has worked out that a thing that is being kept on a stall in a market is a thing somebody is waiting to be asked about, and nobody has asked me and nobody is going to.**"

"Will you ask somebody."

"**No. I have a number in this market that I have not spent since the forty-seventh day of that month, and nobody has worked out that I am not saving it, I am not carrying it round with me, and the difference between those two is the whole of what I am doing on these boards.**"

She went four streets down to her own shop with her nine measures still hard under her counter and the basket still on her arm, and the gap in the boards of that stall was about a finger deep by the time the light went off the water and it was not swept.

---

**And the girl of seventeen came back at about the seventh hour to put the stall's awning down and found the grit in that gap had a skin on it, and she stood looking at it for about as long as it takes to fold an awning and then she did the awning instead and left the grit, and about nine feet of the front of that stall went into the dark with a finger of the flats in it.**

"You will tell me in the morning."

"**I will tell you in the morning that it is the same as it was at the sixth hour this evening and that I have not done it, and you will not believe me, because a person who comes at a thing every day and gets a different answer every day is a person who is not looking.**"

"I am looking."

"**You are looking at the boards, and I am looking at the gap, and about four people in this market have worked out that those are two different things on the same ground.**"

---

**And the boards of that stall had been swept at the sixth hour for about nine years and a girl of seventeen had been doing it for about four of them, and the boards under the front rail were the colour of boards that get wet, and the grit in the gap was the colour of the flats.**

"How do you do the front."

"**I do the front last and I do it from the rail side and not the market side, because if I do it from the market side then the grit goes out into the row, and about four people in this market worked that out in about four years and nobody has written it down, and now there is a gap and I cannot do the front at all.**"

"You could do it over the bag."

"**I could reach the front rail from the back of that stall and put the broom over the top of that bag and get the boards either side of it, and I worked that out at about the sixth hour before I said anything to you, and it would take about nine feet of arm that I have got, and the reason I am not going to do it is that the broom would go over a wage and a broom going over a wage is a thing a person watches.**"

"Then leave the front."

"**I will leave the front. There is a strip about the width of a hand along the front of that stall that has not been swept since the eighty-first day of that month, and by the ninetieth it will be a strip about the width of two hands, and nobody has worked out that the place has got a strip in it now and the strip is mine.**"

---

**And a finger of grit in the bottom of a gap about the width of a person, with nothing put in it and no hand on it.**
