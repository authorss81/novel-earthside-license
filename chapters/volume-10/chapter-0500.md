# Chapter 0500 — The Board Washed At The Fourth Hour

The lid was off the box that stands on the step by that store's door, at about the ninth hour on the hundred and eighty-fifth day of the Bare Month, a Thursday. The woman of about twenty-nine who keeps a public register was at the foot of that wall with her left hand flat on the stone beside the trough, and the store's own chalk was in her right hand. The board was on its two nails above her, with the account of the two hundred and twenty-fourth day of this flood on the left of it and a figure of this city's own in chalk on the right of them a hand's width clear, and neither had been rubbed out. The trough was full and the bucket was standing in it and the rag was on the stone lip, and the wall above the board was not washed. What washing went on below it stopped where the board stopped.

---

She put the chalk on the board to the right of the figure that was already there, a further hand's width clear of it. There is no place on that board that has been wiped and there was none before she began. One figure, and the second place of that figure had a stroke in it, and she did not hurry it. Then she took her hand off it and said what it was before anybody said anything.

"**This is a second figure of the same kind of thing as the one on that board already, made in this city, and it is not the same as that one. The two of them do not agree with one another any more than that one agrees with the other set.**"

A man at the end of the row said the thing that is said, to the wall and not to her.

"That is three of them on one board and nobody has said which is the newest."

"**The one on the far right is the newest, and the one on the right of that is older, and the two of them on the left of them are older than either. After this morning there are three things on one board and no way at all for a person standing here to find out which of the three would have been there if only one of them had been, and I have thought about that.**"

Nobody thanked her for that and nobody argued with her about it, and nobody told her she was right.

---

A woman at a stall said the thing that is said to the flags and not to the row.

"Who washes it."

"**The man who washes it washes it every morning. He washed it before the figure went up and he washes it now with three sets of figures on it, and the wall above it is not washed and is not going to be washed, and neither of those two things is a thing I can do anything about and I would not do it if I could.**"

Two stalls along that row the man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall had his own board standing face in against his own trestle, and his hand did not go into his coat at all that morning. Down that row came the man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board, with his own board under his arm. He read the figure that was on the right of the wall, and he went on the length of the row and did not stop at the end of it the way he does.

Nine people were at that end of that market when the third figure went up, and about four of them read it, and about five of them did not. Not one of the nine can say which of the three is the one anybody should be reading, and there is nothing on that board that says which of the three it is.

---

At the fourth hour the board came down off its two nails and the man who washes it carried the bucket to it, and he washed every inch of all three sets of figures and stopped at the top of the board where the wall begins. All three of them came out of that wet as clean as they went into it. The rag went back on the stone lip and the bucket went back into the trough. The wall above that board is as dirty now as it was at the fourth hour yesterday, and it was as dirty then, and it goes on being washed at the bottom of that board and nowhere above it.

Out of this city, past the last named house, a road goes up and a fence stands along the top of it, and there are three barrows on that road, a hundred and nine days out, measured from the day they went, which is the three hundred and ninety-first day of this flood. There is not one person on that road anybody in this city can be shown, and there never has been, and the three of them are on it.

A city that has put three sets of figures on one wall it can wash every morning has not decided anything, and a road nobody can be shown one person on is a road a column has nothing to say about, and the two of them are the same kind of silence, and there is nothing in four hundred miles that can tell anybody which of the two is the worse one to have.
