# Chapter 0486 — The Man Who Washes The Board

It was about the tenth hour on the hundred and seventy-first day of the Bare Month, a Thursday, and the man of about twenty-seven who washes the store's board came up the stair at the back of the room over a market with a bucket in his left hand and a rag over his right shoulder. The hundred and seventy-first day of the Bare Month was in chalk on the door behind him, and no name was on that door. He set the bucket down on the step outside and folded the rag in half and laid it on the sill. He wiped both hands down the front of his coat and came in and stood at the back of that room with his hands at his sides. About nine people were in there and none of them had been sent for anything, and the woman of about twenty-nine who keeps a public register was at the bench with her own working slate face up on it. The register was on its shelf behind her with its spine to the wall and it was not opened.

---

She did not look up off the slate for a while. Then she put the flat of her hand on the table beside it and said it to the room and not to him, and she said the first of it once and did not say it again.

"**Nobody has ever asked you what you are doing to that board, and I am going to ask you. I am asking you because a figure is going on that wall, and because you are the only person in this city who has been at it every morning since the seventh week of this flood, and there is no instrument in four hundred miles that gets a person in front of a wall. I am not going to pretend to you that this is one.**"

He did not say anything for about as long as it takes a man to decide whether a room is going to say anything else. Then he said it, once, in his own mouth, out loud, in that room, in that light. He said it the way he had said everything else he had ever said in a room, which is once.

"**What is left of the old day.**"

Nobody thanked him for it. Nobody asked him what he meant and nobody improved on it, and nobody said the thing back to him to hear how it sounded. About nine people were in that room and not one of them wrote a word of it on anything, and the woman who keeps the register put the flat of her hand back on the table beside the slate and left it there. A man at the back of that room said the thing that is said.

"I did not catch that."

"**You did not catch it because I am not going to say it a second time and neither is he, and that is the whole of what has happened in this room this morning. I am not going to stand here and improve on it or explain it or ask him whether he meant one thing or another, and there is nobody in this city who is going to be able to ask him that after today either.**"

Nobody said one word to the man who had said it. The light came in at the back step and went along the flags and reached the near side of the bench. The man of about twenty-seven stood at the back of that room with his hands at his sides and let it do that. Then he picked his rag up off the sill and went back down the stair with it.

---

He went down that stair and out through the door at the bottom into the middle of that market. The trough at the end of the row was full, and the rag was on the stone lip, and what was chalked on that board was the account of the two hundred and twenty-fourth day of this flood and not one stroke besides it. The wall over the top of that board was exactly as dirty as it had been the morning before and will be the morning after that, and there was no instrument in four hundred miles that would ever have told anybody which of those two things he had come up that stair to do.
