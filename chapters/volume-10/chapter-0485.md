# Chapter 0485 — Round A Board On The Flags

Adrian Vale had both hands on the frame of a barrow of stall boards at about the eighth hour on the hundred and seventieth day of the Bare Month, a Wednesday, and he was taking it down the row to the end of it and back again. About four people at that end of the market stood clear of the frame while he did it, and a woman further along that row had to lift the foot of her own board off the flags to get the wheel of it past. The man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board came along the same row at the same time with his own board under his arm and nineteen rates on it. There is no other way past the end of a barrow, and he was still walking round that end when he said the thing that is said.

Above the trough at that end of the market the store's board was still up where it has been, and what it carried was the figures of the two hundred and twenty-fourth day of this flood and not one other mark. Nobody had washed the wall over the top of it. Two stalls along from it a man kept a stall, and a piece of chalk of his own was in his own inside breast pocket, and it had not been out of it since the forty-third day of the Short Month, which is the two hundred and ninety-eighth day of this flood.

---

He did not stop walking round the barrow to say it, and he did not say it again for the rest of that day.

"**A figure on a wall that anybody can read is a figure the first man through who disagrees with it can change. I have watched that happen on boards in four markets for as long as I have carried this one, and a man who goes out onto eleven miles of flats with a slate and knocks comes back with a different figure from a man who goes out and does not knock.**"

He stood with the board under his arm and the face of it turned out into the row, and the face of that board was the only thing about him that anybody in that market had ever been able to read without being close to it. He had taken a name off it in his own hand in about the ninth year of this flood and had never said so to anybody, and the nineteen rates were still on it and the space where that name had been was not filled. Nobody at that end of the market had ever asked him about the space, and he had never offered it, and about four people in that row had looked at the face of that board that morning and not one of them had looked at the space.

A woman at the stall two along said the thing that is said.

"What about the two on the slate."

"**There is a slate in that room with the two of them on it and the two of them are not the same, and the difference between them is the knocking, and the reason I am standing here saying it out loud is that there is going to be something on that wall and the whole of it is decided and I am the only man in this market who has watched what happens to a figure like that.**"

Nobody answered him and nobody improved on what he said, and about four people at that end of the market heard the whole of it and went on with what they were doing. One of them was a woman who had been coming to that end of that market since before the water and who had never once in her life watched a man with a board be right about anything. She stood with her hand on the edge of her own board for a while afterwards, and then she went and picked up her rag and wrung it out onto the flags. At about the fourth hour the barrow was put against the wall at the end of that row and the boards on it were stacked three deep along the flags, and the man who had been pushing it put the flat of his hand on the top board to see whether it was steady and it was. The flags under the near wheel of that barrow were wet and the flags two feet away from them were not, and nobody at that end of the market had asked him why and he was not going to say.

A man at the far end of that row said the thing that is said.

"That is not your wall."

"**I have never once said it was my wall and I have never once said it was anybody's. I said it because there is going to be something on it and I am the only man here who has watched what happens to a figure like that, and if I am not going to say it now then there is nobody on this row who is going to say it at all.**"

Two stalls along from the store, the man who keeps a stall had heard the whole of what was said there. He put the flat of his hand on his own board where it hung on its string and left it there while a barrow went past him, and then he took the hand off and wiped it down the front of his apron and put it at his side. Then he said one thing, and it took him about as long as it takes to say it, and he said it to the row and not to the man with the board under his arm.

"**There is a gap in the writing on that board and you cannot see it from where I stand.**"

Nobody thanked him for it and nobody argued with him about it, and the man of about thirty-nine turned the face of his own board about as far as a hand's width towards himself and looked at the gap in it and put it back the way it was and went on down the row with it under his arm. The man who keeps a stall did not offer himself to anything else that day, and he did not offer himself to anything the day after, and there has not been an offer from that stall in this market since the hundred and fifty-seventh day of the Bare Month when he made one in a room and nobody in that room accepted it and nobody refused it.

---

The woman at the stall two along from the man who keeps a stall waited until the barrow had gone to the end of the row and come back. Then she came out from behind her own board with her hands in the front of her apron and asked the man of about thirty-nine a question, and it was the first question anybody at that end of that market had asked him in as long as he has carried a board.

"Tell me whether I should say them out loud instead of putting them on."

He stood on the flags with his board under his arm and looked at the board and not at her.

"**I do not know, and there is not one person in this market who does. I have been telling you what a figure on a wall does to a person, and I have not said one word about what you should do about it, and I would rather say I do not know to you this morning than say something in the afternoon that turns out to be what I said this morning.**"

Nobody said anything to him about that. She went back behind her own board and took her rag off it and wrung it out onto the flags and hung it over the edge again, and she said nothing to anybody.

She did not ask him anything else that morning and she will not, and what she took out of the answer she got was that a man with a board does not know what a figure on a wall does to the person who has to stand next to it. She put that in with everything else she knows about that wall, and he went on down the row with the board under his arm and did not stop at that end of the market the way he does.
