# Chapter 0461 — The Light Is On The Water And There Is No Wall On That Wharf

Adrian Vale had both hands on the frame of a barrow at about the seventh hour on the hundred and forty-sixth day of the Bare Month, a Sunday, at the bottom of that street where the wharf boards run out into the water, and the wind was coming off the water from the south-west and there was no wall anywhere on that wharf for any light to get onto. The boards were wet and the salt had come up on them in the night and dried white in the wind. A stack higher than a man stood at the far end with a sailcloth over the top of it and one corner of the sailcloth was working loose and going about once a minute. The man of about thirty-eight at the salt wharf was sitting on a low block with his knees up and his back to the wind and a strip of clear board about as long as a cart lying on the boards beside him.

---

He was not asked anything. Nobody on that wharf asked Adrian Vale where he had come from or what he was doing or whether he had eaten, and nobody on that wharf decided not to ask him, and there was nobody standing about waiting for him to be useful. He had been given the barrow at the top of the steps by a man who had gone back inside before he got to the bottom of them, and the arrangement had not been explained to him because there was nothing to explain. The sack went up on his knees at the top, went down to the lighter, and came back. The wind took the white off the boards and blew it back over the same boards.

The man of about thirty-eight said the figure out loud once, to nobody, the way he said it four times a year.

"**About four hundred and forty.**"

---

It had been cut into the strip in a hand that was not a clerk's hand, and it stood about a third of the way along it, and there was nothing else on the strip at all — no heading, no line under the heading, nothing under that. The round it goes up the road on had been on the road and back before that Sunday, and the strip had been cut on the Friday of it and had been lying on those boards ever since with the cut end up.

Adrian Vale could not read it. He could see there was one number on it and he could not have said what any part of it was a count of, and he did not try, and nobody in that street asked him to. What he had instead was the edge of a tally-board. The man of about thirty-eight took the flat of a knife with a blunt edge off his knee between ten and twelve times in the course of the morning and worked it along the wood a notch at a time. Each time he did it he put the board back inside the front of his coat, and the notches that were being cut were further along the edge of that board than the notches that were already on it.

"**There is a set on that board for what I send and there is a set for what I have got, and I do not say out loud what the second one is, and you have not asked me what the second one is, and that is the arrangement and it has been the arrangement for four times a year for longer than you have been standing on my boards.**"

---

That is all the evidence he had. He counted, because there was nothing else to do with his hands between the top of the steps and the bottom. He counted the notches going on the outside edge of that board, and the figure the man said out loud was a different figure from the one going on the edge of it, and there is nothing in four hundred miles he could put beside either of them to say which of the two is right or whether the difference between them is a mistake.

"**You are going to break the boards running it back empty.**"

A man at a trestle at the near end of that wharf said the thing that is said at the bottom of that street on a Sunday, and he said it to the stack and not to anybody.

"There is no use talking to a man on a barrow."

"**Nobody has ever talked to a man on a barrow in this flood and I have not either, and that is not a complaint, and go and do your own morning.**"

---

About four people came the length of those boards that morning. One of them stopped to look at the man on the barrow and one of them did not. The one who stopped looked at the board lying on the block and not at the man, and neither of them said anything to the other about either.

Nobody took up the thing Adrian Vale said about the boards. The man of about thirty-eight at the salt wharf did not answer it and did not change what he was doing, and the man on the barrow went back up the steps with the sack and came down again, and the boards took it. The wind went on coming off the water and there was nothing on that wharf for it to be stopped by, and the light lay along the boards and did not get onto anything. About four hundred and forty went on being the only figure anybody in this city had said out loud that day, and it was said on a wharf at the bottom of this city to nobody at all, and it is not the figure that came up the road in the room over a market, and nobody in this city has said out loud that it is not.
