# Chapter 0491 — A Board Hung Off The Flags

The man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall had his board up on its string at about the eighth hour on the hundred and seventy-sixth day of the Bare Month, a Tuesday, with the face of it out into the row and the stone sitting on the foot of the string. The flags under that stone were wet and the flags two feet away from them were not. Water comes off the low ground at the top of that row and finds its own level along the flags to the trough against the wall, and the trough is filled twice every morning. Over the trough the store's board hung on its two nails, and what it carried was the account of the two hundred and twenty-fourth day of this flood. A hand's width clear of that account, on the same stone, there was a figure of this city's own in chalk, and neither the account nor the figure had been wiped at any point since either of them went up. The wall over the top of that board was not washed, and what washing went on below it stopped where the board stopped.

---

Adrian Vale had his right hand flat on the stone where it sat in the water. He took hold of the string above it and drew it up a hand's width. The stone came up off the wet flags and he set it down on dry ones, and the whole of that end of the string went slack and the board on the end of it came down about a hand's width. He went along the row to the trestle under the awning and got a length of split wood off the top of it. He carried it back and put it on the flags and put the near corner of the board up on it, and he did it with two hands and not with a word. Nobody at that end of the market asked him what he was doing. Nobody stopped and looked at him, and there was nobody standing about waiting to see whether he would be any use to anybody that morning.

The man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall came out from behind his own trestle and looked at the foot of his board and at the piece of wood under the corner of it. He put his hand up and got hold of the lower rail of it and pulled. It did not come down, and he let go of it and stood looking at it.

"**It is higher than I set it and I have not asked anybody to set it higher, and I am not going to go down that row and find out who did. I will reach for it the way I have reached for everything on this row for the whole of this flood, and if the row cannot get under it then the row can go round the other end.**"

A woman at the stall two along from him said the thing that is said to the row and not to him.

"It is my awning pole at the other end of it."

"**Then it is your awning pole at the other end of it and it is my board at this end of it, and the two of them are a hand's width apart and neither of us put the wood under. That is all I am going to say about it at this end of the row.**"

Nobody thanked him for saying that and nobody argued with him about it. About four people at that end of that market had worked out what a hand's width does to a row, and not one of them said so. A man at the end of the row put his hand up under the board and took the weight of it for a step and let it down.

---

The row went under it. He came out to the front of his stall at the middle of that morning and had to take the whole weight of his board on the flat of his left hand to get his own hand to the foot of it. He did that every time he went to the foot of it. By the middle of the day his sleeve on that arm had a wet band on it, where the water off the trough had come down the rail and run along the wood. The flags underneath stayed wet. The flags two feet away from them stayed dry, and the stone sat on dry ones for the rest of that day.

His own piece of chalk was in his own inside breast pocket the whole of it and did not come out. A woman at the stall two along from him put her hand on the top of her own board and looked at the string going up past her awning pole.

"**Somebody has put a piece of wood under his own board and he is not going to say so and neither am I, and I am going to have a hand up in the air at this end of my own stall every time I want the bottom of my own, and I would rather it than the other way round.**"

Then she wrung her rag out onto the flags and hung it over the edge of her own board and did not say anything else.

---

The stone was still on dry flags at the end of that day and the string was still up a hand's width and the piece of split wood was still under the near corner of that board. Nobody in that market knew whose hands had put it there or had taken them away again. What the flags knew about it and nobody said out loud was that the wet had stopped coming under that stone and had started coming under the wood instead, and the wood was the one thing on that row that was going to be looked at first and asked about last.
