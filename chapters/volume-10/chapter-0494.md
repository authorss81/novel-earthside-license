# Chapter 0494 — A Stack Comes Down On The Boards

Weather came up off the water at about the seventh hour on the hundred and seventy-ninth day of the Bare Month, a Friday, and got into the stack at the far end of the boards of that salt wharf. The light on that wharf was on the water and not on a wall. The stack was higher than a man and had a tarpaulin over the top of it that had been lashed there since the spring and had not been looked at since. A shed stands on those boards with a door in it. That door went back on the other side of its opening on the hundred and seventy-third day of the Bare Month and would shut only if a man pulled it, and a man had been walking round the outside of that shed every morning since to get at the boards he wanted. Adrian Vale had both hands on the near edge of the tarpaulin where it had come off its lashing on the windward side, and it was standing up off the stack about a hand's width.

---

He got the edge of it down onto the boards along the run of the stack and got his weight on it. The wind took the slack out of it and pulled, and the boards under the stack moved about the width of two fingers in a way that made a noise on that wharf. He got a batten off the pile at the foot of the stack and put the end of it under the bottom course at the windward end. He put his shoulder against the end of it and pushed while the wind pushed. The stack came down along its own length and not across it, and the whole of it went over onto the boards at the end of that wharf in one long movement rather than in pieces.

Nobody on those boards asked Adrian Vale anything, and nobody on those boards stopped to look at him either. There was nobody standing about on that wharf at that hour waiting on him to be any use to them, and the man of about thirty-eight at that wharf had a tally-board against his leg and a knife with a blunt edge lying on the boards beside the chute. He walked to the head of the stack and looked down the length of what had come down, and walked back again without saying a word for about as long as it takes a man to count nine.

---

He said it in his own mouth, to nobody in particular, and he said it once.

"**That went the way a stack goes, along what it is made of, and there was a batten under the end of it and the batten is still under the end of it.**"

A man at the foot of the stack said the thing that is said.

"I watched it lean before you got to it."

"**I have watched about nine of them come down on this wharf and I have not watched one of them decide anything about where it was going, and the wind was on it and the batten was under it, and I am not going to thank a man for a batten and I am not going to say a word about it to the man who put it there.**"

Nobody improved on that and nobody argued with him about it. About four people on those boards had worked out where a stack that comes down along itself will end up, and not one of them said so.

"It is on the end boards."

"**It is on the end boards and the end boards are the ones I have not been able to get at since that door went on the wrong side, and it has not moved a foot and neither have I, and I have been going round that building every morning since instead of round a corner I do not need to go round.**"

---

They cleared it before the middle of the day. Adrian Vale and two men and the man of about thirty-eight himself got the boards off the pile one at a time and stacked them along the boards at the end of the wharf. The man of about thirty-eight walked down the run of them with his hand on the top of each one as it came off, and he did not go round the shed once. A strip of clear board as long as a cart was leaning against the other end of that stack, and a barrow stood at the end of the wharf with nothing at all in it. The shed door stood open on the wrong side of its opening all of that morning with the wind coming through it. At about the fourth hour the man of about thirty-eight went and pulled that door to and it shut. He let go of it, and it stood there shut for about as long as it takes a man to count nine, and then the wind came through the gap at the top of it and it stood open again for the rest of that day.

Nobody on those boards has said the name of the man who put a batten under the foot of that stack. The tally-board still has its figure on it, and the figure is not written down anywhere in this flood, and the barrow at the end of that wharf is still standing there with nothing in it at all.

---

A man walked down the front of that shed the next morning and the morning after that, and those are the first mornings of this flood on which anybody has gone at that stack from the front of the building instead of round the side of it. The boards at the end of that wharf are on the side of the building that a man in this city can see from the step at the bottom of it. Nobody on those boards has ever said whose hands were on the batten.
