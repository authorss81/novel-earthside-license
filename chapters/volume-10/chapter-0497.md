# Chapter 0497 — The Second Morning On The Flats

The weather went off in the night and the hundred and eighty-second day of the Bare Month came up grey over the eleven miles of flats, a Monday, and the second morning of a count on foot across them began about the eighth hour at the near end of the stack of boards. There is no standing water in the channel on that morning and the silt in the bottom of it has taken the night on top of itself and is soft about a foot down. The whole of that line of doorways on the far side of it can be walked, and there is nobody on those flats that morning but the people who live on them and the man with the slate.

---

A man sat down on a door-sill at the end of the first run with his elbows on his knees and did not get up again until the light had come right across the sill he was sitting on. A woman wrung a coat out over a bucket at the same end of the line and hung it on a gate. The man of about thirty-one who carries things for a living came along the line with the register-keeper's working slate face up on a board across two trestles. The two figures were on one face of it where anybody standing at that board could have seen them, and he put his hand flat over the face of it when he came to the board and kept it there while he counted. He moved it for each one and put it back.

He said the thing that is said, in his own mouth, to the ground that was not a room, about the middle of that line.

"**There is a place about two thirds of the way along this line where a man walking it on the road side says there is a door. The same man walking the same place on the channel side says there is a wall.**"

A woman at a door said the thing that is said to him and not to anybody else.

"You have had to go round and round that line."

"**He is right both times and the two of them go into the book he is counting into as the same thing, because he has walked it one way and not the other. That is the whole of the difference between what is on that slate this morning and what was on it yesterday.**"

Nobody thanked him for it and nobody argued with him about it, and about four people on that line had worked out what a man does when he is the one holding the slate. Not one of them said so. The light came off the low ground behind that line of doorways at about the tenth hour and lay along the fronts of them, and the silt in the channel dried to about the depth of a finger along the lip on the far side of it.

"I am not going to be the man who goes back and looks for the place again."

"**You are not, and there is no second slate and there is not going to be a second slate, and nobody has asked me for one, and a barrow goes on the road side and the count is in the road side whether I like it or not.**"

---

The man of about thirty-one took his hand off the face of the slate at the end of the line and stood looking down at the two figures on the one face of it. Neither of them was rubbed out and neither of them was gone over, and the two of them were not the same. He said one more thing and he said it to the board.

"**It is not the weather, because the weather yesterday and the weather this morning were the same coming off the low ground. It is not anybody knocking at anything, because nobody has knocked at a door on these flats since the water came and I would have heard it from the road.**"

A man on the far end of that line said the thing that is said to nobody in particular.

"That is a poor thing to bring down a road."

"**That is the whole of what there is to bring down a road, and what is on that slate is what a man walking a line found, and I have brought four things up it in a year and not opened one of them, and this one I am bringing back open.**"

---

The man who had sat down on the door-sill got up off it and went off along the line the other way, slowly, with his hands behind him. He stopped at one place on it and stood there and then went on. Nobody followed him and nobody said anything to him about it. The barrow went up the far bank with the slate face up on the board across the two trestles on it and the fold of sacking over that, and the fold of sacking was put over it and not taken off again after that.

---

There is a place about two thirds of the way along that line where a man can stand on one side of a thing and call it a door and stand on the other side and call it a wall. Both of those are on this city's side of the channel and one of them is on the slate, and which of the two was walked first is written down nowhere in this flood and never has been.
