# Chapter 0487 — The Offer, Withdrawn

The man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall was at his board at about the ninth hour on the hundred and seventy-second day of the Bare Month, a Friday, with the string up on its post and a stone on the foot of it, two stalls along from the store. At the end of that row stood the man who carries things for a living, a satchel with a flap under his arm and his hand flat on it, and he and the man at the stall had not said one word to one another since the Wednesday. Over the trough at that end of the market the store's board was on its two nails with the old account still standing on it, the one from the two hundred and twenty-fourth day of this flood, and there was no second set of figures anywhere on that board, and the wall over the top of it had not been washed. The row was doing what it does. The man of about thirty-four took his hands off the string and held them out in front of him and turned them over once, and looked at the two of them, and put them at his sides again.

---

He said the thing that is said, and he said it to the man of about thirty-one and not to the row.

"**I am not going to be the man who goes out and makes that other number, and I have had since that Wednesday to say so and I am saying it now. The reason I am not is that I cannot knock, and I have not knocked at a door since I was a boy in this market.**"

A man at the end of that row said the thing that is said.

"That is going to sound well."

"**It is going to sound well and it is not going to be well, and I would rather it sounded well than have it not sound well at all, because a thing that sounds well is a thing people carry.**"

He said the rest of it to his own board and not to the man at the end of the row.

"**I have got a board of my own on a string and I have not been on a door since I was a boy in this market, and if I went out onto those flats I should have stood in a gateway and counted, and I should have come back with the same figure the first man has, and I should have had to say so in front of about nine people.**"

Nobody thanked him for saying it and nobody argued with him about it. The man of about thirty-one took his hand off the flap of the satchel and put both hands behind his back, and he said it with his back to the row, and he did not turn round while he said it.

"Then that is the end of it, and I have heard it, and I am not going to say you were right and I am not going to say you were wrong. The only thing I am going to say is that the two counts on that slate were made by two men and neither of them was you, and that is the only thing I know about it that is worth knowing."

Nobody improved on either of those. The man at the end of that row put his hand flat on the edge of his own board and left it there for the length of what the other two of them said and did not take it off, and the man who carries things for a living did not look round at him while he did it. The man of about thirty-four put the flat of his hand on his own board where it hung on the string, and turned the board a hand's width so that the face of it went along the row and not out into it, and then turned it back so that the face of it was out again. The stone stayed on the foot of the string the whole time. He went out to the front of his board about the middle of that and pulled the string up a hand's width and let it go, and it came back down and hit the stone. He did it again with the same result, and the third time he caught it before it hit and stood with his hand on the string, and his hand did not let go of it for a while. A man at the end of the row said the thing that is said.

"You could have gone and not knocked."

"**I could have gone and not knocked, and I would have come back with a figure, and I would not have known what I had done. There is nobody in four hundred miles who could have told me afterwards, and that is the part of it I cannot put my hand to, and I am not going to stand here being admired for a thing I have not done.**"

The woman at the stall two along from him turned to the man beside her at about the middle of that and did not say what she had been going to say. She put her hand over the edge of her own board and left it there, and she has not said it to anybody. The man of about thirty-four picked his stone up off the foot of the string, turned it once in his hand, and put it back down where it had been, and went back behind his own board and did not come out to the front of it again that day.

---

He put the flat of his hand on his own board where it hung on the string and left it there until the hand had gone cold on it, and that board was the whole of the reason and he did not say that it was. Not one person on that row was going to be the one to say it to him.
