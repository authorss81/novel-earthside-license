# Chapter 0479 — A Stone Under The Far End Of The Trough

Adrian Vale had both hands under the far end of the trough at about the seventh hour on the hundred and sixty-fourth day of the Bare Month, a Thursday, in that market. He was the only person at that end of the market who had noticed that the trough had been running across the flags without a stop, and he was the only one who had ever looked at where the flags under the far end of it were going. He put his shoulder into it and lifted, and the trough came up about a hand's width and stayed up while he got a flat stone out of the wall of that store with his foot and worked it under the end of it. He let the trough down onto the stone and the water in it went level and stopped and stayed where it was instead of walking off the end and across the flags.

---

Nobody asked him what he was doing. Nobody in that market decided not to ask him, and nobody stood about waiting to see whether he would be any use to anybody, and the man of about twenty-seven who washes that store's board came out of the door with the rag over his shoulder, looked at the stone under the end of the trough, went back inside, and shut the door behind him without a word. The bucket was standing in the trough and the rag was on the stone lip, and the board on the wall above them still carried the figures of the two hundred and twenty-fourth day of this flood and nothing else at all, and the washing of the wall above that board stopped where the board stopped and has stopped there every morning of this flood.

The man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall came out with his own board under his arm and the stone from the foot of his string in his other hand and stood for a while looking at the flags where the water had been going. Then he put the string back up on the post, put the board up on the string with its face out, and put the stone on the foot of the string on a piece of flags that were dry.

---

He said three sentences about it and nobody thanked him for them and nobody argued with them either.

"**I have had that string on that post for nine years and I have never once had to move the stone off it, and a board on a string wants dry flags under the foot of it as much as it wants anything else, and I am not saying a word to anybody about the trough and I would like everybody to go home knowing that nobody in this market said a word about the trough.**"

A woman at a stall two along said the thing that is said.

"You could have told somebody."

"**I could have told somebody and there is no way in this city of telling anybody anything in advance of anything, and I have been in this market nine years and the way you tell somebody is to wait until you already know and then say it, and that is not the same and everybody at this end of this market knows that it is not the same.**"

Nobody at that end of that market improved on that. The woman at the stall two along went back to her own board and cleared the water off it with the flat of her hand, and about four people at that end of that market had worked out since that a man had put a stone under a trough and that nobody was going to be told anything about it by anybody, and not one of those four people was going to say so to anybody else.

---

The water in that trough was still standing in it at about the eleventh hour with the level of it a finger's width below the stone lip, and it was going nowhere at all, and a man could put a hand flat into it at any hour of that day and it would be the same cold all the way to the bottom of it.
