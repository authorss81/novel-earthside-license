# Chapter 0481 — The Bucket Goes Up The Row Twice

The man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall went up the row with the store's bucket in his right hand at about the sixth hour on the hundred and sixty-sixth day of the Bare Month, a Saturday. He came back down it with the water going over the rim at every second step. He put the bucket into the trough against the wall under the store's board, and the water came up over the stone lip and stood there. He went up the row again with an empty bucket and came back down it with a full one and put that in beside the first, and the water came up over the stone lip a second time and stood there both times. A woman at a stall two along from his own watched him make both trips from the end of her own board and said nothing at all about either of them.

---

The water in that trough was what that board was washed with. The man who washed it had filled that trough himself every morning of this flood, and he had stopped filling it himself. The woman at the stall two along from him had been carrying it up that row in that bucket at the sixth hour and putting it in the trough, and nobody had asked her to, and nobody had thanked her, and she had not said one word about it to anybody. She came out from behind her own board at the sixth hour on that Saturday with her own pail in her hand the way she came out every morning. She got as far as the edge of her own board with it, and she looked at the trough, and she stopped. She stood there with the pail in her hand for about as long as it takes a man to decide whether to put a thing down. Then she went back two steps and put the pail under her own board with its mouth up and did not say one word about it, and she has not carried water up that row since.

A woman at the stall two along said the thing that is said.

"You have carried it twice."

"**I have carried it twice because it was empty, and I am not going to say anything else about it at this end of the row. I would like it said once and only once that a bucket is a thing anybody can pick up and a trough is a thing anybody can fill, and neither of the two of them is a favour.**"

A woman at the stall two along said the thing that is said.

"It is a favour."

"**Then it is a favour, and I would rather nobody said a word about it at all, and in particular I would rather the man who washes that board did not find out from me.**"

Nobody thanked him for carrying it and nobody argued with him about carrying it. A man at the end of the row said the thing that is said.

"That is the store's bucket."

"**It is the store's bucket and it is the store's trough. The water in it came off the low ground and into a pipe that the store has never once paid anybody to keep, and I have not touched the pipe and I am not going to touch the pipe. I would like this morning to be the end of the subject.**"

---

Nobody improved on that. The man of about thirty-four took his hand off the rim of the bucket and put it flat on the stone lip of the trough to see whether the water had come over. It had not. He stood with the flat of his hand on that stone for a moment and then he took it off again and went back to his own stall. He picked his own board up off the trestle under the awning with two hands and carried it the length of his own stall. He hung it up on the string with the face of it out into the row, and dropped the stone onto the flags at the bottom end of the string, and the flags there were dry. He did not say one word about any of that either. A man at the far end of the row turned a board face down on his own board. The woman at the stall two along put the flat of her hand on the rim of her pail where it sat under her own board and left it there, and that was the whole of what that end of the market did about a bucket.

---

The pail was under the stall board at the stall two along from his own with its mouth up for the rest of that day and for the day after that, and it has not been carried up that row since. The man who keeps the bucket has carried it up that row on every morning since, and he has not asked the woman who put the pail down to carry it again, and the trough against that wall has not been dry once in the whole of this flood.
