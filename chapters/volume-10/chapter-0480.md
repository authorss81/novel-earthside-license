# Chapter 0480 — The Second Morning On The Flats

The man of about thirty-one who carries things for a living had the register-keeper's own working slate in his left hand at about the sixth hour on the hundred and sixty-fifth day of the Bare Month, a Friday, and he had taken it off that bench without asking while the woman of about twenty-nine who keeps a public register was wiping the table at the far end of the room over a market, and she had seen him take it and had not said a word. The register was on its shelf behind that bench and was not opened, and the second slate was on the shelf behind it with a date on it and nothing under the date, and it was not picked up and it was not turned over and it was not written on, and no hand in that room but hers went near that shelf. He had a satchel on his other arm with the flap down and there was nothing in it at all.

---

He came to the first gate on those flats at about the sixth hour and went through it and went east, and the wind was off the low ground again and there was nothing on eleven miles of flats for it to come up against, and the ground gave under his weight in one strip and not in the one beside it, and he had no way of telling which was which until he was standing on it. A channel runs across those flats to take the water out at a gate and it has been dry since the hundred and ninety-sixth day of this flood, and the silt in the bottom of it is the same from the gate to the far end, and he crossed it twice on the boards and stepped over it the rest of the way. He put one stroke on the slate for every place there was a door at, the same mark he would have put there if nobody had ever heard of knocking, and then he knocked.

Nobody was watching him go out. Nobody followed him along those flats and nobody was standing at the end of them to be shown what was on the slate, and there is no instrument in four hundred miles that could have sent anybody out there to be shown anything. He did not wait at any of them for longer than a man can stand on a step and decide to go, and about the ninth hour the light came flat over the low ground and showed him the whole width of the channel at once.

---

He said it to the wind at about the ninth hour, when there was no house within a quarter of a mile of him on either side.

"**A man who knocks gets a different figure from a man who does not, and the difference is not in the ground and the difference is not in the doors, and the difference is in about ten places where I stood in a gateway and knocked and nobody came, and I cannot tell you whether anybody was in any of them and neither can anybody else in this city.**"

He said the other of it a long way further on, where the ground came up under his boots and the wind had dropped to nothing.

"**Both of these are a count of places. Neither of them is a count of people and there is nothing in four hundred miles that will turn one into the other, and I have got two different numbers about the same ground made by two careful men who were both trying, and no way whatever of knowing which of the two is the right one, and I am not going to pick one.**"

---

He came back at about the fourth hour with the cold out of the north in his face and put the slate on that bench with the marks on it, and the first set of marks was still on it, and he had not rubbed any of them out, and he had made the second set down the other side of the same face so that the two of them could be looked at together by anybody who cared to look. About four hundred and forty on the first, and about four hundred and thirty on the second, and he said that the difference was in the places where he had knocked and nobody had come, and that he could not tell whether anybody had been in any of them.

Nobody in that room asked him a single question about either figure, and nobody thanked him for walking out of that city and back into it. The register was on its shelf behind that bench with its spine to the wall and was not opened, and the second column of it held nothing, and the third column of it was empty on purpose and was going to stay empty on purpose, and the two lines in that book were not looked at.

---

There is a form in four hundred miles on which a clerk could write down that a man came in with a slate and that it did not agree with a slate, and there is nobody in this city who has ever seen one of those forms filled in and there is nobody in this city who would know what to do with one. The slate went back onto the bench it came off with both sets of marks on it and neither of them rubbed out, and the bench in that room had a thing on it that somebody had made a mistake about and nobody had thrown away.
