# Chapter 0780 — A Barrow Handle On Eleven Miles Of Flats

Adrian Vale put his right hand on the near handle of that barrow and his left on the far one, and lifted, and started walking. The wheels came off the stone and onto the flats in about the first ten yards, and after that he was pushing it up the first of eleven miles of them.

It was the fourth hour on the four hundred and sixty-fifth day of the Bare Month, a Thursday. The wind came up those flats behind him from about the fifth hour, and it came up them the whole day. The barrow was not his. It had a week's work in it, and eleven miles is a long way to push a barrow for a man who is not going to be asked about it.

He wanted that barrow stood in the corner of the room over that market before the seventh hour. That corner was a foot off a wall and out of the weather, and a man came into that room at the fourth hour and got his coat off in it every morning of the flood. Standing in that yard with his hand on a handle, he had worked out that a man should not have to be standing in a yard with a foot of water coming across it at the seventh hour to find out where his own barrow had gone.

---

He got a mile and a half up those flats before he understood that he was doing the wrong thing with his own body.

A barrow goes up a rise on its handles, and he was lifting it on the axle instead. The load shifted on the turn of it, and he had to stop and put his shoulder against the near wheel and get it straight again. He stopped three times in that first mile, and each time he stood afterwards for a while with his hands still on the two handles and his back not straight. The wind was coming up the flats behind him and getting into the cloth of his coat.

At about the sixth hour he was still on the second of those eleven miles, with a barrow that belonged to another man and his hands on it, and no more idea of what he was going to do with it than he had had at the bottom of them.

He had carried it about a mile and a half off the ground it belonged to.

---

At the seventh hour the man of about thirty-eight came through the gate of that yard and stopped on the flags just inside it.

There was the trough, and there was the stack of boards, and there was the stone step with the rag lying on it, and there was the low place in the stone at the side of that yard with water standing in it. The bucket lying in that water is the only bucket in this city, and it lay in it. There was no barrow.

He stood in the middle of that yard and looked at the whole length of it. Then he went out of the gate and up the near end of those flats on foot, the way a man goes to look for a thing he has lost. He went a mile and a half up them and found a barrow standing on the second rise with a stranger's hands coming off it.

They stood there together for the length of about a minute, and neither of the two men said one word to the other. Then the man of about thirty-eight took hold of the far handle and walked it down the rest of those flats himself, one wheel at a time along them. The other one walked beside it for the first half mile, and then turned round and went back down.

---

That yard was emptied an hour later than it was emptied on a morning of the flood.

The barrow came back down those flats at about the eighth hour and was not emptied until the ninth, and the man of about thirty-eight was still working at the low place at the ninth hour on a job he had finished by the seventh hour every morning of a flood.

The weather came over those flats at about the seventh hour and went into that yard and into the low place in the stone in one long push, because the thing that usually stood between the low place and the rest of that yard was a mile and a half up the second rise with a stranger's hands on it. The low place held more at the tenth hour than it had held at the fourth hour of that morning, and the bucket that lies in it lay in that water for a longer time than it had lain in it on any morning of this flood.

Nobody in that yard said one word to anybody about where that barrow had spent the morning. Nobody was thanked, and nobody was told that anything a stranger had done there was the reason for anything.

---

At about the seventh hour a barrow handle was in a man's hand out on the second of eleven miles of flats, and the wind was coming up the whole length of them from behind him. A yard was standing empty at the near end of them, with a trough in it and a step and a bucket.

At about the tenth hour he was on the fourth of those miles, and there was more of that walk in front of him than there was behind him, and the wind was still coming up the whole length of them into the back of his coat. His right hand had been on that handle since the fourth hour and his left hand had been on the other one, and both of them would be on them again at the fourth hour on Friday morning, with the whole of eleven miles of flat still in front of them.
