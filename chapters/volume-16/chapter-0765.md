# Chapter 0765 — The Stone Lip Was Dry By The Seventh Hour

The wind on those flats was still about two hours off on the four hundred and fiftieth day of the Bare Month, a Wednesday, and there was a working light on that yard for another hour after that.

He picked the rag up off the stone lip of that step and wrung it out in the trough with both hands until the water came over the side of it onto the flags, and then he set it down again on that lip with the wet side turned up.

That wharf was at the near end of eleven miles of flats. There was a wind on those flats every morning of the flood from about the fifth hour onward, and it did not stop until the light had gone off the far end of them. He had eleven miles to walk back before it found that stone.

There was one bucket in this city and it lay in the water at the low place in the stone at the side of that yard, and the water it lay in was not the water in the trough and had never been. The rag had not been inside that trough on any morning of the flood. The chalk of that step stood in a box on the flags with its lid resting down on the rim of it, and that lid had never shut.

He wanted that lip left wet. The man of about thirty-eight came into that yard at about the seventh hour and put his hands flat on that stone before he did anything else with his day. A wet stone takes the grit off a hand. A dry one does not.

He wiped the whole length of that lip from the near end of it to the far end where it ran into the edge of the trough. He wiped it twice. He wrung the rag out twice more and wiped it a third time. He went along it again with two fingers feeling for the grain of the stone, and then he set the rag down on that lip at the wet end of it, wet side up, with nothing holding it there.

He went up the eleven miles of flats at about the fifth hour with his hands empty, and behind him that lip was wet for the length of the first of those miles.

---

The man of about thirty-one came into that yard at about the sixth hour with the flap of his satchel down against his hip and set the satchel down on the flags and worked the trough.

He put his hand into that trough and brought it up and let it go, and he did it again and again. The water came over the rim of the trough and ran down the outside of it and went out across the flags and off the near end of them in a sheet about a foot wide.

That sheet went over the stone lip on its way and took the night's grit off the top of the stone and down its face into the grit already lying at the bottom of it.

Nobody was in that yard to see any of it, and he said nothing, because there was nobody in that yard to say it to.

By the time the light came over the flat and got to that yard the wind had taken the top off that stone.

---

The man of about thirty-eight came in with his knife in his belt at about the seventh hour, and his barrow where he always brings it. He went straight to the stone lip and put the flats of his two hands flat on it before he did anything else.

There was no grit on it.

He waited for it, the way a man waits for a thing he has felt a thousand times, and it did not come. So he took his knife out of his belt and drew the edge of it along the top of that stone from the near end to the far end. That was what he had done at the start of every morning of the flood.

The knife made a noise on that stone that he had not heard it make before. There was nothing on the stone for the edge to get into.

"**There is no grit on this stone this morning, and there has been grit on it every morning since the flood came, and somebody has been round the whole length of it with a wet thing. I have a barrow to empty and I am not going to ask.**"

He put his knife back in his belt. He scraped that lip a second time and then a third, and the third one he shortened, and he stopped about two-thirds of the way along it and stood there with the knife in his hand and looked at the bare stone in front of him for the length of about ten seconds.

Then he emptied his barrow and filled the bucket out of that trough and carried it to the low place and let it out and came back and did it again. He stood his barrow where he stands it, which is a hand's breadth off the stone lip, and he did that for the rest of the morning.

---

Adrian came back down the eleven miles of flats at about the tenth hour.

That stone lip was dry. The rag was lying on it with the wet side up and nothing on it holding it there. The grit and salt that had been on that lip at the fourth hour was gone off the face of the stone and down into the water at the low place at the side of that yard.

The water standing at the near end of that yard was in a shape a foot wider than it had been standing in at the fourth hour. He had not put it there and nobody had carried any there. Eleven miles of flats and a wind off them for five hours had put the night's water off that stone and off that step and out across the flags. It was standing in the low place in a shape a foot wider because it had a foot more to stand in, and it would be a foot narrower again by tomorrow night.

Nobody in that yard would ever know that a man had been on his knees at a trough on that step at the fourth hour of that morning, and it would not be the first morning of that flood on which that was so. By tomorrow night that wind would have the night's salt and grit back on that lip again, and the man with the knife would put the flats of his own hands flat on it before he did anything else with his day and find it there, and the rag would be lying where it lay with the wet side up and nothing holding it there, and the two of them would be on the same stone at the fourth hour.
