# Chapter 0765 — The Stone Lip Was Dry By The Seventh Hour

Both of Adrian Vale's hands were on that rag where it lay on the stone lip of that step and he picked it up and wrung it out in that trough with both of them until the water came over the side of it onto the flags, and then he set the rag back down on that lip with the wet side of it turned up.

It was about the fourth hour on the four hundred and fiftieth day of the Bare Month, a Wednesday, at that salt wharf at the near end of eleven miles of flats, and there is a wind on those flats every morning of this flood from about the fifth hour onward that does not stop until the light has gone off the far end of them.

There is one bucket in this city and it lies in the water at the low place in that stone at the side of that yard, and the water it lies in is not the water in the trough and never has been. That rag is on the stone lip of the step and it has not been inside that trough on any morning of this flood. The chalk of that step stands in a box on the flags with the lid of that box resting down on the rim of it, and that lid has never shut.

He wanted that lip left wet, because the man of about thirty-eight at that wharf comes into the yard at about the seventh hour and puts his own hands on the stone before he does anything else with his day, and a wet stone takes the grit of a hand off it and a dry one does not.

He wiped the whole length of that lip at the fourth hour from the near end of it to the far end where it ran into the edge of the trough, and he wiped it twice, and he wrung that rag out twice more and wiped it a third time, and he set the rag down on that lip at the wet end of it with the wet side up and nothing on it holding it there.

---

He went up the eleven miles of flats at about the fifth hour with his own two hands empty and that lip was wet behind him for about the length of the first of those miles.

At about the sixth hour the man of about thirty-one came into that yard with the flap of his own satchel down against his own hip and filled his own bucket out of that trough and carried it to the low place and let it out and came back and filled it again, and the water that came over the rim of the trough ran down the outside of it and went out across the flags and off the near end of them, and he said nothing to anybody because there was nobody in the yard to say anything to.

At about the seventh hour that stone lip was dry.

The man of about thirty-eight came into that yard with his own barrow and his own knife in his belt and put the flats of his own two hands flat on the stone before he did anything else, and the grit of his own hands came off onto it and stayed on it.

"**There is no grit on that stone this morning and there has been grit on the stone every morning since this flood came, and somebody has been round the whole of it with a wet thing in his own two hands, and I have got a barrow to empty and I am not going to ask.**"

Nobody in the yard was asked and nobody answered him. He emptied his own barrow and filled his own bucket out of that trough and carried it to the low place and let it out and came back and did it again, and Adrian Vale was eleven miles away at that hour and was told none of it and said none of it.

---

He came back down the eleven miles of flats at about the tenth hour and that stone lip was dry and that rag was lying on it with the wet side up, and the grit and salt that had been on that lip at the fourth hour was gone off it and into the water at the low place in the stone at the side of that yard, and the water standing there at the near end of the yard was in a shape a foot wider than it had been standing in at the fourth hour, and the rag was lying on that stone lip with the wet side up and no more water standing on the stone than any other morning of this flood leaves there.
