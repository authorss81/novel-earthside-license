# Chapter 0771 — Two Handles Against The Wall

Adrian Vale got both hands under the two handles of that barrow and lifted it off the flags. He walked it the length of that yard, and there was water in the bottom of it, and some of that water came out on the flags behind him in two lines.

It was the fourth hour on the four hundred and fifty-sixth day of the Bare Month, a Tuesday, and the weather was still two hours off those flats. That yard was at the near end of eleven miles of them. The wind came up the whole length every morning of the flood from about the fifth hour onward, and there was nothing at that hour to stop it.

He stood the barrow up against the wall on the near side of the yard, the long way on, the handles up. A man stands a thing he is not going to move again before he goes away that way. The wood of it was wet through in three places.

He wiped his hands down the sides of his coat and stood looking at it. He wanted the inside of it dry at the seventh hour, when a man comes into a yard and puts his hands on a barrow before he does anything else with his day.

---

The weather came over the far end of those flats at about the sixth hour, and it was over that yard by the seventh.

It came across those flags flat and hard, the way it comes in off eleven miles of water with nothing to slow it. The wall on the near side of that yard had no gutter on the inside of it and no lip on the top of it, and everything the weather brought off that wall went straight down it into whatever was standing against it.

He was not in that yard to see any of that happen. He was eleven miles off with his back to the weather.

---

The man of about thirty-eight came in out of it with his knife in his belt at about the seventh hour.

The first thing his hands went to was the barrow the stranger had stood against that wall at the fourth hour. He put them flat on the bottom of it, and there was water in it up over his knuckles.

He stood there with his hands where they were and looked at the water. Then he turned the barrow over on its side at the near end of those flags and let the whole of it out, and the water went out across the flags and into the low place in the stone at the side of that yard, where the bucket lies.

The bucket is the only one in this city. It lies in that water, and it has lain in that water through the whole of this flood, and the water it lies in is not the water in the trough and never has been.

He righted the barrow and lifted it and set it down where he puts it. He has stood that barrow a hand's breadth further off that wall every morning since the morning a stranger took two boards off the top of his stack. He did not say one word about the water, and he did not put it back where the stranger had put it.

He came in to that yard four times more that morning, and he set that barrow down in the same place four times, and four times he had to walk round the length of it to get from the trough to the low place with the bucket in his hands. He had never had to do that before, and he did it without once looking down at what was making him do it.

There was no figure cut into the inside of that barrow and no figure on the outside of it, and no chalk on either handle, and nothing anywhere in that yard that said which of the two men had stood it against that wall, or for how long.

---

At about the tenth hour the weather came off the far end of those flats and there was a mark on the flags beside that wall.

It was the shape of two wet handles about a foot apart, and it was the shape of the bottom of a barrow that had stood against that wall through six hours of weather. The flags either side of that wall were dry, and the marks were not, and they were going out at their edges the way a wet thing goes out.

Nobody came into that yard again after the man of about thirty-eight went down the flats. By the middle of the next day the sun would have the last of both marks out of that stone, and under that wall there would be a stretch of flags the same colour as the rest of them. In the morning the weather would come over that flat again, and the wall would have nothing standing against it, and the rain would find nothing to run down into, and the barrow would stand where it stands.
