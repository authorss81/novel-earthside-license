# Chapter 0772 — A Room Finding Out What It Is Asked

The man of about thirty-four had his two hands down on the wood at the near end of that bench, one on either side of the register, and the woman of about twenty-nine had the book open in front of him.

It was the fourth hour on the four hundred and fifty-seventh day of the Bare Month, a Wednesday, and nobody in that room had anything they had come up that stair to get. That had been true for the length of this flood. Nobody had ever said it out loud, and this morning it was the shape of the whole of the room between the fourth hour and the seventh.

At the fifth hour the woman of about twenty-nine gave the figure into the middle of that floor, in the ordinary course, and then she went on looking at her page.

"**There are about nine of us in this room at this hour. I have said that in here before I have said anything else on a great many mornings of this flood, and there is nobody in here I am saying it to.**"

Nobody improved on one word of it. The man of about thirty-nine was at the far end of that bench with his boots in the gap between the bench and the wall. He put the flat of his right hand on the face of his own board where it stood against the leg of it, and said nothing at all.

At about the fifth hour, and a little after it, the man of about thirty-four asked the man of about thirty-eight where he would be on the morrow.

---

The man of about thirty-eight came up that stair with his barrow and put his hands flat on the two handles of it before he took his coat off.

"**I will be at the wharf by the fifth hour and in here by the seventh, the way I am every morning, and I am asking you because I have never asked you.**"

He said it in the ordinary way. He did not turn round to ask it, and he did not lower his voice for it.

"**Then you have been asking everybody and I am the last one. I am not going to take that back, and I am not going to ask you anything else, and you may go down that stair when you have finished.**"

Nobody in that room said a word to either of them about any part of it. The woman of about twenty-nine turned a page over and did not look up off the wood. The man of about thirty-eight took his hands off the handles of that barrow, went back down that stair with it, and did not answer him at all.

It came out afterwards, to a person who was in that room, that the man of about thirty-four had been asking everybody all flood. Nobody had ever heard him do it, because he had never once done it where anybody could hear. He had been doing it out in the row on his way up, in the ordinary course of a man who keeps a stall two stalls along and has a word for everybody he passes.

That room had been hearing the answers to questions all flood, and had not known there were questions in it.

---

At about the sixth hour the man of about thirty-four got up off the near end of that bench.

He had held the same place at the same hour every morning of this flood. The man of about thirty-eight had been given the length of it to himself for a run of mornings without anybody saying so, and the register had been lying on the wood of it since the fourth hour with the woman of about twenty-nine's own hands on either side of the page.

He went the length of that floor and sat down at the far end of the bench, a forearm's length from the man of about thirty-nine, with his back to the wall along the far side and his board against the leg of the bench within reach of his right boot.

He sat there the better part of two hours and said nothing whatever, and the man of about thirty-nine said nothing whatever, and neither of them looked at the other at any hour of it. The whole of that room came past them at the near end of the bench for the rest of the morning, which it had not done for the length of a flood.

The man of about thirty-eight came back up at the seventh hour with his barrow. He stopped on the top step for the length of a minute, the way he had stopped on the top step once already in this flood, and then he came the rest of the way in and set the barrow down where he sets it and worked the morning out of it.

At about the eighth hour the woman of about fifty-two gave the figure into that room, the way she goes at it, and about nine of them were in it at that hour. Not one of them took a word of it up and put another one beside it, and the room went on being louder than it had been for the length of a flood without a single person in it appearing to notice that it was.

---

The bar was in its sockets on the far side of that door at the foot of that stair, the way it had been on the two mornings before that one. There was a date in chalk on the outside of it and nothing under the date.

At about the tenth hour the man of about thirty-eight went down that stair with the barrow, and stopped at the bottom of it with his hand flat on the bar.

He stood there the length of a breath with his palm on the wood of it, the way a man stands with his hand on a thing he is going to come back through in the morning. Then he took his hand off and went out into the row and left the bar where it was.

He had never once done that at the foot of that stair in the whole of this flood.
