# Chapter 0778 — He Named What It Had Cost Him

The man of about thirty-nine came up that stair at the fourth hour with his board under his arm. He stopped at the near end of that bench and stood the flat of his right hand on the face of it and left it there. At about the seventh hour he took it off and said it.

It was the four hundred and sixty-third day of the Bare Month, a Tuesday. On the outside face of that door at the foot of the stair there was a day in chalk and nothing under it. The light was in the middle of that floor, and the man of about thirty-eight was at the far end of that bench with his barrow's handles round the corner behind him.

"**I have put that figure into this room on more mornings of this flood than I can put in an order. Since Saturday morning I have not been able to tell you for one morning of this flood whether I stood in this room on it, and I am not going to be able to for the rest of my life, and there is no way of getting any of that back. I am not saying it to be thanked for, and I am not asking anybody anything about it.**"

---

Nobody up there thanked him for it. Nobody improved on one word of it. Nobody argued with it, and nobody put another one beside it. Nobody asked him why he had done what he had done on the Saturday, and nobody asked him whether he agreed with it.

The woman of about fifty-two was standing where she stands on the mornings she says nothing, which is the far end of that bench, her hands down the sides of her own dress. She said nothing whatever about any part of it. Earlier in that flood she had put a thing of her own into that room, and nothing had ever come of that one either.

The woman of about twenty-nine had the book open on the wood in front of her and did not look up off the page. She was not asked a word about any of it, and neither was the man of about thirty-four, who put his hands in his coat and left them there.

Then everybody in that room went on with the morning, which is what a room does.

---

The change in that room came at about the ninth hour, and it was not a word.

The man of about thirty-nine took his board from under his arm and laid it down flat on the wood of that bench at the far end of the room, face up, with the long edge along the edge of that bench. He put his right hand down flat in the middle of the face of it, and then he took his hand off and left it there and put both his hands in his coat.

He had carried that board under his arm through four markets for about nine years. He had laid it face up on that bench on the two mornings since the Saturday, and on both of those mornings he had stood at the near end of that bench with the flat of his hand on the face of it, and both of those mornings it had gone back under his arm before the morning was out.

This time he left it lying there for the rest of the morning. It lay on that bench with the rates cut into the face of it in three columns, and the bare place at the foot of the column between the other two standing up in the light that came over the roof at the ninth hour. People in that room walked past it and looked at it and did not stop.

At about the tenth hour he went along that bench to the far end of it, picked the board up, turned it over so that the face of it went against his side, and went down that stair with it under his arm, the way he went down that stair every morning of the flood.

It had not been left behind by anybody. It was his. In the morning he would come up that stair with it again, because he had carried it a long way, and it was the only thing he had that anybody in that room had ever been able to look at.

---

The room emptied the way it emptied on every morning of that flood. The man of about thirty-four went off the row first, and the man of about thirty-eight went down that stair with his barrow behind him, and the man of about twenty-seven came up out of the yard under that room on his way out and said that the water in that yard had gone down another inch.

By the tenth hour the light had just gone off that floor, and the bench stood with nothing on it at all where that board had been lying. The board was face down against a man's own side by then, and the shape of his own right hand was still there in the middle of the rates where the light of the ninth hour had stood on it.

That face had lain up in the light where he had put it for the length of an hour, and the woman of about twenty-nine sat at her own end of that bench with the book open in front of her the whole of that hour and never turned a page of it.
