# Chapter 0769 — A Foot Further Along, Out Of The Light

The man of about thirty-nine came up that stair at the fourth hour on the four hundred and fifty-fourth day of the Bare Month, a Sunday. He brought his board up the way he brings it up and put it where he puts it, and sat down at the far end of that bench with his boots in the gap between that bench and the wall along the far side.

Nothing was said to him. It was not that morning that this began and it was not that morning that it was noticed. He had been right about a page in about four seconds in volumes running and he was thanked by nobody for it.

About nine people went in and out up there over the space of six hours that morning, and not one of them said one word to him at any hour of it.

The register lay open on the wood of the bench at the near end of it from the fourth hour to the tenth hour, ruled in three columns.

Five figures stood down the middle of it one under another in the order they were made in, with a day set against each of the five. None of them was struck through. Nothing was entered after them. Nothing was ever taken out.

---

At the fifth hour the woman of about fifty-two stood with her hands at the sides of her dress and gave that figure into the middle of that floor the way she had always gone at it.

"**Nine of us, most mornings, every morning of this flood, and it has gone out of my mouth on more of them than any other thing I have ever said in this room.**"

Nobody in that room improved on one word of that. Not one of them looked along that bench at the far end of it while she was saying it, and nothing was said to the man of about thirty-nine about it or about anything else, and he said one word back to nobody about any part of it.

At about the seventh hour the man of about thirty-eight carried a length of board up out of the yard below. He stood it on its edge against the wall on the near side of the room, on the spot where he had stood his own barrow on the Sunday before. He went back down again. Nobody in that room asked him one word about what he had brought it up there for or where it was going.

---

At about the ninth hour the sun got over the roof of that building and came down onto that bench from the far side, and where it landed on the face of that length of board it made a bright shape about the length of a hand.

The man of about thirty-nine put the flat of his right boot on the face of that board and pushed it. It went along the bench about a foot toward the near end of it and out from under the light, and the bright shape went off the wood and onto nothing and was gone. Then he took his boot off it and put his right hand flat on the face of it where it stood and left it there.

The man of about thirty-eight had brought that board up into that room and left it against a wall and gone back down out of it. The man of about thirty-nine had moved it a foot along another man's bench in his own room.

Neither of them said a word about it, and there was nobody up there to ask.

At about the same hour the woman of about twenty-nine turned a page over in that register and did not look along the bench. The man of about thirty-four went out of that room and came back into it and went out of it again and never said one word to anybody about where he had been.

---

At about the tenth hour the light had gone off that board about a quarter of an hour before it went off the rest of that bench. The man of about thirty-nine's hand had gone off it about the same time as the light had, because it had been on the same surface.

That board was standing against that bench a foot further along it than it had been an hour before. The wood of the bench under it was a shade darker than the wood either side of it, where the light had stood and had gone off.

The face of that board was warmer than the bench it stood against, and it would give that heat back into the wood by morning. There was nobody in that room at that hour who would have known how to tell it from a board that had always been there.
