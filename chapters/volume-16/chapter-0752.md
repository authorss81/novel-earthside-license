# Chapter 0752 — What He Saw Going Past And Could Not Say

Both of Adrian Vale's hands were flat on that length of board where it lay on the flags at the foot of that stair, and he got his fingers under the near end of it and lifted it off the ground and turned it a half-turn and put it down again with the other face up.

It was about the fourth hour on the four hundred and thirty-seventh day of the Bare Month, a Thursday, out past the last named house in that city and on the eleven miles of flats, and the ground at the foot of that stair is a strip of it running from the bottom step to wherever the paving gives out.

He wanted that board set down level with the sill of the one window in the room above, because the sill is a hand's breadth deep and a board laid along the length of it takes the weight of a wet coat off a man's shoulders, and he had carried that board up eleven miles in his own two hands for it. He got it up that stair and it would not go along that sill, and he stood at the near end of that bench holding one end of it for about the length of a minute with the sill against the middle of the board and no more of it along the wood than there was at any hour before, and he carried it back down and leaned it against the outside of that stair wall at the foot of it.

Nobody in that room asked him what he wanted it for and nobody thanked him for carrying it eleven miles.

---

He stood at the foot of that stair from about the fifth hour to about the ninth hour with his own back against the wall on the low side of it, and that strip of ground ran out from the bottom step in front of him.

About four people a day walk on it in the ordinary course of a day. Some of them that morning went over it and some of them stopped on it and one of them stood still in the middle of it for about the length of a minute and then went on, and Adrian Vale watched every one of them go over it and could not afterwards have said to anybody how many he had seen.

That is a fact about him and not about the ground. He was the one person on that morning standing in a place where he could see the whole length of it from the bottom step to wherever the paving gives out, and every one of them went past inside the edge of what he could see, and there is no mark on that ground and no page anywhere in this city on which a person could be put down as having crossed it, and a room decided on a morning that is not one of the fifty days anybody has kept that the people who walk on it are not to be asked what is said upstairs.

At about the seventh hour he went along the outside wall of that stair to where the two ends of that flagstone are open and the mortar has gone out of them, and he got both his own hands into the gap and worked one stone sideways with his own right thumb and his own left forefinger until the stone came up out of its bed about the thickness of two fingers. He turned the stone over and looked at the underside of it, where there was nothing on it at all, and set it back down in the same bed with the same face up. He got both his own hands into the gap once more and pressed the two ends of the stone in against the mortar with his thumbs and took his hands out of it again, and the two ends of that flagstone were closed again and about four people walked over the top of it in the rest of that day and not one of them knew anything about it.

He did not go up that stair and ask one of them anything and he did not go up that stair and ask the woman of about twenty-nine who keeps that book whether she had ever been on it, and it was not anybody's business whether he had.

---

At about the ninth hour the man of about thirty-eight at that salt wharf came up the eleven miles of flats with a length of board on his own shoulder and got to the foot of that stair and put the near end of that board down on the flags, and he set it against that same wall on the other side of Adrian Vale's from about a foot along.

"**There is a board already leaned up here and I am going to put mine beside it and go back down with an empty barrow, and I am not going to say whose that one is and I am not going to say who put mine here either.**"

Nobody in that yard was asked and nobody answered him. Adrian Vale had his own two hands in the sleeves of his own coat and said nothing back to him, and neither man asked the other one thing, and they stood at the foot of that stair a foot apart on either side of two lengths of board until the light had come over the roof above it.

At about the tenth hour Adrian Vale came back down the eleven miles with his own hands empty, and the two boards were leaning against that wall, and one of them was his and one of them was not, and there was nothing on either of them and no way on that wall to tell which of the two men had put which one there first.
