# Chapter 0774 — Two Hollows Worn In A Bench

She had her own right hand flat on the wood of that bench, her thumb down the edge of the page, and she had been holding that book flat like that every morning of the flood.

It was the fourth hour on the four hundred and fifty-ninth day of the Bare Month, a Friday. The light had not come over that roof yet and was standing in the middle of that floor. Five figures stood in the column that holds them, and a day had been set against each of the five, and they were in the order they were made in. None of the five had been struck through. Nothing had ever been entered after them.

The book lifted a corner of itself off the wood while she was not looking at it.

She put her hand on that corner and pushed it down, and it came up again where her hand was not. So she held the corner. Then she took her hand off the page and put it flat on the wood on the other side of the book, and it lifted there too, at the same time, both ends at once, like a tray with the middle of it sagged.

---

The bench at the near end of that room was not flat, and had not been flat for a length of days she could have named, and there was no light on it at that hour to show her.

There were two hollows in the wood of it, side by side down its length, a forearm apart, one on each side of the place where a book lies when a book is lying open in front of somebody. Both of them were the shape of a person leaning on a bench with two hands out on either side of a book. The one on the near side of that bench was deeper at the near end of it than at the far end of it, which is the wear of a man who puts his weight forward while he is talking and takes it back when he is not.

The book lay across both of them and the high wood between them, and rocked on the high wood. She had been holding one end of it down with her hand every morning of this flood, and nobody had ever asked her why.

Nobody in that room was going to be told what was under her hands. She had worked out on the second or third morning of the flood that a hollow in a bench is not a thing anybody in a room says out loud, and she had gone on holding that book down ever since.

At about the fifth hour the woman of about fifty-two gave the figure into that room, in the ordinary course.

"**About nine of us. I have said that every morning of this flood and I could not do a morning without it, and I would like somebody in this room to stop me.**"

Nobody improved on one word of it, and nobody in that room stopped her. The man of about thirty-four had a figure of his own, and he gave it to nobody, and he did not take hers up either.

---

At about the seventh hour the man of about thirty-four came in off the row. He put his coat over the end of that bench and came and stood at the near end of it where he stands.

He put both hands flat on the wood on either side of the second of those two hollows, the way he had put them down every morning of this flood, and the far side of one of his hands was the near side of hers.

The dip in the wood took the weight of him. His hands went about half an inch lower than they had gone a week before, and it was not the wood that had changed. He did not look down at either hand, and he did not say a word about it.

Then he sat down in it, and it took him the way it had taken him every morning of this flood, and he put his elbows on the wood on either side of his own place and was comfortable in it.

He never once worked out that his own weight was doing it. In all the years he had been leaning on things he had never once had a thought about where the shape of a thing came from.

---

The man of about thirty-eight came up the stair with his barrow and went round the end of that bench and out again. The man of about thirty-nine was at the far end of that bench, on the far side of it with his back to the wall, and the flat of his right hand lay on the face of his own board where it stood against the leg. Not one person in that room said a word to him at any hour of that morning.

At about the ninth hour the light came over that roof and came across the floor and reached the near end of that bench. It found the book in the two hollows in it, and the woman of about twenty-nine took her hands off the wood, because the high wood between the two hollows took that book flat and held it there on its own.

That floor went dark at about the tenth hour, and the near end of that bench stood with a book lying flat in the two hollows in it and nothing at all holding any part of it down. She would be up that stair at the fourth hour and her right hand would go back onto that page, and it would be a hand on a book and nothing else at all. She knew what the wood between those two hollows was doing, and her hand would be on it anyway.
