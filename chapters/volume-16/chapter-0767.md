# Chapter 0767 — He Said It Aloud And Looked At Nobody

The man of about thirty-four came in off the row with his coat open and stood at the far end of that bench with his hands on his knees, and he had said it before he had taken his coat off.

It was the fourth hour on the four hundred and fifty-second day of the Bare Month, a Friday. The day turned out to be the only one of that run on which anybody up there ever put that thing into words in front of anybody else.

"**There are about nine of us in here this morning. I have given that into this room on more mornings of this flood than I have said anything else, and I have never written it down, and nobody in this room has ever asked me or anybody else where it came from, and I am saying that this morning out loud, and I am not saying it to anybody in particular.**"

He was not saying it to anybody in particular. He had said it to the middle of that floor, the way a man says a thing into a room he has been saying things into for a run of days.

Then he took his hands off his knees, put the flats of them on the wood of the bench, and left them there for the rest of that hour.

No person in that room asked him where it came from. Not one of them improved on a word of it, and none of them told him he had it right and none of them told him he had it wrong.

The piece of chalk stayed inside his coat against his chest from the fourth hour of that day to the tenth hour of it. It had been in that pocket since a morning nobody in that room could put a date to. It did not come out on any morning of the flood, and it did not come out that morning.

There was a sheet on a shelf at the height of a person's shoulder, with a heading on it and one ruled place under that heading, and nobody had ever asked him about that either.

---

At about the sixth hour the man of about twenty-seven came up out of the yard below with a bucket hanging out of his hand and stopped short at the near end of that bench.

"**There is a date in chalk on the outside of that door at the foot of this stair, and there is no name on it, and I have carried water past that door every morning of this flood, and I do not know who put it there and I am not going to find out by asking anybody in this room.**"

The woman of about twenty-nine who kept that register put one hand flat on the wood of the bench without looking up off the page and said out loud that she did not know either. The man of about twenty-seven stood there with his hands on his knees for the length of a minute. He said that he did not know, and that nobody in that room ever had. Nobody said a word back to either of them about either of those two answers.

The woman of about fifty-two was standing where she stood on the mornings she said nothing, with her hands down the sides of her dress, looking at the middle of that floor, and she said nothing at all about any of it.

By the seventh hour, two people in that room had said out loud that they did not know where a thing came from.

One of them had said it about the number that had been said up there for the whole of the flood and had never once been asked after. The other had said it about a day in chalk on the outside of a door. Neither had asked the other. Nobody had asked either of them.

The room had gone on being a room.

---

At about the tenth hour the man of about thirty-nine, who trades on a board, came up that stair with the board under his arm and came the length of that room to the near end of the bench.

He drew the board out from under his arm, turned it over once in the air so that the face of it came up, and laid it down flat on the wood with the long edge along the edge of that bench and squared it up with two fingers. Nineteen rates were lying in the open there in three columns. At the foot of the column that stood between the other two, the wood was bare for the width of two fingers. Nothing had ever been cut into it. Nobody in that city had ever asked him about that gap, and in nine years of carrying that board he had never found a hand that could tell him whose it was.

He put the flat of his right hand down on the face of the board and left it there, and he looked along the rates for the length of a minute without moving his hand.

He had been right about a page in about four seconds in volumes running. He was the man in that room who could look at a thing and be right about it faster than anybody who had spent longer looking. About nine people were standing in that room at that hour, and not one of them said a word about what was lying on that bench.

Then he took his hand off it, put the board back under his arm and went down the stair with it.

The woman of about twenty-nine put her palm flat down on the wood of the bench where that board had been lying, and held it there, the way a person holds a hand on a place to find out whether it is warm.
