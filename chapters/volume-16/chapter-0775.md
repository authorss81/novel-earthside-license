# Chapter 0775 — He Named The Cost First

The man of about thirty-nine came up that stair with his board under his arm. He stood it against the leg of the bench at the near end and put the flat of his right hand on the face of it, the way he had put it every morning of the flood.

It was the fourth hour on the four hundred and sixtieth day of the Bare Month, a Saturday. On the outside of the door at the foot of that stair there was a date in chalk and nothing under the date. The bar was in its sockets on the far side of it. The light was still in the middle of that floor and had not got to the near end of that bench.

About nine people went in and out up there over the space of that morning.

At the fifth hour the woman of about fifty-two gave the figure into the middle of that floor. She had gone at it on more mornings of this flood than at any other thing she had ever said in that room.

"**About nine of us in here this morning. I am saying it first because it is the first thing I say, and I am not going to say anything else about it.**"

Not one of them took one of those words up and set another one beside it.

At the seventh hour the man of about thirty-eight came up with his barrow and gave a different one.

"**Eight of us in here now. I counted what I could see of that floor as I came into it, and it was eight an hour ago, and I am not saying a word about anybody who is not in it.**"

He gave it as his arithmetic and not as anybody else's. Two people in that room said out loud that they could not settle it, and a third one said nothing at all.

Nobody in that room asked him about it, then or afterwards.

---

The woman of about twenty-nine had the book open on the wood of that bench from the fourth hour to the tenth hour. Five figures stood in the column that holds them, one under another, with a day set against each of the five, and the last of those days was a good way back from this one. Nothing had been entered after them on any morning of this flood. Nothing had ever been taken out of that column. No hand went near the page at any hour of that morning.

She sat with her hands flat on the wood on either side of it, the way she sat. She said nothing whatever to anybody about anything at any hour of that day, and nobody in that room said one word to her about it.

At about the eighth hour the man of about thirty-nine took his hand off the face of his board and stood up straight. Every person in that room who was in it at that hour found out where he was standing without turning round.

"**There is one thing I am going to lose out of saying that. I am naming it now, because after this morning there will be nobody in that room left who can hear me name it. I will not be able to say whether any of you came.**"

He said it into the middle of that floor. He was not saying it to anybody in particular, and he was not looking at anybody while he said it.

Nobody improved on a word of it. Nobody argued with it, and nobody agreed with it. Nobody thanked him for it, and nobody said one word to him about whether he had it right or whether he had it wrong. Nobody asked him why, and nobody asked him afterwards whether he agreed with it.

The woman of about fifty-two stood at the far end of that bench with her hands at the sides of her own dress and said nothing whatever about any part of it. She had said a thing of her own into that room four days before, and nothing had come of that one either.

"**No number is said in this room.**"

Seven words. He said them in his own mouth, in front of about nine people, in the middle of the day, with a date in chalk on the outside of that door at the foot of the stair behind him.

---

There was no notice. There had never been a notice in that room. There was no second morning set aside to take a thing back, and nobody in that room had asked anybody for either of those, and there was nobody in four hundred miles that anybody in that room knew of who could have been told.

So the room was the people who turned up. It was done faster than a thing of that weight ought to be done, and it was done in the ordinary way, on a Saturday morning, between the light coming over that roof and the light going off that floor again.

There was no vote. Not one of them put a hand up. Nobody said one word in favour, and nobody said one word against. About four of the people in that room said out loud, in four different words, that they had not known what that was about. About four of the others said nothing at all, and went on with what they had in their own hands.

It was not written down. There was no sheet of anything in that room and no second one either. No hand went near the wood of that bench, or the table, or the shelf at the height of a person's shoulder, with anything to make a mark with. Nothing about that morning was on the face of a thing in that city at the tenth hour of it.

---

At about the ninth hour the man of about thirty-four put a hand flat on the wood of the bench at the near end, on the side of it that was his, and left it lying there. Then he took it off and put it in his coat and left it there. He did not say one word about anything, and there was a book and a forearm of wood between the two of them, and neither of them moved.

At about the ninth hour the man of about thirty-one came up that stair with his satchel under his arm and the flap down against its own side. He set it on the wood of the bench, stood up straight, and said it into the middle of that floor, the way a man says a thing that has just occurred to him and is not said to anybody.

"**I have brought up what I was given this morning and I am going down that stair with it, and I have not the least idea what I am going to do with my hands at the fourth hour on Monday.**"

Nobody improved on a word of it, and nobody said a word back to him about it. He picked the satchel up off the wood and went back down that stair. The flap was down against its own side the whole of the way.

The man of about thirty-eight took his barrow down that stair at about the tenth hour. The woman of about twenty-nine shut the book and put it back in the place on the shelf behind that bench where it had stood every evening of this flood. She put her own hand flat on the cover of it and left it there for the length of a breath.

---

The man of about thirty-nine got that board from under his arm, turned it face down against his side, and went down the stair with it.

He stopped on the top step, halfway down, and put the flat of his hand against the wall beside him, the way a man steadies himself on a stair going down in a room that is not lit yet. He stood there the length of a breath with his palm on that wall.

Then he took his hand off it and went on down and out into the row.

The wall at the top of that stair had dust on it the length of a hand's width, and there was a print in it now, the print of the flat of a palm. The stair was dark. The print would be there in the morning, and it would be the first thing anybody put a hand into.
