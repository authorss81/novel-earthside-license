# Chapter 0782 — Nobody Asked What The Morning Held

The man of about thirty-nine laid his board flat on the wood at the near end of that bench and stood with his right hand resting on the face of it. The woman of about twenty-nine sat beside the book with her hands on the wood on either side of the page.

It was the fourth hour on the four hundred and sixty-seventh day of the Bare Month, a Saturday. The light had come over the roof and lay across the middle of that floor. Down below, the stair-foot door showed its chalk day outward, and the bar rested in the sockets inside.

People came up and settled. The man of about thirty-four took his hollow. The woman of about fifty-two waited near the far end, her hands hanging by her dress. The man of about twenty-seven set his bucket by the window and stayed beside it.

---

At about the sixth hour the keeper turned a leaf and smoothed it with her thumb. The middle column of the page held five ink strokes, each with a day set against it in her own hand, none struck through, with nothing entered after them. The high ridge of wood between the two hollows held the book level without her help, and still she kept her hands where they were.

The man of about thirty-eight did not come up. His corner at the far end stood empty, and his barrow was nowhere on that floor. At about the seventh hour the man of about thirty-four looked toward that corner and then back at the middle of the floor.

**"He will be out with the weather," he said to nobody in particular. "The flats have had two days of it."**

The woman of about fifty-two nodded once and said nothing. The man of about thirty-nine lifted his hand off the board and folded his arms. The keeper did not look up.

A silence settled that was not the silence of waiting. It was the silence of a room that had said a thing every morning for a season and now passed the hour where the thing used to stand. At about the eighth hour two people shifted on the bench at once, as if the same thought had touched them.

The man of about twenty-seven cleared his throat from the window.

**"Yesterday at this hour we were louder, I remember the sound of it," he said.**

**"It was Friday," the man of about thirty-four said. "Fridays carry the week's talk."**

Neither of them said what had made Friday loud. Nobody at that bench named the hour that had gone quiet. The woman of about twenty-nine turned another leaf, though she had not finished the one before it.

---

At about the ninth hour the man of about thirty-nine picked his board up and carried it the length of the room to the far end. He set it down flat on the wood there, face up, squared with the end of the bench, and left his hand on it for a while. Then he took his hand away and stood back.

Three people looked at the board and looked away. The bare place at the foot of the middle column of rates caught the light for a moment, about as wide as two fingers of wood with nothing cut in it. He did not cover it and did not turn the board over.

The woman of about fifty-two came a step nearer and stopped. Her hands rose a little from her sides and fell again. She might have been going to straighten the board, or only to steady herself on the bench. She did neither.

At about the tenth hour he lifted the board again, turned the face against his side, and went down that stair with it under his arm. The room emptied behind him. The keeper carried the book to the shelf behind the bench and set it down before the dark shape that is never lifted by any hand but hers. She laid her palm flat on the cover for the length of a breath.

That stretch of the bench kept the shape of the board in its dust for an hour after the stair went quiet. Then the light moved off that floor, and the dust settled back, and the wood showed nothing where hands and boards had been all morning.
