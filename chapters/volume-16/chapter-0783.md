# Chapter 0783 — Two Men At The Market End

The man of about thirty-four stood at his trestle at the market end with both hands on the bare back of his own board. The morning damp had got into the wood, and he wiped it with his sleeve and then stopped, as if wiping was more than the place needed.

It was the fourth hour on the four hundred and sixty-eighth day of the Bare Month, a Sunday. The stalls stood in a line under wet canvas, two along from his own to the store's end. Out past the last named house, sixteen willow posts stood with eleven withies bound onto them with the same cord, and no hand had moved any part of them.

The man of about thirty-one came up the flags with his satchel under his arm, the flap down, and stopped at the next trestle to tighten a leg. His hands worked the pin with care. He did not open the satchel and did not set it down.

---

**"What hour did you get in?" the stallkeeper asked, without looking up from his board.**

**"Before the light," the carrier said. "The ground past the gate is soft. I came round by the paving."**

The man of about thirty-four nodded. He took a cloth from his pocket and ran it along the edge of his trestle, though there was nothing on the edge to take off. The carrier finished the leg and stood back to test it with his weight.

**"That will hold," the carrier said.**

**"It held yesterday," the stallkeeper said. "It will hold tomorrow."**

They stood a few feet apart at the same end of the market, with the whole length of empty flags in front of them. A woman went past with a basket and wished them the morning, and both men answered her together and then smiled at having spoken at once.

The carrier shifted the satchel higher under his arm. The stallkeeper folded the cloth and put it away. Neither asked the other what had kept him late or what he carried. The stallkeeper's chalk stayed in his inside breast pocket, where it has stayed since long before this flood, and his hand never went near it.

At about the sixth hour the rain came on again, light and steady. The carrier pulled his coat over the satchel flap. The stallkeeper tipped his board a little forward so the wet ran off the back of it into the flags.

---

At about the seventh hour the man of about sixty came down the side turning with his handcart, the mallet with its cord-bound head riding on the bed under a folded sack. He raised a hand to the two men and went on toward the shut road. The carrier watched him go.

**"He mends that cart every Sunday," the carrier said.**

**"He mends it when it wants it," the stallkeeper said. " Sundays only look that way."**

The carrier laughed once, short. He lifted his satchel strap and settled it. The two men had nothing more to say, and the lack of it sat easy between them. They had stood at that end through a whole flood of mornings, and asking would have been a different kind of morning.

At about the tenth hour the stalls came down in the ordinary way. The stallkeeper turned his board face in against his trestle, the way he always leaves it, and the carrier went off with his satchel still shut. The flags held their puddles, and the canvas dripped from its lowest corners onto the wood below.
