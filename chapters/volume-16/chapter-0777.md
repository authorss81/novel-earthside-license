# Chapter 0777 — A Morning Exactly Like The One Before It

The man of about thirty-four came in off the row at the fourth hour. He put his coat over the end of the bench and came and sat down in the hollow in that wood, and his elbows went onto the wood on either side of him, and the wood took him the way it had taken him the day before.

It was the fourth hour on the four hundred and sixty-second day of the Bare Month, a Monday. The man of about thirty-nine was at the near end of that bench, his board flat on the wood in front of him and his hand on the face of it. The woman of about twenty-nine had the book open in front of her and her own hands on the wood on either side of the page. The light came over that roof at the ninth hour and reached the near end of that bench, the way it had reached it on every morning of this flood.

Nobody in that room could have told you one thing that had happened in it the day before that had not also happened in it the morning before that. Nobody tried.

---

At about the fifth hour, where a thing had been said into that floor every morning of this flood for longer than anybody in that room could date, nobody said it, and the room went on getting past it.

The man of about twenty-seven came up out of the yard below with his bucket, and said the water in that yard had come down a foot since Friday and that he was going to have to find somewhere else to put the trough. The man of about thirty-eight came up at the seventh hour with his barrow and gave the room his own account of that morning at the wharf, which was about the wind. The woman of about fifty-two said the light in that room came in about a quarter of an hour later every day this week, and that one day it would not come in at all.

Nobody asked her how she knew, and nobody asked anybody anything else, and that was the morning.

At about the seventh hour the man of about thirty-four said it out loud into that room, to nobody in it.

"**A man could have come in at the fifth hour on Friday morning, or Saturday morning, or this morning, and he would not have been able to tell one of the three from the other two, and we have been getting on with it, and not one of us has said one word about it.**"

Nobody improved on a word of it. Nobody put it back to him. The man of about thirty-nine put the flat of his right hand on the face of that board and said nothing whatever, and he was not asked about it, and he was not asked anything at all that morning.

---

The man of about thirty-four had a figure of his own.

He had kept it in his own head on that same bench at that same hour on every morning of this flood. It was his arithmetic and not anybody else's. It had never once come out of his mouth in that room, and nobody in that room had ever asked him for it, including the man of about thirty-eight, who had his own, and the woman of about fifty-two, who had hers, and the man of about thirty-nine, who had had the loudest of all of them.

On this morning nobody in that room was asking anybody for anything at all. They were all sitting in a room where nothing was on offer, in the ordinary way. The man of about thirty-four went on keeping his figure in his own head where it had been for the length of a flood, and it was the only number in that room that had ever been anybody's own.

At about the eighth hour the man of about thirty-nine got up off the near end of that bench with his board under his arm and went down that stair and out into that row.

The room carried on. That is the whole of what happened while he was gone. He came back up at about the tenth hour with the board under his arm again and stood it against the leg of that bench and put his hand flat on the face of it. Not one person in that room turned round. The man of about thirty-four said goodnight to him, and he took it with him down that stair without stopping.

The man of about thirty-four was the only person in that room who noticed any of it. He noticed it at the eighth hour, and he sat with the two hollows under his elbows and his own figure in his own head, and worked out for the first time in a run of mornings that he had been counting a room from the corner of his own eye at his stall two stalls along.

Nobody was going to do it with him any more.

---

At the tenth hour the man of about thirty-four lifted his two hands clear of the wood of that bench.

He turned them over, both of them, palm up, the way a man looks at what he has been holding for six hours to see whether there is anything on them. There was nothing on them at all, and that was the whole of what six hours of a bench had come to.

He shut them, put them back into his coat, and sat on in that bench after the rest of the room had gone, which was the first morning of that flood he had not got up off it the moment the room emptied.
