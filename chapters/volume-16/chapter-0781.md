# Chapter 0781 — The Empty Place At The Bench

The woman of about twenty-nine set the book down square on the wood at the near end of that bench and laid both hands flat on either side of the open page. The man of about thirty-nine climbed that stair with his board under his arm and set it upright by the bench leg on her side of the page.

It was the fourth hour on the four hundred and sixty-sixth day of the Bare Month, a Friday. Light stood in the middle of that floor and had not reached the wood where her hands were. On the outside of the door below there was a day in chalk and clean wood beneath it. The bar sat in its sockets on the far side.

He put the flat of his right hand on the face of the board and kept it there. She turned a leaf over without lifting her elbows. The two hollows in that wood sat one on each side of the book, and the high ridge between them held the leaves level. People came up that stair in ones and twos and found their places along the bench and the wall.

---

At about the fifth hour the man of about thirty-four came in from the market with rain on his shoulders and sat down in the hollow that was his. He put his elbows out and looked at the middle of that floor for a while, the way a man looks when he is waiting for a thing to start.

The woman of about fifty-two stood at the far end of the bench with her hands at the sides of her dress. She had been on her feet since the fourth hour. At about the sixth hour she crossed to the table, lifted the jug there, and set it down again without pouring.

The man of about twenty-seven came up out of the yard with his bucket swinging and stopped at the foot of the bench. He nodded to the keeper and went to stand by the window, where the sill was dry. Nobody told him where to put the bucket, and he set it against the wall by his boot.

At about the seventh hour the man of about thirty-four leaned toward the keeper and spoke low, so that only the near end of the bench heard him.

**"He has not come up yet this morning. I kept his place clear till the sixth hour, and then I took it back."**

She did not lift her eyes off the page. The man of about thirty-nine lifted his palm from his board face and pocketed the hand. Nobody else along that bench turned round.

---

The place he meant was the stretch of bench at the far end, where the man of about thirty-eight sits with his barrow behind him on the mornings he comes up. The barrow was not on the floor above. It had not been carried up, and the corner where its handles rest against the wall stood bare.

The woman of about fifty-two looked once toward that end and then away. She put her hands together in front of her dress and then let them fall again. The man of about thirty-four sat back in his hollow and folded his arms.

**"He is out on the flats. The weather has the second mile under water, and he will be walking round it."** The man of about twenty-seven said it from the window.

Nobody answered him. The keeper smoothed the page with her thumb and went on reading the middle column, where five ink strokes stood one under another with a day set against each. Nothing had been added after them on any morning of this flood, and nothing had been taken away.

At about the eighth hour the man of about thirty-nine went down that stair with his board under his arm and came back up before the ninth. He stood the board against the leg again and put his hand on its face. The far end of the bench stayed as it was. No coat hung there. No hands rested on that wood.

The woman of about fifty-two moved along the bench by half a pace, as if to cover the gap, and then stopped herself. She looked at her own hands and put them at her sides again.

---

At about the tenth hour the room emptied in the ordinary way. The man of about thirty-four went first, then the carrier with his satchel, then the man of about twenty-seven with his bucket. The keeper shut the book and carried it the length of the room in both hands and set it on the shelf behind the bench, in front of the dark shape that stands there and is never lifted.

The man of about thirty-nine lifted his board off the leg with his left hand and went down that stair with the face of it against his side. His right hand stayed open at his side all the way down.

At the far end of the bench the wood held the cold of the morning after every hand had left it. The jug on the table stood where it had stood since the sixth hour, with the water in it untouched, and the cloth under it folded twice and smooth at the corners.
