# Chapter 0784 — The Stool Beside The Bench

The woman of about twenty-nine came up that stair first and set the book open on the wood at the near end of that bench. She laid her hands on either side of the page and waited for the light to reach her.

It was the fourth hour on the four hundred and sixty-ninth day of the Bare Month, a Monday. The man of about thirty-nine followed her up with his board under his arm and leaned it on the bench leg beside her book. He laid his right hand on the face of it. Below them the door showed a chalk day on the wood outside, bare timber under the marks, and the bar resting inside its sockets.

At the side of that bench stood the stool the woman of about fifty-two had carried out and left where anybody could sit. The wood of its seat was dark with use, and one leg stood on a knot in the floorboards. Nobody had moved it for days.

---

The morning filled in the ordinary way. The man of about thirty-four came in from the market and took his hollow. The man of about twenty-seven brought his bucket up and set it by the window. The carrier stopped short of the bench with his satchel under his arm, the heel of his hand on the flap, and then went to stand by the wall.

The woman of about fifty-two came up last and stood at the far end of the bench. Her hands were at the sides of her dress. She looked at the stool, and for a moment her weight shifted as if she would go to it. Then she stayed where she was.

At about the sixth hour the keeper turned a leaf. The five ink strokes in the middle column stood with a day against each, none struck, nothing added after. The ridge of wood between the two hollows held the page level. She kept her hands on the wood all the same.

The man of about thirty-nine took his hand off his board and put it in his coat. The stool stood a pace from his boot, empty, with the light beginning to touch its edge. He did not look at it. Nobody in that room looked at it for long.

At about the seventh hour the man of about thirty-four stretched his legs out under the bench and his boot touched the leg of the stool. It rocked once on the knot in the floor and settled. He drew his foot back at once.

**"Beg pardon," he said to the floor in general. "It is in the way of my feet."**

**"It has always been in the way of somebody's feet," the woman of about fifty-two said from the far end. "That is what it is for."**

She did not move toward it. She had sat on it once, weeks back, and had not sat on it since. Her hands stayed where they were. The stool stayed where it was, at the side of the bench, with nobody on it.

---

When the ninth hour came the man of about thirty-nine bore his board down the room and set it face up at the far end, where it stayed while feet went by. At the tenth he took it up again with the face against his side and went down that stair.

The room emptied. The keeper closed the book and laid it on the long shelf at the back of the bench, ahead of the shaded form only her hands ever approach. The stool stood on in the thin light after every boot had gone down the stair, with dust on its seat and one leg on the knot, holding its place beside the bench it serves.
