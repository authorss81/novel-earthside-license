# Chapter 0785 — Water Over The Stone Lip

The man of about twenty-seven went down to the yard at the near end of eleven miles of flats with his bucket swinging from his hand. He set the bucket down by the trough and put both hands on the rim to test it for rocking.

It was the fourth hour on the four hundred and seventieth day of the Bare Month, a Tuesday. The wind came up the flats behind him and lifted the water in the trough into small ridges. The flags were wet from the night, and the low place in the stone at the side of the yard held a palm's depth of standing water.

He lifted the bucket and let it down into the low place, where it settled on its side in the water. The rag lay on the stone lip of the step, folded once, and not inside the trough. On the step beside it stood the box of chalk with its lid ajar, the way it always stands, for the lid does not shut.

---

He worked the trough first. He took the rag off the lip and wrung it into the flags, then wiped the lip from one end to the other, pushing the night's grit ahead of it. The stone came clean in a band a hand's breadth wide. He laid the rag back on the lip with the damp side up.

At about the fifth hour the carrier came through the gate with his satchel under his arm and stopped to watch. About four people a day cross that paving at the foot of the stair in the market, and this yard has its own tread of feet, and the two do not meet.

**"That lip holds the wet longer than the flags," the carrier said.**

**"It holds what you leave on it," the man of about twenty-seven said. "I leave it damp for the next man."**

The carrier nodded and went on. The man of about twenty-seven picked up the bucket from the low place and poured a steady stream along the lip, washing the grit down into the flags. The water ran clear by the second pour. He set the bucket back down in the low place, on its side, where it belongs.

At about the seventh hour the man of about thirty-eight came in with his barrow and stood it where he stands it, a hand's breadth further off the wall than he used to. He looked at the clean lip and ran his thumb along a stretch of it.

**"You have got it bare," he said.**

**"It will not stay bare," the man of about twenty-seven said. "The wind brings it back by night."**

The man of about thirty-eight grunted and went to his work at the stack. Neither asked the other for anything more. The trough stood full, and the lip stood damp, and the bucket lay in the low place, and the rag lay where it lies.

---

At about the ninth hour the weather came over the flats in a single push and put a skin of water across the flags. The trough took it and sent it over its rim in a thin sheet. The lip shed it in beads. The rag grew heavy and stayed where it was.

The man of about twenty-seven wrung the rag once more and spread it again on the stone. He set the chalk box a finger's width further from the edge, out of the drip. The lid rocked but did not close, and he left it as it was.

By the tenth hour the yard emptied. The bucket rested in the low place with the light going out of the water around it. The trough stood level and full, with nothing in it but water, and the stone lip held its damp against the evening wind.
