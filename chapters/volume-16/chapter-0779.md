# Chapter 0779 — Two Fingers Of Bare Wood

The man of about thirty-nine came up at the fourth hour carrying that board under his arm. He did not stand it in the gap at the far end of that bench with its back to the wall along the far side, which is where he had stood it on every morning of that flood before the Saturday.

He put it down flat on the wood at that far end instead, face up, with the long edge of it along the edge of that bench and its own far end squared up with the end of the bench. He put the flat of his right hand down on the face of it, and then he took the hand off and left the board lying there, and went down that stair and out into the row.

It was the fourth hour on the four hundred and sixty-fourth day of the Bare Month, a Wednesday. The woman of about twenty-nine was at the near end of that bench, the book open in front of her, her own hands flat on the wood on either side of the page. A board lying face up on a bench where people sit is the one thing in that room that a stranger would be entitled to pick up.

---

At about the sixth hour the man of about thirty-four came in off the row and stopped in the middle of that floor.

He had come up that stair at the fourth hour, before the light had got to the near end of that bench. He had sat down in the hollow in that wood with his elbows out on either side of him, and he had gone off the row again with a bundle under his arm, and he had not seen anything on that bench at the far end of it then.

Now there was a board lying face up on the wood at the far end of that bench, in the light. A good many of the marks cut into the face of it were standing up in that light, and the bare place at the foot of the middle one of the three was not one of them.

He stood in the middle of that floor looking at it for the length of about ten seconds.

Nobody in that room said one word about it. The woman of about twenty-nine did not look up off her own page. The man of about thirty-eight was at the near end of that bench with a barrow behind him. Nobody said one word to anybody about a board lying face up on a bench, and nobody in that room touched it.

He did not touch it either. He went the length of that floor and sat down at the far end of that bench, a few feet along from it, and put his hands on the flat wood on either side of where he was sitting, and looked at the middle of that floor.

---

For the length of about a minute the woman of about twenty-nine was the only person in that room who stood up off that bench.

She had been in that room every morning of this flood, and he had carried that board the length of that room to its far end on the morning before and had laid it down flat on the wood there. She had been at the near end of that bench on that morning with her own book open in front of her, and she was at the near end of it now with the same book open in the same place.

She came the length of that floor and stood over it and looked at the face of it where it lay in the light, and this was the first morning of that flood that she had ever stood that near to it. Nineteen rates were cut into that face in three columns, cut deep and gone dark at the edges. Down at the foot of the middle one of those three, between the other two, there was a stretch of bare wood about two fingers across, and it was the colour the wood had been before anything was ever cut into that face.

Nothing had ever been cut into that place. Nothing had ever been cut out of it either. There was no smoothing over it and no paint on it, and the grain of the wood in it ran the same way as the grain either side of it, and a man's hand had been on that board for nine years and had not worn it.

She stood over it for a minute and put her right hand out about a foot over that wood and stopped it there. Then she took it back and went the length of that floor and sat down at the near end of that bench and put her hands on the wood on either side of the book in front of her.

She was not going to put a finger on a stranger's board, and there was nobody in that room to say anything to about it, and the man of about thirty-four did not look up off the middle of that floor while she was standing over it.

---

The man of about thirty-nine came back up that stair at about the seventh hour and came the length of that floor and stood over that board where he had left it.

He did not look at the bare place. He picked the board up off that bench and stood it against the leg of it with the face of it turned out, the way he had stood it every morning of that flood. He put the flat of his right hand on the face of it where it stood and kept it there for the rest of the morning.

Nobody in that room asked him one word about the board at any hour of that day, and the man of about thirty-four sat a few feet along that bench from it and looked at the middle of that floor for an hour. At the far end of that bench the woman of about fifty-two stood with her hands down the sides of her own dress and said nothing whatever.

At about the tenth hour he took the flat of his right hand off that face and put it low down over the bare place at the foot of the column between the other two. His own hand came to that place without him having to look for it, and then he lifted the board off the leg of that bench with his left one.

He kept his hand there. He went down that stair with the palm of his right hand flat on a piece of bare wood about two fingers wide. The last thing that happened to that place in that room's daylight was a hand covering it.

It went down that stair under his palm and out into that row, and it was on no page of anything in this city, and there was no name on it anywhere at all. By the time the light came over that roof in the morning that board would be lying face up in it at the far end of that bench, the ninth year of it and about two fingers wide. The hand that went down that stair over that place was the same hand that would go up it in the morning.
