# Chapter 0761 — The Chair Stood Against The Wall All Day

That chair stood at the far side of the room between the far end of the bench and the wall, turned the wrong way round for anybody who wanted to use it: a man sitting in it had his back to the middle of the floor and his face to the wall.

He got his fingers under the two ends of the seat and turned it about on the flags and walked it the length of his own arms until the back of it stood against that wall. Now a man sitting in it would have his back to the wall and his face to the door.

It was the four hundred and forty-sixth day of the Bare Month, which was a Saturday, and the light had not got into that room yet. He had been up that stair twice in the dark that morning to learn the shape of it and had stopped at the bench both times.

Adrian had been coming up that stair for a fortnight. He had watched two mornings go past the face of a man sitting in that chair with his shoulders square to the door, and had thought, both times, that he was not watching the door. He had looked for a reason to put it down to the man and had not been able to find one that was not contempt.

He put the flats of his hands on the arms of it and sat.

From there he could see the near end of the bench, the flags, and about half the floor. The one window stood high in the wall along the far side and its sill was above his head where he sat. The shelf at the height of a person's shoulder was behind his left shoulder, so that to see what was lying on it he had to turn about and go on looking at it over the top of his own shoulder. He did that twice. The second time, the room behind him had gone on without him in it.

A man sitting there had his back to the wall and his face to the door. He had been trying to arrange that for a fortnight, and a chair was all there was, and the chair was standing in the wrong place for it and he had turned it into the right place for it and it was still in the wrong place.

It took him about four minutes to work out why that was. The chair had not been standing in the middle of that floor. It had been standing in the gap at the far side, between the far end of that bench and that wall, and that gap was a man's shoulders wide, and it was the width that room used all day to get past its own bench. He had got a chair with his back to the wall and put it exactly where the room walked.

He was still in it when he heard the stair.

---

The man of about thirty-nine came up with his board under his arm, the way he came up every morning of the flood, and got as far as the flags before he stopped.

Adrian watched him stop. He had come up that stair four hundred times. In all of them the man had walked straight to the far end of that bench and put the flat of his right hand on the face of his board. And in the first two seconds of that walk he had turned his head to find the chair, the way a man turns his head for a thing he has been using a long time.

He could not get to the far end of that bench. His head came round a second time, looking for the gap he had been standing in for the whole of the flood, and stopped against a chair.

Nineteen rates were cut into the face of that board in three columns, and it had gone on that man's arm through four markets for about nine years. At the foot of the column that stood between the other two there was a bare place about the width of two fingers. Nothing had ever been cut in it, and nobody in this city had ever asked him about it.

Adrian put his hands on the arms of the chair and pushed himself up out of it and stood to the side of it against the wall, out of the line between that man and that bench.

The man of about thirty-nine looked at the chair against the wall for the length of a minute. Then he put the flat of his hand on the face of his board and stood where he was.

"**There is a chair by this wall that has not been by this wall since the flood came, and I have come up that stair on every morning of it, and I am not going to ask which of you put it there.**"

Then he turned his board about under his arm and went along the bench to the far end of it. He put his boots in the gap between that bench and that wall and stood in it with his board against the leg of the bench.

He did not sit down on the chair.

He was not going to sit down on it. That was the whole of it. It was a chair somebody else had moved. He was not going to be the one to make it furniture again by the first use of it. He would rather stand in a gap in the dark for six hours than hand somebody the pleasure of a question he was not going to ask.

Adrian had built a morning out of that chair. Adrian had moved it and sat in it and turned about twice looking at a shelf behind his own shoulder. The man of about thirty-nine had come up the stair and dismantled the whole of that by not sitting down, and had not said one word that anybody could call an answer.

---

At the seventh hour the man of about twenty-seven came up with the bucket hanging out of his hand and stopped at the top of that stair.

He had done the same thing every morning for a length of days he had stopped keeping. You came up, you put the bucket down under the bench at the near end, you got on with the morning. This morning the near end of that bench was still free and the flags under it were still free. But there was a chair standing against the far wall with its back to the room, and that made a channel of the whole width of that floor between the chair and the far end of the bench. The gap between that bench and that wall had been a man's shoulders wide for as long as anybody could remember. It was a chair's width less than it had been an hour before.

He came down the room sideways with the bucket held out in front of him and turned it on its side in the channel. He went along it like that on the strength of his boot. At the near end of that bench he set the bucket down where it went and wiped his mouth with the back of his hand.

"**I am not saying anything about it. I have come up this stair with this bucket for a run of mornings you could not put a number on, and I have never once been able to put that stair down that width in one go, and I am not saying anything about it.**"

Nobody answered him. The woman of about twenty-nine who kept the register had the book open on the wood at the near end of that bench and did not look up off the page.

The man of about thirty-four went on resting his weight against that wall. The chalk stayed inside his coat against his chest, and it did not come out of that pocket on any morning of the flood, and it did not come out of it that morning.

The man of about twenty-seven took his bucket and went back down the stair.

---

The figure was given in that room before anybody was up the stair, and it was given once, and it was not said again. Nobody put how many of them were in that room out loud after that, and nobody wrote it down, and no hand in that room had ever been near a place where it could have gone.

Adrian went out of that room at about the ninth hour and down onto the flags at the foot of that stair and stood there with his hands empty. He came and went along the near side of that floor, which is where that room walks. The ground at the foot of that stair took about four people a day and had never been measured by anybody. He looked at the part of it inside the stair's own shadow, which was the part that had any edges to it at all. Then he sat down in that chair again in front of the whole of that room, and nobody in that room said one word to him about it.

The man of about thirty-nine had not moved out of the channel. The light had gone off the flags some while before, and the room had got to the point where the far end of that bench and the wall beside it were one dark shape with a man's boots standing in the middle of it.

At the tenth hour Adrian was still in the chair. He had eaten nothing that day. He had got out of it once, early, to let a man past, and had stood against that wall with his hands at the sides of his coat until a man with a board under his arm had got himself to the far end of that bench, and then at the ninth hour he had gone out of the room altogether and had come back and sat down in it again in front of all of them. There was no way out of that chair that did not begin with standing up in that room, he had known that since the fifth hour, and he went on sitting in it because the chair was his own idea and the floor it had come from was going to be the same floor in the morning.

He looked down at the flags where his feet had turned that chair about at the fourth hour.

There was a skin of grit on them a fortnight deep, and the two ends of that chair had cut two arcs through it, about a foot long each, running side by side. They were the only clean marks in the middle of that floor. Nobody had walked over either of them at any hour of that morning, because everybody in that room went along the near side and everybody came in at the near end of the bench.

One of the two arcs had a man's boot standing in the middle of it. It was not stepped on. It was set down on it, square, the way a man sets a boot down on a place he has decided is his. The clean grit was showing either side of it. Whoever had put it there had not looked down, and had not moved it again.
