# Chapter 0764 — She Said The Cost Before Anything Else

The woman of about fifty-two put the flats of her two hands down on the wood at the far end of that bench, a forearm apart from each other, and left them lying there, and that was the first thing she did that morning.

She had come up the stair in the dark and had the room to herself for the length of about half an hour on the four hundred and forty-ninth day of the Bare Month, which was a Tuesday. On the outside face of the door at the foot of that stair there was a day in chalk and nothing else under it. She had looked at that chalk on her way in the way a person looks at a thing they have looked at a hundred times. Then she had come up and had put her hands on the wood.

At the fourth hour the light came up over the roof above the one window and reached the near end of that bench and stopped short of it. She spoke before she had said anything else that day.

"**It has cost me the only thing I have got, and I am the one person in this room who knows who is not in it. I have never once said that out loud in here, and nobody in this room has ever asked me how I know it, and there is no mouth in four hundred miles that is going to ask me either, and that not having it out loud is the whole of what I have that is mine, and I have paid for it every morning of this flood by not having it out loud.**"

Nobody up there thanked her for it. Not one of them improved on a word of it, and nobody argued with her about it, and she was not asked how she knows it, because no one in that room had ever put that question to her about anything at all.

The man of about thirty-nine, who trades on a board, was at the near end of that bench with the board against the leg of it and his right hand flat on its face. He heard every word of it and he looked at the bare place at the foot of the column between the other two and did not say one word back about any part of what she had said.

---

At the fifth hour he gave the figure into the middle of that floor the way he gave it on most mornings of the flood, and about nine of them were in the room at that hour, and not one of them looked at the woman of about fifty-two while he said it.

"**About nine of us in here this morning. It has come out of this room in my mouth more often than it has come out of anybody else's in it, and that is all I am going to say this morning, and I am saying it before I say anything else.**"

Nothing was done about what she had said. That was the honest shape of the morning and it stayed that shape for an hour.

Then, about the sixth hour, the room began to empty.

It was not a decision. Nobody announced anything and nobody looked at anybody. A person came in, saw the woman of about fifty-two standing at the far end of that bench with her hands at the sides of her dress, and found a reason to be somewhere else, and left. The man of about twenty-seven came up out of the yard below, put his bucket down at the foot of the bench, looked at her for as long as it takes to decide a thing is not your business, and went back down again with the bucket. The man of about thirty-four went out of the room twice and did not stay in it.

Nobody said one word to her about any of it, and that was how she knew they had all heard her, and she stood at the far end of that bench and let it happen.

---

The man of about thirty-four came back up that stair at about the sixth hour with the water jug in his two hands. He set it down on the table where the cloth was laid flat on the wood. Nobody in that room said a word to him about having gone down that stair for it.

At the seventh hour she took her hands off the wood of the bench and put them at the sides of her dress and went and stood in front of the stool at the side of that bench.

She had carried that stool out of a room over a market and set it where anybody could sit on it. Nobody had sat on it on any morning of the flood. She had not sat on it herself on any morning of the flood. She had put it there so that it would not be a thing kept for anybody in particular. She had watched a run of mornings go past without one person in that room or out of it taking her up on it. She had told nobody she was keeping count.

She looked at it for the length of a breath and then she sat down on it with her back to the wall behind that stool and her hands at the sides of her dress, and she looked at the middle of that floor.

The man of about thirty-four moved along that bench about a forearm's length. He did it without being asked and without saying a word about having to, and he did not look at her while he did it. Nobody else up there moved at all. The man of about thirty-eight came up the stair at about the eighth hour with his barrow and went round the two of them and said nothing to either of them about anything.

The light came over the roof above that one window about the ninth hour, came across that floor and reached that bench, and stopped a hand's width short of the woman of about twenty-nine's page. The woman of about fifty-two was sitting at the side of that bench when it came. She did not turn her head towards it.

---

At the tenth hour the light had gone off that floor and the bench at the far end of it stood empty with nothing on the wood of it.

The woman of about fifty-two was still on that stool at the side of the bench with her hands at the sides of her dress, and nobody in that room had said one word to her about the cost or about the sitting down.

The man of about thirty-four was a forearm's length further along that bench than he had been at the fourth hour. He had not moved back in three hours and he was not going to.

There was a jug of water standing on the table in front of him that nobody had drunk out of, and everybody in that room knew the whole length of that bench, and exactly where on it each of them was sitting.
