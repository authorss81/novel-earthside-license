# Chapter 0790 — Empty Hands At The Far End

The man of about thirty-one came up that stair holding his satchel beneath his arm and stopped a third of the way up, where the stair turned. He went back down to the bottom landing, set the satchel against the wall out of the light the door lets in, and came up the rest of it with nothing in his hands.

It was the fourth hour on the four hundred and seventy-fifth day of the Bare Month, a Sunday. The keeper sat at the near end of that bench with the book open before her, both palms resting on the bench wood flanking the leaves. The man of about thirty-nine stood beside her with his board against the leg of the bench and his right hand on the face of it.

The door at the stair foot showed the chalk day outward, with the bar home in its sockets within. Light stood in the middle of that floor. The stool stood at the side of the bench with nobody on it.

---

The carrier walked the length of that floor to the far end of the bench and stood there with both hands empty at his sides. He looked at the wood where the board had lain on other mornings, face up, squared with the end. This morning the wood stood bare.

The woman of about fifty-two stood near that end with her hands at the sides of her dress. At about the sixth hour she moved a pace along the bench to give him room, and he thanked her with a nod. The man of about thirty-four sat in his hollow at the near end and watched them without speaking.

**"I left it below," the carrier said, meaning the satchel. "It has come up this stair every morning of this flood, and this morning I thought the bench should have one morning without it."**

Nobody answered him. The keeper turned a leaf. The man of about thirty-nine lifted his palm from his board and slipped that hand into his coat. The carrier stood on at the far end with his palms open and empty, as if to show there was nothing in them to set down.

At about the seventh hour the man of about twenty-seven came up with his bucket and set it by the window. He looked at the carrier's empty hands and smiled once, briefly. The woman of about fifty-two put her hand on the bench for a moment, then took it away.

---

At the ninth hour the man of about thirty-nine took his board down the room and laid it flat by where the carrier waited. The carrier stepped back to give it room. The two men stood on either side of it for the hour without speaking, one with his hands in his coat and the other with his hands empty.

At about the tenth hour the man of about thirty-nine gathered his board face-in to his side and descended those steps. The carrier went down with him, and at the bottom landing he picked the satchel up by the strap, the flap still down, and carried it out through the door behind him.

The keeper shut the book and set it on the shelf behind that bench, in front of the dark form, the way she had set it every tenth hour of this flood. The far end was not the wood the carrier had looked at in the fourth hour. There was an outline on it now, clean along its four edges, where a board had lain for the length of an hour and the dust had not got under it.
