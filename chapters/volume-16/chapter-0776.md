# Chapter 0776 — The Room Got Louder

The book lay open on the bench at the near end of that room at the fourth hour, with the woman's own hands flat on the wood on either side of the page. He came up at the fourth hour the way he came up it, with that board under his arm, and put it against the leg of that bench, on her side of the book and not at the far end of it, which is where he had put it every morning of this flood.

He put the flat of his right hand down on the face of it and he stayed where he was.

It was the fourth hour on the four hundred and sixty-first day of the Bare Month, a Sunday. People went in and out up there between the fourth hour and the seventh. The light came over that roof and came across that floor and reached the near end of that bench. That was that room's ordinary morning, hour by hour, and not one person in it did anything about the thing that had been said in it the day before.

---

At about the fifth hour, where a thing had been said into the middle of that floor every morning of this flood, nobody said it.

The man of about thirty-nine was at the near end of that bench with his hand on the face of that board and his mouth shut. The woman of about twenty-nine was at the same bench with her page in front of her and her mouth shut. The woman of about fifty-two was on her feet in the middle of that floor with her hands at the sides of her own dress. The man of about thirty-four was sitting in the hollow in that bench with his elbows out and his mouth shut.

About four people in that room noticed the place where a thing usually came, and not one of them said one word about it. Two of them caught themselves and looked at something else.

And then, with nothing standing in front of it, the small talk came forward.

It came forward the way water comes into a low place. The man of about twenty-seven came up out of the yard below with the bucket hanging out of his hand, and said there was a foot of water standing in that yard that had not been there the day before, and that he had got his own boot in it up to the middle coming up that stair. The man of about thirty-four said the boards at the store's end of that row were letting the rain through onto the trestles for the first time this flood, and that he had moved the whole of his own pitch a foot along. The woman of about twenty-nine said the lamp on that table wanted oiling and had wanted it for a length of days.

The man of about twenty-seven and the man of about thirty-one had not had a word with one another in a run of mornings. They had one now about a yard, and it was about nothing whatever, and it took the better part of a quarter of an hour.

That room had been quiet under that figure for the whole of a flood, and it had not known it was quiet. By the seventh hour it was making more noise than it had made at any hour since the flood came.

---

The man of about thirty-nine did not go to the far end of that bench at the fifth hour.

He had gone to the far end of it every morning of this flood and stood in the gap between that bench and the wall along the far side, with his board against the leg of the bench. He had been the first thing anybody saw when they came up that stair. About four people had planned the way they came into that room around where he was standing.

He stayed at the near end of that bench instead, on her side of the book, and he put his board down flat on the wood and stood his right hand flat on the face of it, and he stayed there for the rest of that morning.

Every person in that room came past the near end of that bench for the rest of that morning instead of the far end of it. The whole length of that floor bent itself around a man who was not standing in the place he stood in, and not one of them said a word about the bending.

At about the seventh hour the man of about thirty-eight came up that stair with his barrow and had to come in at the near end, close past the two of them, with the handles up over his own shoulder. He had never once had to do that.

---

The woman of about fifty-two said nothing at all that morning about any part of it.

The man of about thirty-four said nothing at all that morning about any part of it either, and he had said something out loud in that room on most mornings of this flood.

At about the ninth hour the light came over that roof and reached the near end of that bench, and the woman of about twenty-nine lifted her hands clear of the bench, because that high ridge between the two hollows would hold the book flat by itself. She turned a page over.

There was nothing whatever on the back of it, and there had never been anything on the back of it.

She put her hand back down on the wood on either side of the book, and went on sitting at the near end of that bench with the man of about thirty-nine at the same end of it, and a room behind her making more noise than it had made the day before.

At about the tenth hour the light had gone off that floor, and that room was still talking. A man at the near end of the bench with his hand flat on the face of his own board, and a woman at the same end of it with her page in front of her, and the whole of that morning's noise coming in over the two of them from people who had not said one word at the fifth hour.
