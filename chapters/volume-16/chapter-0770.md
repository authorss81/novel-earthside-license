# Chapter 0770 — Nobody Could Say Since Which Morning

The man of about thirty-nine came up that stair at the fifth hour on the four hundred and fifty-fifth day of the Bare Month, a Monday. He turned his board about under his arm and stood the edge of it against the leg of the bench at the near end, and gave the figure into the middle of that floor by himself, the way he went at it on most mornings of the flood.

"**There are about nine of us in here this morning, and that is what there are in here this morning.**"

Not one of them took a single one of those words up and put another one beside it. Nobody asked him where it came from. Nobody said a word to him about it.

At about the seventh hour the man of about thirty-four turned round on that bench and said one thing out loud into that room without turning to anybody in it.

He had a stall of his own two stalls along that row. He had a piece of chalk in his coat that he had not taken out since a morning nobody in that room could date.

He had come up the stair that morning the way he came up every morning of the flood, and he put both hands flat on the wood of that bench on either side of where he was standing.

"**The man who brings that barrow up this stair has been coming up it on the mornings of this flood, and I have been leaving him a length of this bench at the near end of it since the beginning of it, and I cannot tell you this morning since which morning he began, and I have been at it for about an hour and I have not got it.**"

About four of them said out loud that they did not know, in four different words, and none of them was the same word as any of the other three.

The woman of about twenty-nine said that she did not know, and went on looking at the page. The man of about thirty-one said that he carried what he was given and kept it. If a thing had been there when he came up the stair it had been there when he came up the stair. He had not been keeping the beginning of anything. The woman of about fifty-two said that she was not going to guess at it in front of anybody. The man of about thirty-nine said that he did not know, and put his right hand flat on the face of his own board where it stood against the leg of the bench, and said nothing else at all.

About four people in the rest of that room said nothing whatever about it and went on with what they had in their own hands.

The man of about thirty-eight came up at about the eighth hour with his barrow and set it along the near wall where he set it. Nobody in that room said a word to him about anything. He took himself back down out of it.

---

At about the tenth hour the man of about thirty-four put it to the woman of about twenty-nine in the ordinary way, across the wood of the bench. It was the way a man puts a thing to a person he has sat beside every morning for a length of days he could not date.

He put to her, out loud and without any weight on it at all, that not one person in that room could say what that figure had been at the fourth hour of that morning.

"I cannot tell you what it was at the fourth hour," she said. "I gave it at the fourth hour to a room that was not full yet. Nobody put it on a page and there is no rule that says somebody had to."

He nodded at that and did not improve on it, and he did not put it to anybody else.

Then he took his two hands off the wood of that bench and put them into his pockets, and left them there.

He had had his hands on that bench at the near end of it for the length of that morning, one of them flat on the wood on either side of where her page was. It was the way a man holds a place he is not going to leave. In the wood of that bench there were two places a forearm apart where the flood-weather had not got to, because two pairs of hands had been on it since the fourth hour. By the next morning those two places would be a shade paler than the wood round them.

There was nobody in that room to be told about either of them.
