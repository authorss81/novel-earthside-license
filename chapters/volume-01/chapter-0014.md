# Chapter 0014 — Roles

Nobody in the loft had said out loud that the six of them ought to get jobs, and Adrian had been thinking it for four days in a form he was ashamed of, which was the form of a man calculating how much trouble six idle people generate in a city with a flood.

He said it to the table on Thursday morning instead, badly, in the wrong words, and got it taken apart.

"You have a rota on that wall," he said. "And nobody in this room is on it. And every day that is true, somebody in this city has a reason to be annoyed about the six, and eventually the annoyance finds a shape, and the shape is a queue outside a shop." He looked at the page. "So I want us all to have a thing we are doing, where people can see us doing it, that we would keep doing if there were no wall and no gate and nothing to go home to."

"That is a lovely idea and it is the gate," said Jonah Pike.

"It is not the gate."

"It is a gate with six handles," Jonah said. "You are describing a role for every person in this room. And a role is a claim on everybody else's time, and a claim has to be made to somebody, and the somebody has to be a person who can say no, and the *moment* you have six roles you have six relationships with six local institutions, and those six institutions now have a small piece of you, and in eleven months you will have a settlement with a name and a sign and a reason why nobody can throw you out." He put his bread down. "I have watched that movie. I am the villain in it. I would still rather watch it than be a man with no role in a flood."

"Then what is the alternative," said Owen, "besides six people sitting in a loft being described?"

Mina found it on Thursday morning, and it happened the way local things happened, which was that somebody handed her something.

Hesper Saye kept the low room by the salt wharf — two rooms, a bench, a long table, a smell of vinegar and willow bark and boiled bone — and she was fifty-eight and she set Owen Park's scalp open again on Thursday at about eleven, with a pair of tweezers and a bowl, in front of nine people, because she had been told on Wednesday that there was a room over the market where six strangers slept and she did not do private work.

"His head is not closing," she said, in the local language, to Tamsin, twice, because she did not believe the first time. "Nine days. I have had that nine days and it is not a wound that has gone bad, it is a wound that has gone *nowhere*. There is clean skin over it and under the clean skin it is doing nothing."

"What does that mean?" Mina said.

"It means it is not a wound." Saye wiped her hands. "I have a word for it and the word is not medical, it is the word you use for a thing that has been made a promise and the promise was not taken up."

Mina did the thing she was good at, which was to stop, and ask a question, and wait.

"May I write the word down?" she said, in the local language, very slowly, three times, and Saye said yes, and Mina wrote it, and asked for it again in English, and Saye said it, and it came out as **unclosed**.

And then Saye asked Mina, in the local language, and Tamsin translated it into English before she had got to the end of the sentence: "And who is the witness on it? Because a thing with no witness does not stay open. It closes and it goes wrong. A thing with a witness stays open until the witness comes back."

Mina put the pencil down.

"Who made the promise?" she said.

"I do not know. I have not been asked and it is not my room."

It was not the local room. Adrian had been in the yard when Mina came up the stair at noon and she had the flat, careful walk she had had since Tuesday, and she said one sentence and then sat down on the end of the cot and put her hands on her knees.

"Since Tuesday," she said. "I have been the only person in this room who has been in charge of anything real since Tuesday, and I have been getting it wrong, and I found out this morning that the reason I have been getting it wrong is that I have been doing it in a room where nobody can correct me."

"You've been—"

"I changed two things about how we wash." She said it in the flat voice. "I put a rule that we do not use river water on the grazes, because river water here is not river water. And I was *right*, and the reason I was right is that I read it in a book about hospital water in 2019 and I have not seen this river. And so I put it in the loft and everybody obeyed it, and there is a woman in this city with a low room by the wharf who has been washing Veyran wounds in boiled willow bark since before I was born, and she came up to my loft this morning because somebody had told her there were strangers with injuries, and she took one look at Priya's forearm and said the word *cleared* in a way I have not heard a doctor use in six years, and I have been wrong in a room that could not tell me for four days."

The room was quiet.

"So I want to go and be wrong in a room that can tell me," Mina said. "That is the whole of what I am asking for."

Tamsin said, in English, without any ceremony at all: "Then you are not asking. I am telling you that I have been asked for a translator twice this week by people who are too polite to ask twice, and one of them was the woman by the wharf, and the other one was a man on the upper road who has nine grandchildren and a bad chest. If you go to Saye's room on Monday and you do not say my name, she will assume you are a woman who does not speak it, and she will turn her back. Say my name. That is all. Say my name and it is arranged."

Dante got his arm back on Thursday afternoon, and he got it back badly, and it was the same afternoon he discovered what a rota was.

It had been dislocated since Tuesday and it had not been reduced by anybody with authority, and it had been held against his chest for three days by a man who had been told to hold it against his chest by a woman with a needle and boiling water, and the three days had turned an arm into a rumour.

Hesper Saye put it back in the low room, with Mina watching, in front of eleven people, because Saye did not do private work either, and it took eleven seconds and Dante made a noise like a dropped barrow and then apologised for making the noise.

"You are a very large man to be carrying that," Saye said. "Three days. You should have had it put back on the second day. Why did nobody put it back?"

"Because nobody could," Dante said, through his teeth. "Because it needs a person who knows, and we don't know anybody, and we were not going to ask a stranger to put my arm right in a room with five other strangers in it."

"That is stupid," Saye said, cheerfully, and started strapping him. "You have a wall in it. Ask the wall. That is what it is for." Then, in the local language, to Tamsin: "Tell him the thing about the arm, tell him the arm is never the arm."

"What is the thing about the arm," Dante said.

"The arm is not the shoulder," Saye said, "and the shoulder has been doing the arm's work for three days without being asked, and that is the part that will cost you. In three weeks you will lift a sack and something will go, and it will not be the arm and it will not be the sack, and you will spend a year telling everybody it was the sack. And you will not believe me, and I have said this to nine hundred men and I have been right about four hundred of them." She finished the strap and did not let go of it. "You will be careful with it for eleven days and then you will stop being careful with it and that is when it goes. I have seen it nine hundred times. It is never the heavy thing. It is always the day after the heavy thing."

Dante Kuo sat on a bench in a room by the salt wharf with his arm back in its place for the first time since Tuesday, and put his face in his good hand, and did not say anything for a while.

Then he asked the question that turned the rest of his week over.

"Where do the men come from," he said, in English, to nobody, to the room, to Tamsin. "For the flood work. Roke's got forty. The gate crew is what, twenty-two? Where do they come from. Somebody has to *make* them come."

"They come from being asked," said Tamsin.

"Then who gets asked?"

"The person who is stood down the day before," she said. "Roke is stood down and he asks the next man. And the next man is stood down and he asks the next. It goes down a line. It is a line, Adrian. It is not a company. It is a *line*, and the man at the top of it is stood down tomorrow and asks the man below him, and if the man below him cannot come then the man below *him* is asked, and if nobody can come then the thing does not happen, and that is the day it does not happen."

"That is a chain of command."

"That is a chain of *asking*," said Tamsin, "and the difference took me eleven years to be able to say out loud to a man from another world, and I am saying it to you now because you are going to get it wrong, and because you have a thing in you that thinks a man who asks for help is a man with a chain and therefore a gate." She put the strap end in her own hand and tied it. "Ask. That is all of it. It is the smallest word in my language and it is the one that does the most work."

Dante spent Friday morning in the yard with Renna Ord and came back in the afternoon with a list and a headache and a completely reorganised view of himself.

It was not a list of people. It was a list of *jobs*, eleven of them, each with a name against it and a rotation against the name, and the whole of it fit on one side of a sheet of paper, and Renna Ord had drawn the whole thing herself in about four minutes and had been slightly offended that he had wanted to do it first.

"So the food is not a problem," he said. "The food was never the problem. The problem is I have been buying flour off the market at a price and there are three men in this city who would have delivered it for a shift."

"What shift."

"Any shift," said Dante. "Renna. Any shift. You do not pay for flour with money. You pay for flour with a man on a wall for four hours, and then the flour is free, and then you do not owe anybody anything you cannot do, because what you owe is the wall."

"Four hundred measures of flour already in the loft," said Noor, without looking up. "Since Tuesday. Paid for. Legally. Through Sesh."

"Then somebody has been *over*-paying," said Renna Ord.

Noor Haddad found it on Friday at about eleven, in the loft, with a slate, and it took her two hours and it was the single most useful thing anybody did that week.

She did not start with the money. She started with the *week*, because she was a baker and a baker counts a week. She had a sheet with ruled columns — what we have, what it is for, who it belongs to, who may use it, who is owed — and she had been running it since Tuesday night, and on Friday she added a column, which was *who gave it to us and what they expect*.

It is a bad column. It is the column that a person does not add in the first week, because adding it in the first week makes the first week look like a series of transactions, and nobody who has just come out of a wall wants their first week in a new world to be a series of transactions. Noor added it anyway, on Friday, in her own hand, with a small square round it.

"Nine days," she said. "Nine deliveries. Bread, on the first, from Sesh, forty-one loaves. Vegetables, the first, from Sesh. The same since. Salt, on the fourth, from the market's common box, entered by me, with the box's own mark against it. The same again every day since." She put the slate down. "And the *flour*. Which is not Sesh's. The flour is out of the common box, and the common box is fed by a levy on every stall in the market, and the levy is for widows, and the box is administered by a rota of nine women of whom Sesh is one, and I have been eating out of the widows' box for nine days and I did not know, because Sesh Brack carried it up the stairs at six in the morning with the other breakfast, every morning, in a cloth, so that nobody would see."

Nobody said anything.

"So it is a claim," Noor said. "Not a small one. Sesh is on the box rota. Sesh controls nine women's names. And she has been feeding us out of it, which means that for nine days a woman in this market has been taking money that goes to widows and putting it in a cloth and carrying it up a stair, and if anybody ever wanted to say the six have been living off the dead of this city, they would not have to make it up."

"Sesh knew," said Tamsin.

"Sesh knew. Sesh has known the whole time. And here is the part that I cannot make fit in my head." Noor turned the slate round. "I went and asked her. I made her say it out loud, in front of Sesh's cousin, because that is how we do it now. And she said: *yes, and I would do it again, and that is exactly why you should know about it.* And she was right, and she was also saying something else, and the something else is that if I had found it out in a month instead of in nine days, then in a month I would have had a reason to distrust her. And she would have had a reason to be ashamed. And I would have had a number in a column and no person."

"Sesh Brack is the only person in this city who has not tried to explain herself to me once," Adrian said.

"She is a market woman," said Noor. "She has been doing this since before you were born. She is going to keep doing it and the only thing I can do is be in the column and be honest in it. So it goes in. Sesh Brack, market's common box, through the box rota, unrecorded, nine days, and I have written *unrecorded* in the column and *she chose to be found* in the second one, and I have read both lines out to her and she has agreed to them."

"And what do we owe her?"

"Whatever she tells us we owe her," said Noor. "That is the whole trick, and it took me two hours and it is worth nine years. You do not get to decide what somebody's kindness cost. You ask them. Then it goes in a column and it is a debt and not a rumour."

Jonah Pike went up the hill to the Brass Measure on Friday morning and came back at four in the afternoon with a price, and the price was the problem.

The Brass Measure is not a villain. It is a guild of forty-one metalworkers and factors who sell iron, brass, tin, rope, good steel, and honest advice, and who have a legitimate reason to want an arrangement and a better one to be in the only arrangement.

"There is a woman there called **Ottavia Reek**," Jonah said, in the yard, with his coat over his arm, and looking at Adrian in a way that meant he had already decided to do this and was going to report rather than ask. "She is the second of the Measure. She has offered a full set of lock irons, forty elms of bar iron, twenty of bar, six weeks' rope, a set of shoring oak, and delivery to the lock yard, for the season. Free of charge."

Silence.

"In exchange for what," said Owen.

"Priority. On every working at the lock, from this season forward, the Measure's men go first. And their names are on the ledger beside ours."

"And that is a good price," said Jonah, and he said it flatly and without any embarrassment at all, which made it very much worse. "It is *extraordinary*. It is roughly what a good price would be if we had money, and we do not have money, and it cost them about four hundred measures' worth of iron, and I have signed nothing and I am telling you before I do, which is the only reason I am still talking to you in this yard instead of having it in my pocket."

"Then do not do it," said Priya.

"Look at the numbers—"

"Jonah." Priya had the slate in her hand and she had been quiet all afternoon, which for Priya was a weather system. "I have got the numbers. I have got them because I wrote them down, which is the only reason I am worth anything. And I am going to read them back to you. Sixty measures of bread is what this group eats in a week. We are six and we are living in a room we do not own, on a road where the man who owns the freehold has not been asked, and you have just gone upstairs and made the only guild in this city the person who decides who stands on a wall. And the price is that the price is *good*."

"Everything is expensive and I got us the cheap—"

"That is exactly what I am saying," said Priya. "You got us the cheap one. In nine years of watching people get the cheap one, the cheap one has never once been the cheap one. It has always been the one that is cheapest if you only count money, and the man who is sitting in the room in nine years is the man who has the cheapest deal, and he is going to be somebody, and it is going to be a thing he did for us."

Jonah Pike stood in a market yard in a good coat and looked at a twenty-four-year-old with a slate and did not say anything for about ten seconds.

"That is a very good argument," he said, "and it is also a professional argument, and I want you to know that I noticed you made it, and that I am going to go upstairs on Monday and refuse it, and that I am going to do it slowly and in front of people so that the Measure hears it from me and not from a girl with a slate."

"Do not call me a girl with a slate in front of me again," Priya said, "and do not build a refusal speech. Refuse it in one sentence, in the yard, and put the sentence in Priya's log with your name on it, and then never bring it up again. A speech is a thing you can be proud of later."

Owen Park went to see a woman about a freehold on Friday at eleven in the morning, because Owen's whole life was securing rooms, and on Friday it finally occurred to him that the six of them had a problem he could solve with money he did not have and a manner nobody else in this room had.

The building the loft is the top of belonged to a woman called **Delya Crane**, thirty-four, a widow with a nine-year-old son called Tam, who lived in the two rooms over the chandlery with a window that looked at the lock. She had been trying to sell the freehold for nine years because the roof was bad and there was a boy to feed, and Sesh Brack had held the ground floor of it on a lease for eleven years and had been subletting the two upper rooms to whoever she chose since before Owen Park was born. Owen had worked out the ownership in about nine minutes, on Tuesday, standing in a market, and had told nobody, and had gone up the hill on Friday.

Owen did it properly. That was the thing about Owen that made him impossible. He put on the good shirt, and he went in the front door, and he waited four minutes for the door to be opened, and he did not mention the wall, or the gate, or the six, or the flood. He asked about the roof, and about the boy, and about the water, and he did the thing he is extremely good at, which was to make a person feel that the next four minutes were about them.

Delya Crane listened to all of it.

At the end of it she said, "You want to buy it."

"I want to buy it for six people," Owen said, "and I have to take the title in my own name because none of the rest of us have any standing here, which I am aware is the reason you should say no to me before I finish the sentence."

She looked at him for a moment, and then she stood up and went to the window and called for her son, and Tam came out of the back room eating something, nine years old, with a shirt on backwards, and she said, in the local language, in a completely level voice, "This is Mister Park. He is going to buy the building from me. Say hello to Mister Park, Tam, and then go downstairs and stay there until I come down."

"Tam," she said, to Owen, "is going to be fourteen in this house. And in about four years he is going to be eighteen, and he is going to want to know why the family sold the building above the chandlery to six people who came out of a wall, and he is going to ask his mother, and his mother is going to have to answer him, and I am telling you now, in advance, what my answer is going to be."

"I understand."

"I do not think you do." She had not raised her voice once, and she was not going to. "I am not refusing you. I want you to hear that clearly, because I have watched what happens to a man who is refused by a woman in a doorway and decides she was difficult. I am not refusing you because I do not think you are a thief. I am refusing you because of the middle room.

"There is a woman called Sesh Brack who has been the tenant of my ground floor for eleven years and who has been putting six of you in my upper rooms since the day you came out of the wall, and she has not asked me, because she is not a fool. And if I sell this building to you, then in about a month Sesh Brack has nowhere to be, and you have a landlord, and a boy of nine has a landlord, and a woman who has fed six people out of the widows' box for nine days without telling anybody has been quietly removed from a piece of work she has been doing for eleven years. And the price of the removal is not mine and it is not yours. It will be hers, and she will pay it, and she will say nothing."

Owen Park opened his mouth and shut it.

"And there is the other thing," she said, "which is the thing I would have said if there had not been a middle room. When the six of you cannot pay — and you cannot pay, there is no money in the world you came from, and I have worked that out from what you said about a door that has no road out of it — then it is not six people who have nowhere to live. It is six people who *own a building they have not paid for*, and the child in this house is living in the property of people who are in arrears, and he will be told about that. Not by me. By a woman at a market who has been waiting nine days to be able to say something about the six."

"I would not—"

"You would not care," said Delya Crane. "You would care enormously, for about nine days, and then there would be a flood, and a gate, and a thing in a wall, and one of you would go out to the wall, and you would come home in the evening and not have thought about my son's roof. And that is not a failure of character. That is what a person is."

Owen Park stood in a small front room over a chandlery with his good shirt on and found that he had run out of directions again, for the second time in a week, and that the second time was worse, because the first time a market woman had named the cost of each direction and this time the cost was a nine-year-old's childhood.

"You are right," he said.

"I am not right. I am *first*. There is a difference and it is not a good one and I do not have a better offer." She folded her arms. "What I have is a counter. I will not sell. But I will put a twelve-month lease on the upper room in your name — yours, Park, in your own hand, for four people — and I will do it at the same rent I have been refusing, and the reason I will do it is that in twelve months either you have found a road home or you have not, and if you have not then you will be six people with a legal right to be in this building, which is worth more to you than a deed and worth nothing at all to me."

"That is very generous."

"It is a landlord's calculation," said Delya Crane. "You are the only one of the six who came here to tell me what he was going to do before he did it, and I would rather have you on my stairs than the other one who tells me afterwards. And go up the street on your way down and ask Sesh whether the roof over her head is the one I have been trying to sell since you were born, because she will know, and because a man who has bought a building he did not know he was living in has not bought a building. He has bought a lie with a chimney on it."

He came back to the loft at two in the afternoon and put the whole thing on the table in about ninety seconds, and the dead phones and the nine-year-old and the woman with the middle room and all of it, and then stopped, and then added the part he had not expected to have to say, which was the name Sesh Brack, because the two conditions attached to the lease were that he tell her in the landlord's kitchen with her in the room, and that he go up the street and ask her whether the roof over her head was the one being sold.

And then Adrian, who had been holding his notebook, and who had been — as everybody at that table understood and nobody said — waiting for this to be a good moment, did the thing he had been wanting to do for nine days, which was to take a role off somebody.

"No," he said.

Jonah said, "I do not think you get to—"

"You do not think I get to say what the six of you do," Adrian said, "and you are right, and I am not going to. I am going to say something smaller and worse." He put the notebook on the table, open, at the page where he had written the six names on Wednesday in the rain. "I have spent nine days assigning work. I made the medical list. I handed the inventory to Noor. I worked out the repairs and put Dante on them. I have not assigned any of it out loud. I have arranged it the way you arrange a system, and every single one of you has let me, because for nine days I have been the only person in the room who knew what was happening, and that is exactly the thing I have been frightened of since Tuesday and I have been doing it anyway, in a small way, six times."

He looked round the table. Priya with a slate. Noor with a second slate. Dante with his arm newly in a strap. Jonah with a good coat and no deal. Owen with a lease offer he has not signed and two conditions he has not yet met, and his hands open on the table. Mina, who had gone to a room by the wharf on Monday and had been wrong in it.

"None of that was mine to assign. I am not the person who assigns things. I have never once in nine days asked a single one of you whether you wanted the thing I had decided you were going to do, and I have been extremely pleased with myself about it, and the person I am describing is a man I used to work with."

Nine days ago, in the loft, Mina had put a rule on the wall and Adrian had noticed himself doing it. On Friday, sitting on the end of somebody else's cot with his arm in a strap and a landlord's kindness in his ears, Dante Kuo said, "Yeah," and it was the closest he had come to an apology in nine days and he did not make it, and Adrian let it stand.

"Who assigned you the arm," Adrian said.

"You did," said Dante. "You asked Renna for a name on Tuesday."

"I asked Renna for a name on Tuesday," Adrian agreed. "And I have not asked anybody anything since. So: I am not the person who assigns them. I am the person who is at the counter when somebody comes to the door, and I do not get to decide what they came for. That is the seat, and it is a seat, and Nell Bray told me on Friday morning that a seat is a place to be angry at, and I have been treating it for six days as a place to *be useful from*, which is how a chair works."

---

The thing that happened at four o'clock on Friday afternoon happened because Hesper Saye came up the stair.

She had come up the stair twice that week, and both times she had come up with a question that was about consent, and both times the question had been addressed to nobody in particular and therefore to the room, and both times the room had found out afterwards that it had been addressed to Adrian.

She had a bowl in her hand and a piece of cloth and she sat down on the end of the cot by Owen without asking, which was, Adrian understood by then, the local way of saying *this is my room now, medically*.

"Nine days," she said, turning Owen's head by the ear with two fingers. "The wound has not closed and there is clean skin over it. That is a promise with no witness on it. Somebody at that wall made a thing and then the thing did not go all the way through and there is a thing in your head that is still halfway into a promise." She let go. "It will be fine. I want that said. It will be fine and I will not charge you, and in nine years you will have a mark and it will be a mark and not a wound."

"Thank you," said Owen, with his eyes shut.

Then she said, in English, which she had learned from Owen over about forty minutes on Wednesday and which she was extremely pleased with herself about, and which is why nobody could stop her:

"And there is a rule on your wall. The fourth one. *Tell Mina about every injury. Every one. Including the ones that look survivable.* Who gave you the right to make that rule for six people?"

"Because they—"

"No." She held a finger up. "No. I am asking a different question and I have asked it of four people this week and three of them gave me an answer about the rule and I do not want the answer about the rule. I am asking what happens when one of them does not want to tell her."

The room went quiet in a way that Adrian had only heard once before, on Wednesday, in a chapel.

He opened his mouth twice.

The first time he was going to say *because if it is survivable and nobody says it, then* — and he stopped, because that was an argument about outcomes and she had asked a question about a person, and he could feel, with total clarity, that the two were not the same thing and that he did not have the sentence that bridged them in any language he spoke.

The second time he was going to say *the rule can be changed by the six* — and he stopped, because he had not asked the six, and because if he asked them now, in a room with a healer sitting on the end of somebody's cot, then a rule he had made in order to get authority would have been ratified by frightened people under a healer's authority, and he could not think of a worse sentence to have built.

"Adrian," said Tamsin.

"I do not know," Adrian said.

Hesper Saye looked at him for a second.

"You are the man in the book," she said.

"I am the man in the book and I do not know," he said, "and I have not known since Tuesday and I did not know that I did not know until you asked it, and I want to say the second part out loud because the first part alone is a thing a man says to be polite."

"No," said Saye, with something that was not unkind. "It is not polite. It is the first true thing anybody has said in this room about that wall, and I have been in a room with men telling each other they were careful for thirty-four years." She got up and picked up her bowl. "You have made a rule and nobody agreed to it, and the rule is probably a good rule, and it is going to be obeyed for about a month, and then somebody is going to be hurt and not say so, and you will find out about it too late, and the thing that will have caused that is not that you made the rule. It is that you made the rule about *them*."

---

He did not fix it that night. He wrote the question out on the back of a flour merchant's invoice, in his own terrible hand, and got Tamsin to put it into the local language, and on Saturday morning he put it on the wall under the four rules in a small hand, with nothing above it and nothing below it, and the six of them, and Sesh Brack, who came up at eight to see it, read it standing.

*Hesper Saye, Friday, in this room, to the man in the book: who gave you the right to make the fourth rule for six people, and what happens when one of them does not want to tell? The man in the book does not know. He has written that here so that the question is on the wall and not only in a room, because a question that is only in a room gets answered by whoever is in the room.*

And below it, in Priya's hand, and dated:

*Answered Saturday, at the low room by the wharf, by Owen Park: then she finds out anyway, and he does not get to be the reason she didn't. — O.P.*

And below that, on Monday, in Mina Okoye's flat capitals, a line nobody had asked for:

*And if I am the one who finds out, then I am also the person who decides whether it was survivable. Ask somebody else. — M.O.*
