# Chapter 0031 — What a Date Is For

It is the seventh day of the new month and the month began four days ago, and a man in a good coat told a boy of nine on a step on the twentieth day of the flood that he had to be in this city today, and told nobody else, and had said the same thing to four men in a market since without one of them putting it next to the other one.

Adrian had it on a page in his own handwriting and it had been on the page for twelve days, and on the morning of the seventh day of the new month he got up in the room above the river market at about the fifth hour and could not make his hand do anything with it, which was new, and which he noted, because he had begun to note the days his hands would not work, the way you note the days a river is not rising.

Nora's name was on the last page of the notebook. He had not turned to it in twenty-eight days, and he was aware of the twenty-eight the way a man is aware of a step he has stopped using.

---

The binding in the yard is said twice a day, in the open, in any weather, by whoever is nearest, and it has no name at the top of it. By the thirty-second day of this flood about forty people in Saltwake can say the five words in the right order and about nine of them can say the fifth one properly: a mason of fifty called Peth Lomas, a girl of sixteen called Kirsty Lund who was born in another hemisphere, and eight others. The ninth person to say it on the morning of the seventh day of the new month was a man from another world who was the holder of the pull of an instrument that could not be used, and who was within four hundred yards of that yard, and therefore, by the second item of the cost of his own seat, was the nearest.

Owen Park was at the bottom of the stair in the dark with a lamp he had not lit, and at some point in the last hour he had shifted the trapdoor cover off the cellar of that building with his right hand, which is a two-inch oak board on a frame and which weighs about thirty pounds wet, and which he would not have thought twice about a month ago, and which nobody had seen him do. Owen Park was at the bottom of the stair in the dark with a lamp he had not lit, and had said that a woman came up the market at eleven the previous night and asked at three doors whether the man out of the wall was still here, and two of the three said yes and one said no on purpose, because her husband is a Hearthguard and he does not want a house with a man in it on a night when the market has a number on a board. She did not say whose wife and he did not ask, because he had been told about asking.

He said the words badly the first time. The fifth word is the hard one, because it is not in the oath; it is the keeper's word, it belongs to the arch and to the family that has kept it, and a man who has learned it from a woman with a bowl in a room by the wharf cannot put it where a keeper puts it.

"You put it in the wrong place," said Nyla Ferrow, from the archway, without moving. "It goes in the middle, and it goes in the middle because it was put in the middle about a hundred and forty years after the four words by a family that had no business putting anything in the middle, and we have kept it there ever since, and that is the whole of what this yard is. You will say it in the middle about four times a day for the rest of whatever this is, and by the fiftieth time you will stop hearing where you put it. You are late. He is waiting."

---

Ivenn Marrow was standing under the chapel with a cloth over his shoulder and nothing in his hands, which Adrian noticed, because a man who comes to a keeper's yard usually brings something.

"Seventh day of the new month," he said. "You have had that written down for twelve days and I have watched you not ask about it, and I have decided to save you the trouble, which is uncharacteristic of me and which I am going to be insufferable about for a week."

"You told a boy of nine."

"I told a boy of nine because a nine-year-old tells his mother and a man of forty-one is told nothing. I have been in this city eight weeks and I have learned exactly one thing about it, which is that information travels downward and not across, and that a child is the only person in a city who will say a thing to a stranger and repeat it accurately." He put the cloth over his arm. "That was tradecraft. It worked. I am sorry about the method."

"What do you want."

"What I asked for on the thirtieth. Not in this yard."

"You said it in this yard. In chalk, in daylight, in front of thirty people."

"I said it in chalk. I am asking you in a room." He looked at the board on the chapel door, which is four feet high and which Nyla Ferrow washes every Sunday morning. "Do you know the difference between those two things?" Adrian said he had a difference and had not worked out what it was. "Neither have I, and I have had two days and I would like you to have them before I say it, because I am not going to be able to be this useful to you if you are going to be suspicious at me for asking." He turned and looked at the yard, which had about thirty people in it and would have two hundred by six. "I will say one thing out loud, in front of the keeper, and then I will stop. The stopping is the last honest thing I have done in this city and I want it marked."

He said it in the local language, correctly, which Adrian had stopped being impressed by about four weeks ago and had started being frightened of instead.

"In this yard, when a man in a good coat asks a man with no rights in this city to put a sentence in his own hand on this keeper's wall, and the man with no rights in this city says no in front of thirty people, then the thirty people have a sentence, and the sentence is *the man in the book would not say why*. And a sentence in thirty mouths is a shorter sentence than the true one, and it is entered in nobody's book, and in nine days somebody can use it." He said it to Adrian, not to the yard. "So I am not going to let you refuse me in a yard. And you are not going to be able to, either, and we both know that, and the reason neither of us can is that you cannot say the word *no* to a man who has been useful to you in front of a keeper, and I have made myself useful to you on purpose, and I have been doing it for eight weeks, and you have known about it since the second night."

The yard went on being a yard. Somewhere above the arch a shutter came down on its hinge.

"That is the most frightening thing anybody has said to me in this city," Adrian said, in English, and then had to say it again in the local language, badly, because it needed saying twice.

"It is the first thing I have said to you that I have not said to somebody else first," said Ivenn Marrow. "Write it down that it was only once."

---

He wrote it.

That was the whole of the seventh day of the new month between the seventh hour and about the ninth, and it took the whole morning, and he wrote it four times on four sheets of flour-merchant's paper because the first three were in his own voice and the fourth was not.

He wrote it in the loft above the market at a table with Tamsin Quill at the other end of it and not helping, which on this morning was the only reason he got it done. He did not have the notebook out. It was in the bag and the bag was in the corner, and this was the first time in a month he had deliberately not had it on a table, and he did not examine why, and it took him four days.

The reason he is not giving in a room, and the reason he gave in the hall four days ago, are not the same sentence, and the difference between them is the entire content of what a man in a good coat has been asking him for since the thirtieth.

What he said in the hall, in front of about three hundred and ten people, and which is in a book with two refusal dates on it, was this and only this:

*I am staying Unbound. I have no workway, I am not going to have a workway, and the reason is not that it is dangerous. It is that a man in this city who has taken a workway is a man who can be asked where he is going, and I am a man nobody in this city is owed anything by, and I have spent a month finding that out, and the whole of what a workway would have given me is the ability to be asked, and I have got that in another way, and I got it from a woman who will not name me, in a yard, where anybody can stand in it and watch it happen.*

It is a good sentence. He has read it back to himself about forty times. It is in a clerk's book and it will be in a roll in a wooden case in Aurel in a fortnight, and a Crown scholar will read it, and it will hold, because it is a sentence about a system and systems do not change their minds in front of you.

The true one is this.

*I refused it because it worked.*

He wrote it out on the fourth sheet at about half past eight in the morning and then sat looking at it, and it is in the book in his own words, and there are two hundred and nine people who have read it.

---

Here it is, entire, as it is on the second board of the yard of the lock chapel, in a hand that is not a clerk's, under a line of chalk by a man in a good coat and over a name in a mason's:

> **I refused the workway because it worked.**
>
> **I liked saying no. In a room, in front of people, in the rain, with witnesses. It is a good way to be a person in a city where I have no rights and everybody has decided I am the interesting one, and for a month every time I said it I got a little safer and a little smaller, and I counted, and I have written in a notebook that I counted.**
>
> **And then I said it in a hall to three hundred people, and three hundred people believed a sentence about a system, and they were right to, and the sentence was true, and I have watched since that day what a true sentence does when a man who is good at sentences hands it round.**
>
> **A workway is a duty somebody else was doing. I did not want a duty. I wanted to be the man who said the duty was somebody else's problem, and I said it well, and I said it in the rain, and I got four men out of a gate crew that morning and it has cost that crew an hour a day every day since, and nobody in this city has ever thanked me for it, and I noticed the not-being-thanked and I went on doing it.**
>
> **I do not want to be a reason that works. That is all of it. A reason that works is a rule, and a rule is a thing that stands there after the man who made it has gone, and in about nine months there will be a man in a yard who says the sentence I said in the hall because it works, and he will not have read this board, and I will have built it.**
>
> **So: I refused it because I wanted to be a person in a room, and I am not that, and that is the true reason, and it is worse than the one in the book, and I asked for it to be the true one and I would like it to be read every morning by the people who use the lock, and not by me.**

There is no punctuation trouble. There is no sentence in it that a person in this city could not follow, and Tamsin Quill read it twice in the local language, slowly, before she would let him take it to the yard, and refused three words in the first reading and took one of the three back.

"You cannot write *the interesting one*," she said. "Nobody in this city thinks that. You think it. It is a word from a place where a hundred and ten thousand people are paid to decide what is interesting, and it has come out of your mouth and it is the only sentence on that board that belongs to another world."

"What should it be."

"*The one everybody is looking at.*" She turned the sheet round. "That is what it is here. It is not interesting. It is that in a town of eleven hundred people in a flood month, there is one man the whole town can look at, and he is a stranger, and a stranger is a thing you can look at for a month. Write that, and it is true, and it is worse."

He wrote that.

---

The keeper's board is not a roll and both women who keep it have said so out loud in a yard.

"It is not the Concord's book and it does not go to Aurel in a case," said Nyla Ferrow, at about the ninth hour, with the chalk in her hand. "It is a board on a door. It is read by whoever comes up this yard. It is washed every Sunday. And if a thing on it stops being true it comes off on a Sunday, in daylight, with somebody standing here when it comes off. If it stays true, a Crown clerk will come to this door one day and stand in my yard and write it down, and there is nothing in it he may not write down, because I have never had a secret on this board in thirty years and I am not starting. I will keep it while it is true. I do not promise it for long, and how long it is, is as long as it takes for somebody to be angry about it." She put the chalk in her apron. "You have written the true reason on my mother's ground. I am going to be the woman who kept that. If you had written the fair one I would have been the woman who kept a thing that made a man look better than he is, and my mother's mother would have taken the board down herself."

And then Ivenn Marrow asked to have it read, and it was read, by a man out of the yard who was not asked and volunteered — Peth Lomas, fifty, a mason, who had learned the fifth word on the twenty-first day and who had never in his life spoken in a yard and who read the whole of it in the local language in a flat voice with a bar in his hand, and who took about four minutes, and at the end of it said one sentence that was not the board.

"Twenty-one days ago you learned the fifth word and you put it at the end," Peth Lomas said. "I have watched you put it at the end four times a day for three weeks. I did not say anything. I am saying now: I said the one to Renna Ord in the yard in the first week and it worked and there is no name on this board and there should be, and if the mason who learned it is going to be angry about it, then put me on it and let the man in the good coat read that."

It took about eleven seconds to write and it is under the fourth line, and it is in Peth Lomas's own hand, and the first reader of the sentence on the thirty-first day of the next month, when it comes back round, is a mason of fifty with a bar.

---

Peth Lammay's boat went at the second hour of the afternoon with the light behind it and the wind wrong, and Adrian got four hours on the Reach road and four hours in a boat and then four hours in a room at Verrow, and the four hours in the room is the part he has written about most.

She is twenty-four and she carries the Crown's second-table mail on the upper river and the Reach run, and she has carried it for eleven years, and she went out of Saltwake on the seventh day of the new month with a leather case that did not have the Crown's seal on it, in her own hand, ahead of a case that does, and she put the case down on the table of the meeting-ground of the Reed Reach in front of about thirty people and said the sentence she had said in a hall in Saltwake, which is that the people a document is about ought to know it exists before a clerk does, and then she sat down.

The meeting-ground is a room over a boat-yard at Verrow with a stove in it, a long table, and no chair at the head of it, because a chair at the head of it is an office, and a man in a good coat explained that to four people on the road down, and it is the only time in eight weeks that Ivenn Marrow has cost Adrian anything and it was four minutes of a man's time.

They read it in four hours.

It is the account of the other side, taken over six hours in a salt store in Saltwake on the twenty-ninth day, and it is eleven people who came out of the ground under a chapel floor, and the entry in the Concord's book says that the making of it and the content of it are witnessed by nobody in this world, and the four disagreements are in it with the names on and nothing reconciled.

Adrian read none of it out. He had four hours in a room in the Reach and no notebook, and he was not asked to speak, and what he did for four hours was sit at a long table with about thirty people around it and be a man with nothing in his hands.

He has written about it three times and all three times the same sentence: the thing he was worst at was not the language. It was that he was in a room with a document that had cost a man eleven seconds in a seam in a wall, and every twenty minutes somebody asked a question and every twenty minutes he had a place where his answer went, and twice he opened his mouth with a design sentence in it — a sentence about what the thing was *for* — and closed it again, because Priya Shah was not in the room and there was nobody here to stop a word and no form on a wall.

The second time it happened, Ilda Marsh, who is seventy and who has refused twice and who is the reason a fourth line exists, put her hand flat on the table and said the sentence that ended it for the rest of the evening.

"You have been holding something," she said, in the local language, and Tamsin did not translate it because everybody at that table knew four words. "You have had a shape in your face since the second hour and it is the same shape you had in a yard in Saltwake on the eleventh day, and I have watched a lot of men hold things."

"I am holding a sentence."

"Then say it out loud and let it be wrong in front of us, and somebody here will stop you. I have been stopping men of your age since before the Crown put a seal on this river."

And Adrian Vale said, out loud, in a room over a boat-yard at Verrow with about thirty people in it, the sentence he had been holding since the second hour, and it was this:

"A place that people already agree on, used already, for another reason, is the only kind of place a thing like that can stand up in, and there are eleven of them in this room who know what that is, and the reason this room has been strange for four hours is that all eleven of you have spent your lives agreeing on places, and so have I, for nine years, professionally, and I have never once in my life had to say out loud that I was only good at it in order to get something done."

Nobody stopped him. He had wanted to be stopped.

"That is not a sentence about the document," said Ilda Marsh. "It is a sentence about you, and the room has now had four hours of you, and about nine people at this table did not come for you." She put her hand back on the table. "Sit down. Read the part about the four openings. I have a question and it is not about a place, and I have been waiting three days to ask it, and I want it in a book this time and not in a market."

---

The question was asked at about the seventh hour of the evening, and it was asked by Petra Oake, who is thirty-one and who does not speak for the assembly and who has been to three meetings in this city and has made a nuisance of herself in all of them.

The account says: a room, no window, no door anybody found in four minutes, four openings in one long wall at different heights, the fourth already open, two people out of the third, the rest divided, a sound like a page turning every forty seconds, and a smell of wet stone in a room with no stone in it that one man of thirty-one had smelled before.

"Four openings in a wall," Petra Oake said, in the local language, slowly, twice, and Tamsin translated it both ways because Tamsin always translates both ways, and it took eleven minutes. "And what the meeting-ground has done for three days with this page is ask what is in the room, how big it is, how the four openings are set out, and whether the paper is a list of things that can be done, which I am told it is, and a man of thirty-one has measured the sills."

She put her own sheet of the copy down on the table. She had been keeping it folded in her coat all evening, which meant she had not come to be surprised by a document. She had come to ask one question, and she had read the page four times to find the place to ask it.

"Nobody has asked this," she said, "and I have asked it of two other people in this Reach and they have both looked at me as though I had come in wet. So I will ask it here, and I want it written down, and I want the man who wrote the page to hear it in his own language and answer it or say that he cannot."

"What is the question," said Adrian, in the local language, and got it right.

"Is there a person on the other side of it."

The room did not make a sound.

"Not is there a door," Petra Oake said, and she was not talking to Adrian any more, she was talking to the table, and Adrian has written that down as the moment he understood that she had asked it in a market in Nine Elms eight days earlier and had been told that a woman in a boat-coat had answered her already, and had not believed the answer. "Not is the room open, is there a room. Not is it a hole, is there a hole. **Is there a man on the other side of that floor.** Because every single thing anybody in this room has said about it in three days has assumed that if it is anything at all then it is somewhere to send somebody to fetch from, and there is not one word in that page about there being anybody to fetch."

And then she said the part that has been in the Reach since the twenty-second day of the flood and is in the book, and Adrian entered it in his own notebook on the boat coming back, in the dark, in about nine seconds, with a pencil and a bad hand, and this is the entry, and it is the last thing written in that notebook for a long time:

> *Is there a person on the other side of it.*
>
> *Asked by Petra Oake of Nine Elms, at the meeting-ground at Verrow, at the seventh hour of the evening of the seventh day of the new month, in front of about thirty people, having asked it of two other people in this Reach already and been answered by neither.*
>
> *The question the Reach has been building since the twenty-second day, asked for the first time about a person, out loud, in a room, by a woman who does not speak for the assembly, and entered by a carrier into her own hand in the margin of her own copy, and read back twice and translated twice. It is not in the Concord's book. It is on the Reach side of the river, which is the correct place for it and is the first time in a month I have understood why.*
>
> *I did not answer it. I want that entered too. Three of my four answers are lies and the fourth is that the eleven were in a room for four seconds and came back with a piece of paper, and there was nobody else in the room, and I know that, and I know it because a man of thirty-one smelled wet stone in it and eleven of them disagreed about the size of it, and not one of them has ever said the word *other* about it except in the last three days, and every one of them who has said it has been asked what the word means.*
>
> *The fourth answer is the one I cannot rule out and the one I am most afraid of, and it is this: that there is somebody in there, and that we know it, and that a room with a man in it is a room somebody has been in twice.*
>
> *Ask the people who maintain the gate. Ask the keeper. Ask the woman who keeps a count.*
>
> *And on Tuesday, on the thirty-sixth day, I am going to Nine Elms, and Petra Oake is of Nine Elms, and the question is going to come at me again in a yard, and I am going to have to say a whole sentence to a woman of seventy about a man in a wall, and I have not got one, and the reason the book does not have one either is that everybody who has written anything about this in a month has written about a place.*

---

Renna Ord came up the stair behind him at a quarter past six that evening with a barrow, read the board over his shoulder, and read it again with her head on one side, because the word *chair* was not on it and she was looking for a word she knew.

"A man refused a thing because it worked," she said. "I have watched about nine hundred men in this city refuse a thing because it worked, and about nine of them wrote down why, and one of them is a mason who has put his own name under yours, which is the part of it I would not have believed this morning."

"It is not for me. It is on a board."

"It is in a market by Tuesday. And you cannot tell from the sentence whether a man wrote a true thing to be safe or to be brave. You tell from what he is frightened of afterwards." She got the barrow up onto the sill. "Which is why I am telling you in a gallery, with nobody in it, and not in a yard in the morning. In about nine days a man in a good coat will use that board and will not have to do anything else, because a rule that is a man's own true reason is the hardest kind there is to argue with. The only thing that beats it is a longer true reason from another person, and there is one of those in a room in this city about eleven days a month and she has tweezers."

---

He went down the two hundred steps at about half past ten that night because the tide was at the top of the thirty-third and because a man with a rule was going to be there at one in the morning to put it in a crack, and because he had been doing that since the second night and had got about four hours of sleep in three nights and did not intend to stop on the thirty-second of a flood.

The lower gallery of the Lock of Salt is a barrel-vaulted chamber above the sill with three arched bays, a central channel, and a black timber and iron slab across the near end that the dock crew holds under during repairs. Four marks in chalk on the second bay wall were made by a docker on the twenty-fifth day so that a number would still be in a month, and the number itself is written over the second bay in a hand that is not a clerk's and will be there in the winter.

The pale edge of the join in the gate is a line of hard light in a dark wall where new stone has been let into old stone badly, and it is where the arrivals came out on the first morning of this flood, and it has been measured every night since the second night by three chalked marks and a folding steel rule, and the readings are on the wall of the second bay in his own hand because he is the only man in this city who writes in that hand.

Seven and seven-eighths on the eighteenth day.

**Eight and five-eighths** on the thirty-third.

Three-quarters of an inch in fifteen days, in one step, and the step fell on the twenty-fifth at the eleventh hour and eleven minutes, and a crack does not step, and the four nights since have been ordinary nights and the number has not moved one sixteenth of an inch on any of them, and the number chalked on the wall of the second bay — nine, across, toward the near gate, in a docker's hand — is not this number and has never been this number, and he had been reading them as one thing for eight days, and on the thirtieth at a quarter past six he had told Renna Ord in this gallery that there would be about a quarter of an inch in it by Saturday morning.

He wrote that down. He wrote it in the gallery, in his own hand, on the back of a leaf, and the leaf is in the notebook, and it says:

> *Thirty-third day, the first hour. Eight and five-eighths. Three-quarters since the eighteenth, one step, on the twenty-fifth, and four ordinary nights with nothing in them.*
>
> *I told a docker at ten to six on the thirtieth that there would be about a quarter of an inch by Saturday morning, and there is three-quarters, and the three-quarters is exactly one step of three-quarters, and a step is a decision and a curve is a press, and I predicted a quarter because I was thinking of fifteen nights of ordinary work and I did not count the twenty-fifth, and the reason I did not count the twenty-fifth is that on the twenty-fifth I was four feet back from a gate with a rule in my hand and not in it, and I have been counting the nights that I stood there for, and not the one that mattered.*
>
> *Eleven readings would be a curve. This is twenty-one readings and the twentieth is where a number stops being a number and starts being an argument.*
>
> *Ask the people who maintain the gate. Ask the keeper. Ask the woman who keeps a count.*

And then he came up the stair at about a quarter to two, and Renna Ord was in the gallery with the chalk in her hand.

"You did it again," she said. "Forty-one nights in a row you have come up here at one and gone down at quarter to two."

"It's a rule."

"It's a habit, and a habit with a rule in it is how a man builds a thing that does not need him and then is angry when it does not." She put the chalk on the ledge. "And I have done the Saturday one."

"What Saturday one."

"You have had the tide table in your head for a week and I have watched you not say it out loud, and on Thursday I asked Vail for the board to be wiped and cleaned and the last column emptied against Saturday, which is standard, and this morning I came up at eleven and I wrote it in, because whoever holds the chalk that day writes it, and I held it, and I am a docker, and I do not have to be told what to write in a box by a man who has been measuring a wall for a month."

He looked at the board.

The last column has one word in it for every tide of the salt year and the word is *works* or it is blank, and the tide of the thirty-third is a Saturday and is a small one and is an ordinary one, and the box against the thirty-third had been empty since the twenty-eighth, and it was not empty now.

**works**

In a chalk hand that is not his and not a clerk's and not a good one, and the letters are uneven, and the *k* is pressed too hard.

"Forty men under the near gate twice this morning," Renna Ord said, "at their ordinary volume, out of the line and not together, because a woman in a felt hat made a docker promise it in a ledger eight days ago. And the tide was nothing and the work was nothing and the number in the second bay did not move while we were at it, and Hesk stood on the sill looking at the stone and not at the water, and at the top of the second stair he says to me, *it is not the tide*, and he has said that in four markets this month and I have entered every one and believed none of them, and I have believed this one, and I am a fool and I have had a week of it."

"You did not have to write it."

"It is not a job. It is a Saturday." She picked the chalk up and put it in her pocket. "You told a docker in a corridor two days ago that the man who writes the word in that box is the last man in this city who can decide anything, and he decides it with a stick of chalk on a wet morning, and nobody will ever ask him what he decided and he will not be able to tell you himself by the Tuesday."

"And can you tell me."

"Not today." She went past him up the stair. "Today I put a word in a box about a lock doing its ordinary work on an ordinary morning, and I did not think about it, and I did not have to, and it is going to turn out to be the most useful thing anybody has done in this city for a month, and I am not going to be told otherwise, and if anybody asks me on Tuesday what I decided I am going to say *nothing*, and it will be the truest answer anybody in this city has given anybody in a month."

The stair was dark behind her and the water was going out under the floor, and Adrian Vale stood in the lower gallery of the Lock of Salt at about two in the morning on the thirty-third day of the flood with a folding steel rule in his hand and a tide table above his head with a word in it that he had not written, and he wrote the number up on the second bay in his own hand underneath the docker's nine, and then went up two hundred steps and along the bank and through a market where one shutter was open and a woman behind it was doing something with flour that had nothing to do with him, and got into a room above the market at about half past three and lay down for two hours and a half and did not dream, which was the fourth night in a row.

Eight and five-eighths. A curve, and a decision, and a word in a box, and a lock keeping its own time in a flood month while a hundred and forty acres four miles away went on losing an hour a day and did not know it.

*works*.
