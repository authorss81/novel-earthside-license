# Chapter 0021 — Two Dates in One Yard

The first thing on the twentieth day of the flood was a hole in a rota, and Adrian found it before he had finished his tea, and he found it because Owen Park was not there at six.

That was the whole of it. That was the entire content of the morning. Owen had said the four words and the fifth at six on eight mornings, in the rain, with a mason correcting him, and on the twentieth morning at twenty past six there was a yard and there was rain and there was nobody in it, and the words were not said.

Nyla Ferrow was in the chapel, because the keeper sleeps in the chapel, and she came out with her board of lime and clay under her arm and a face that anybody in that yard could read from thirty feet.

"Where," she said.

"He is not here," said Adrian.

"He is nineteen minutes late. He is at the field. He has been at the field six mornings." She said it in the local language, and then, because she had decided some weeks ago that she would say the useful half of it in the strange tongue so that it could not be softened: "He goes at four and comes back at seven. You did not ask him. I want you to understand that I know you did not ask him, and that it has made no difference, and that I am saying both of those things in a yard at seven in the morning in the rain."

"He taught me the words on Friday," Adrian said. "He can say all five."

"He can say all five. He said them on eight mornings and there has never been a morning when he was not here and there is going to be a morning when he is ill, or he is across the river, or he is in a room with a woman who is bleeding. That morning is this morning and you have got about two minutes to do something about it and I am not going to tell you what, because if I tell you what then tomorrow you will come and ask me what to do on the second morning as well, and the day after that you will come and ask me, and in three weeks this yard will belong to a man with a notebook." She turned the board over. "Say them."

"I cannot say them properly."

"Say them badly. Say them in my mother's words with a foreign mouth, the way he does, and if the arch accepts a bad sentence then the arch is not doing what the arch is for, and I want to find that out this morning while there are three masons in this yard to tell me I am wrong."

Adrian Vale said the four words of the maintenance oath in a wet yard at seven in the morning, and then said the keeper's fifth word, which is not in the oath and has never been in the oath, and put his hand flat on the arch.

Nothing happened.

"Again," said Nyla Ferrow.

He said them again. And the third time, at about a third more slowly, with his hand on the arch, the arch agreed to be a doorway, and Adrian felt the same thing he had felt standing well back from a hole on the Saturday: the specific, unmistakable sensation of a room agreeing to be a room.

"Right," said the keeper. "Now write that on the board. Both of them. That Owen Park is not here, that you said it and it did not take, and that you said it again and it did. And then go and find out whether he is coming, and if he is not coming then find out who is nearest, and do not write a name at the top of anything."

---

It took him until that Sunday afternoon to understand that the second thing about the morning was worse than the first, and the second thing was this: that Vail had been right about the rota, and the rota could not exist, and he had to build one anyway.

He had gone to Sera Vail on the Sunday because she was the person in Saltwake who knew what a record was for, and he had gone with a question he thought was small.

He had a hole in a rota. The keeper's price was that the words be said twice a day in the open by whoever is nearest, and Vail had entered it as a labour with no name at the top, and Adrian had assumed — for about six hours, on the Saturday evening, in the dark, in a cold room, with a notebook — that what he needed was a list.

"A list," he said. "A month on a board in the yard. Five or six names, rotated, and the four witnesses signed at the bottom of it, and a line for the hour, and anybody can read it. And the cost of it goes in the column, one day a month off the gate crew, the same as the Narrow Mark's shift."

Sera Vail looked at him for slightly longer than was comfortable.

"Whose name is at the top of it?"

"Nobody's. I have not—"

"That is not the question I asked and you know it is not." She had the ledger open on the trestle in the archway, chained, with the ring through it, and she had a wet sleeve. "Tamsin Quill told me on Saturday evening that you have had a very good idea and that you would have it by Wednesday. I told her I would rather it did not happen on a Sunday when I have nothing else on. So let us have it on a Sunday."

"It is not a rota," Adrian said. "The rule is whoever is nearest. That is the keeper's rule and it is older than me and it is the whole point of it."

"Yes."

"Then a list is not — a list of names is the opposite of whoever is nearest."

"It is." Vail dipped the chalk and did not write anything with it. "And a list will be on that board by Wednesday, and it will be the correct thing, and in about six weeks every man in it will have learned to come at a quarter to six because his name is on the board at six, and by the third month it will not be the nearest person at all, it will be the man whose turn it is, and by the end of the season the yard will have a rota and the keeper will have lost the one sentence that makes it a keeper." She put the chalk down. "I have had this argument in this city four times in twenty-six years and I have won it four times and I have lost it twice, and both times I lost it by not being in the yard on the first morning."

---

So Adrian went and did the only thing available to a man who is not allowed to be at the top of anything, which was to make the rule survivable by widening the number of people who could be nearest.

Ottoline Vask taught two of her masons over the Sunday afternoon, standing in the mud at the north end of the arch, correcting them the way she corrected Owen, which was every eight seconds, and by four o'clock on the Sunday the older of the two, a man of fifty called Peth Lomas who had been in Saltwake since before Adrian was born, could get through all five without a pause and could not tell you why the fifth word was not part of the other four.

Sesh Brack taught Ovin Sarr, in the market, on the Sunday, while the shutters were going up, and she did it by saying the words out loud twice and then hitting him on the back of the neck with the flat of her hand when he got it wrong, which is a method Adrian had not seen anywhere and which produced a nineteen-year-old who could say all five by Sunday evening and who was extremely pleased about it in a way that worried Adrian for reasons he could not immediately justify.

Nyla Ferrow taught the keeper's boy — Pella, who is nine and thinks it is a joke — the four words, and let him get the fifth wrong on purpose for about a week, on the grounds, she said, that "a child who is allowed to be wrong in a promise is a child who will later be able to tell the difference, and I have not got time to teach that and he has not got the years to unlearn it."

And on the Monday morning at six o'clock, in the rain, Owen Park came up into the yard and said them, and got the fifth word right, and the arch agreed, and Adrian wrote it on the board in two columns, in his own terrible hand, because Vask would not chalk a Latin letter and would not lend her chalk:

> **twenty-first day, six. Said by the keeper's boy, four words, and the fifth, both attempts, the first did not take.**
>
> **twenty-first day, noon. Said by a mason of this city, four words and the fifth, first attempt.**

And underneath, in Tamsin's hand, in the local language, because Tamsin had come up at half past five and had watched and had then done the thing she did about four times a fortnight, which was to be in the room earlier than anybody expected and to make it impossible to be surprised:

> **The words are to be learned by as many people as will learn them. This is not a rota. A rota has a name at the top of it. — N. Ferrow, keeper, and S. Vail, notary, witnessed.**

---

Renna Ord found him at about eleven on the Monday, at the trestle, with his notebook open and forty-one lines on a page and a piece of chalk he was using to draw columns.

She read the page upside down, which he let her do, and then she put her thumb on the fourth line and said, "Who is that."

"A docker called Hesk. He's on the noon rotation at the near gate on the odd days."

"Take him off." She did not say why and then she said it. "Because the noon binding on the odd days is done by a man who is standing in a gate eight feet from the join in that gate, and if a mason is up here saying the words in the yard at noon on the odd days, and that man is the one nearest the arch, then on the odd days the arch is held by a mason and on the even days it is held by a docker, and on the even days a mason three streets away will hear about a binding that did not take and will come, and in a month you will have two masons and no rule at all. You do not take a man out of a gate to make him available for an arch. You take a man who is not in a gate and not in a wall." She moved the thumb. "And that one. Who is that."

"Sesh Brack."

"You have put the market's Sesh Brack on a wall in the rain at six in the morning four times a month." Renna Ord's voice did not rise, which was how Adrian knew she was about to say the true thing. "Have you asked her?"

"No."

"Then take her off and ask her." She straightened up. "Now. Here is the part you have not got to yet, and I am going to say it in about nine sentences and then I am going to carry a barrow. You have come to me because you want four witnesses for a rota, and I have not got four witnesses for a rota, and I have not got one, and I am not going to give you one. But I have got four men who sign the Narrow Mark, and every one of them has a day off, and every one of those days off is a day somebody else is somewhere else in this city holding a join, and if you want four witnesses in a yard at six in the morning then the four men are out of the gate crew for that hour, and the gate crew is nineteen men and one foreman, and the flood crew is twenty-one. Do you know what a day's labour off that crew costs?"

"One day a month. It's in the column."

"That is the column for the *instrument*." She said *instrument* the way you say a word when you are refusing to give it a promotion. "You are not asking me about the instrument. You are asking me about a post. And the difference between the two is that an instrument is entered in a book with four names on it and a review date, and a post is a man who cannot leave a yard, and a man who cannot leave a yard is a man who cannot be sent to a flood, and this city has had four floods in the last nine days, and if you put a fourth of this crew in a yard at six in the morning in the root month then in the eleventh month, when it is not raining and there is nothing interesting to come and look at, there will be a day when the man in the near gate is a man who is tired and is not the man who would have been there, and the gate will not be held as well, and nobody will ever connect that to a chalk mark on a keeper's door."

Adrian wrote that down. He wrote all of it down. He had written down four sentences that week and had wanted forty-one and had got them, and every one of them was a person, and this was the first one that had cost anything.

"Thin," he said.

"The gate crew is the thinnest thing in this city," said Renna Ord, "and it is thin because everybody in Saltwake thinks a lock is a building. It is not a building. It is nineteen men, every one of whom can be somewhere else, and the reason it has not failed is that it is not allowed to fail, and the reason it is not allowed to fail is that when it fails the water is in the market and there is nowhere to put the market." She shouldered a barrow. "Ask Vask to put the masons on it. Ask me for nothing. And do not put a name at the top of anything."

---

She had gone by eleven and the rain had stopped and the yard was empty and Adrian was sitting on the trestle with a notebook and a piece of chalk and forty-one lines on a page, and the trouble with forty-one lines is that nobody in the world can act on forty-one lines, and he knew that, and he had known it on the ninth day of the flood in a room above a market, and here it was again on the twentieth, at the same size.

Sera Vail came up into the archway at about half past eleven with a slate under her arm, and she had a piece of chalk of her own, which she did not use.

"I heard you had a page of forty-one names on it."

"I have."

"May I see it." She did not wait. "Mm. Now tell me what this is."

"It is who is answerable for what on each of the next twelve days."

"Who told you to write that?"

"Nobody. I worked it out at about four in the morning."

"Then I am going to tell you what it is, and I am going to tell you because I have watched a city do this twice in twenty-six years and both times it was a man's idea, and both times the man's idea was better than what anybody else had, and both times the thing that happened in month three is that the man's idea outlived the man." She put her slate on the trestle, and squared it, and did not sit down. "A date in a book is not a plan, Mr Vale. I want you to hear me say that twice, because you have been in this city twenty days and you have already built a chair out of it. A date in a book is not a plan. It is a *place a plan has to be legible in*. I write dates because a city cannot argue with a date. If I put the twenty-fifth of the salt month in a book, then in a fortnight every person in the Reed Reach can say *the twenty-fifth* at me and I cannot say *yes but* anything. That is not me being organised. That is me being *defended*, and I have wanted a defence like that since I was twenty-four and I have had to give one up every single year for twenty-six years because the day somebody uses a date in a book as a weapon is the day the book stops being a thing anybody can believe."

"Then what is the twenty-fifth of the salt month."

"It is the last day of the salt month, and a woman in Corrow has to put a number in a book about how much winter grain goes into a hundred and forty acres, and you owe her the number." Vail looked at the page. "And on that day you are going to stand in this hall and answer three questions that I wrote on the ninth morning of this flood, and I have not softened them in eleven days, and nobody in this city is going to soften them for you, and if you come on the twenty-fourth with a speech you will not get past the first one."

"Three questions."

"I will read you all three and you will not have had a chance to write them down, and that is deliberate." She put two fingers on the slate. "One. *What did the field lose, in measures, and by what work.* Not how sad. In measures. Two. *Who is answerable for it, by name, and what does that answerability cost that person in a month.* Three. *What happens to the households that have already paid, and how will they know that they have been paid.*"

Adrian wrote them on the page, in the flat hand, under his forty-one names, and did not cross anything out, and it took about forty seconds.

"There," said Vail. "Now. Here is the other half of the lesson and I am going to be exactly as unpleasant about it as I was about the first half. That page of yours has forty-one lines and it is four feet long and there is no person in this city who can act on it, and I include the man who wrote it, and I have been a notary for twenty-six years and I have watched about nine attempts to hand this city a list and every one of them died in the same place. They died because a list has no *time* in it. Forty-one names is a description of a city. What a city can act on is a *morning*."

She picked up the chalk — her own, not his — and held it out to him.

"Sit down. I am going to make you do the thing I would have had to do twenty-six years ago and I have never had to do, because nobody has ever come into my yard with a seat and forty-one names and a notebook." She tapped the trestle. "Read me every one of those forty-one names, in order, and I am going to say next to each one whether anybody in this city could be *asked* about it this week. And where the answer is no, we do not have a plan. We have a wish."

It took them until half past two in the afternoon, and it was the longest single piece of work Adrian had done in Veyra, and at the end of it there were nineteen lines left and no way at all to get them done by anybody, because the point of the exercise was not to get them done.

The nineteen lines were not his. That was the first thing. Nineteen days in the city and he had spoken to perhaps forty people, and thirty of them had spoken to him first, and he had built a plan for a city out of the people he had met. Hessa Ruun's water-count was on his page and he had not met her. The mason who would re-lime the arch if the yard flooded was on his page and he had not met him. The man who cut the reed at Long Water was on his page. The three gatehouses. The boy who held the second gate. Nine Elms, all nine households by name, and he had met two of them.

And the second thing was the one he wrote at the top of the page, in the flat hand, and did not cross out:

*A plan that I can execute is eleven lines long. A plan that this city can execute is nineteen lines long and I cannot reach the telephone in any of them. The difference is not a skill. The difference is that I have been here nineteen days.*

Sera Vail read the page upside down, which she did with every document anybody showed her, and then she said the thing that got entered in the ledger an hour later in her own words because nobody could think of anything better to write.

"I have entered one name against him in nineteen days and it is the first complainant and it is on page one, and it is a good entry, and I have had four days this month when I wished I had not entered it. Entering a name is not a punishment. It is the only thing I have that is like a hand, and a hand is a thing you use to write, and if you are frightened of using it you are a notary who is afraid of ink." She squared her slate. "You want to be at the counter. Fine. Be at the counter. But understand what is at the counter. It is not the queue. It is that **you are also the man who finds out that somebody has not turned up.** That is the seat. Nell Bray told you the rest of it in a hall on the Friday and she was right, and she left out the worst half, which is the half that made me thirty years old. There is no version of a counter where the finding out is done by somebody else."

---

He got to Corrow on the Tuesday, no — on the Monday. He walked down the Reach road on the Monday afternoon with Tamsin, three hours, in the cold, because there was a name on the first line of the twelve days and he had a duty attached to it and the duty was: *find out whether Elsa Skell has turned up.*

Elsa Skell had not turned up. Elsa Skell was in her yard, forty feet from the road, with her stick, and she came out to the gate when she saw him coming and she did not stop, and she said the whole of it in about nine seconds in the local language, and Tamsin translated it in the flat voice that gets worse when she is least angry, and it came out almost word for word, which meant Elsa Skell had prepared it, which meant Elsa Skell had been saying it to herself in a yard for two days, which is the single most frightening thing a person can do.

"You told a man in a good coat a thing in a chapel on Wednesday night and he went and found a stone, and ten days later the stone was under a bar and the ground was open and the arch is being said in the rain by a man from another world, and on Saturday morning a man from another world stood four feet back from a hole and wrote numbers in a book. And on Thursday evening you came down this road to tell me the cost of what had happened to a field of mine, and you had a *number* in you, and I have watched you look at my ditch."

"What did you say," Adrian said.

"She said: you did not come for the number, you came for a place to put it." Tamsin did not soften it and did not editorialise. "And she said: the number is in the eleventh house on the left, and it is not yours, and it will be in a book of its own on the twenty-fifth of the salt month at first light, and it is put in by a woman of this village and not by you, because a man who owes a season has no business putting a number in a village's book, and because when the number goes in that book it stops being a debt and starts being a crop, and I am not going to have my crop written by the man who is standing in my ditch."

Adrian stood in a gateway on the Reach road in the cold and found that he had no answer, which was happening to him at a rate of roughly once a week and had begun, in a way he did not examine, to feel like the ordinary condition of his life.

"I am not going to argue with her," he said.

"You are not going to argue with her because she is right and because you would win."

"That is not—"

"That is *not the reason* and you know it is not the reason." Tamsin put her hands in her belt. "You are not going to argue with her because she has decided not to talk to you, and you have decided that a woman not talking to you is a *problem with a schedule*, and you have come down a three-hour road to solve it on a Monday, and if she had said the same thing in a market you would have written it in your book and gone home." She started walking. "I have known you eighteen days and I have already watched this happen to you twice and both times it was in the direction of the field and never once in the direction of the room, and I am telling you now, on a road, in the cold, because on Friday the twenty-fifth there is a hall in this city and about two hundred people in it and one of them is going to stand up and say *we are not talking to him any more*, and if that is a surprise to you on the twenty-fifth then I have not been useful to you for a single one of the twenty days."

---

He got back up the road at about six in the dark and found Tam Crane, who is nine, sitting on the second step outside the loft with a piece of chalk in his hand and a piece of slate propped against the doorframe, and who had clearly been waiting for somebody.

"He says tell him the man has to be here for the seventh day of the new month," the boy said.

"Who says so?"

"The man in the good coat." Tam scratched his ear with the chalk, which is a thing children do. "He was up here an hour ago. He didn't say it out loud to anybody, he said it out loud to me, and I am telling you because you are the one that writes things down and I am not, and my mam says if you know something and you do not say it, then you are not being frightened, you are being *careful*, and being careful is what the grown ones do."

Adrian wrote it down at the top of a clean page, in the flat hand, and the sentence took him four attempts because his hand was cold.

*The seventh day of the new month. That is the Friday of the week after next, which is eleven days from today. M. has a date and has not told the room. He has told a nine-year-old on a step. Asked Tam to carry it to me. — A.*

He did not write down the other part, which was that a man in a good coat had, on the twentieth day of the flood, gone up two flights of stairs to find out whether the holder of the pull was in his rooms, and had not knocked, and had waited for a child to come out of a doorway. He wrote down the date and the boy's name and the words, and he put a rule under it — *every date a person says to me now goes on this page before I do anything else with it* — and he underlined it, and he had underlined two things in twenty days and both of them had been about this man.

---

They were at the second stair at about half past seven when the reed runner came in off the road.

He came up the market at a run, which nobody in Saltwake does, and he was wet through and about nineteen and he had come eleven miles off the Reach road in the dark, and he went straight past the loft and up to the second door and knocked on it four times and then stood there.

Sesh Brack opened it, and made him sit down on her step, and brought him water, and did it while nobody was watching on purpose.

Then he stood up and held out a folded sheet of paper with a seal on it that was not a Crown seal and not the Concord's.

"It is from the meeting-ground," he said. "It is not from Aurel. It is not from the Concord. It is from the Reach, and I was told to say it out loud on a step, in a market, so that anybody who wanted to hear it could hear it, and not in a room, because a thing said in a room in the Reach is a thing four people know and a thing said on a step in a market is a thing forty people know."

Tamsin read it standing, and then read it out loud in the local language, on the step, in the market, with a market about it, and Adrian understood that she had known the whole time that she was going to do that and had let him get the paper first.

> **To the holder of the pull of the thing entered in this city's book, from the meeting-ground of the Reed Reach, on the twentieth day of the flood.**
>
> **The meeting-ground asks a person to come and stand in a room, on the twenty-third, and stay until it is finished.**
>
> **The meeting-ground does not name a person. It asks the holder of the pull to come, or to send a person of his own choosing who has not been asked to come and does not know that he is coming.**
>
> **The meeting-ground has one question, and it is not about the seam.**

Sesh Brack read it too, over Adrian's shoulder, and made a noise.

"You have not been named," she said.

"No."

"You have been *asked for*." She folded her own apron over her hands, which she does when she is about to be pleased about something and is not going to enjoy it. "Twenty days this city, and a post on a keeper's wall, and a seat in a chained book, and a woman in Corrow who will not speak to you, and a boy of nine who follows you at six feet and has not said one word about why, and eleven villages that have given you a season — and a room on the Reach has written a letter that does not have your name in it, and it has cost a boy his Saturday to bring it here, and it was addressed to *the holder of the pull*, which is a thing a person has, not a thing a person is." She looked at him. "That is either a very great courtesy or it is the worst insult anybody has paid you since Tuesday, and I have not got the wit tonight to say which, and nobody on this step has, and it will be found out in a room."

"The meeting-ground does not name a person," Adrian said, slowly, reading it again, and getting to it after everybody else on the step had. "It asks me to send a person of my own choosing. And Tamsin — it does not say *one of the six*. It does not say *one of the wall*. It says a person of my own choosing, who has not been asked to come and does not know that he is coming."

"That is not a courtesy," said Tamsin. "That is a woman who knows what it costs to be sent by a man who has a seat, and who has arranged to be able to say afterward that nobody in this room was *compelled*, and she did it in a letter so that it is in a document and not in a memory." Her voice was entirely level. "Which means there is going to be a room on Wednesday with nine men and eleven women in it, and about three hundred people on the bank behind the meeting-ground, and two strangers in the front of it, and one of the two strangers is the man who has been in their ditches twice, and the other one is a woman with a public table in this city who is going to be talking for a man in a good coat within a year."

"Which one of us goes," said Adrian.

The step was quiet for a moment. Behind them the market was shutting down along the stalls and somebody two rows away was calling for a shutter, and up above them, in the loft, a light came on in a window, and Owen Park's voice said something in English to somebody else, and it was the voice of a man who had been up a ladder all afternoon and did not know yet that a piece of paper with a seal on it had come up the road in the dark.

The man who wrote the letter had not written Owen Park's name either, and Adrian had been in this city twenty days, and he had already worked out that a name absent from a document is the most expensive kind of absence there is, and that he did not know which way it was expensive, and that he had until Wednesday morning to find out.

---

He wrote it at the top of a clean page, at half past eleven, in the loft, with a headache behind the left eye that he did not have any reason to have and did not examine.

*Monday, the twenty-first day. Yesterday a notary gave me three dates and a woman with a slate told me to work out the rest. Four days to the last day of the salt month. Seven days to the next tide. Ten days to the hearing. A post, and a page with nineteen names on it that I cannot reach, and a rota I am not allowed to have.*

*Nothing gained. What I have instead is a sentence from a notary in a yard: a date in a book is not a plan, it is a place a plan has to be legible in. And a second one from the same woman an hour later, which is the whole of what I am going to be for the next ten days: when you are at the counter you are also the man who finds out that somebody has not turned up. I have been building for nineteen days a thing in which nobody is answerable for anything and everybody is present at the time of the event, and it turns out to be a very beautiful design and it is the design of a man who does not want to be told.*

*And a woman in Corrow has told me to my face that a man who owes a season has no business putting a number in a village's book, and she is right, and I walked three hours in the cold to be told something I already had in the notebook on page four, and I want it written down here that I did that, because I am going to want to do it again and I am not going to be able to tell, in a month, whether I walked three hours because a person was in trouble or because I could not stand being outside a room.*

*And a man in a good coat has a date and has not told the room and has told a boy of nine on a step, and I have the date, and the nineteenth line of a page that nobody can act on, and a seat.*

*Ask the people who maintain the gate. Ask the keeper. Ask the woman who keeps a count.*

*And on Wednesday, somebody goes down the road in a boat, and the person it is has not been asked and does not know, and I do not have a way of finding that out which does not use the man in the good coat.*
