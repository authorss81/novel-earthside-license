# Chapter 0012 — The First Use of the Record

Priya Shah was on the stair at six in the morning with her notebook open and the wall in front of her, and she had not slept, and she had done the thing she had wanted to do since Wednesday, which was to read her own log from the beginning in order, out loud, to nobody.

"—and I asked him for a name and a thing he was afraid of because Adrian said it was a good onboarding question, and I wrote down the spelling he gave me, and I wrote down the fear in his words, and he *asked* me to write the name and he did not ask me to write the fear anywhere, and I put all twelve lines on a piece of paper and I nailed it to a wall over a market because Noor said it should go up next to the rules, and Noor was right, and here we are."

She shut the notebook.

"So take it down," she said. "That's the fix. Take it down and put the rules somewhere else and I will write out the rules myself, four times, for a week, to anybody who will stand still. And then in a month nobody will know there was a list."

Tamsin, who had come up the second stair because she had heard voices, said, in the local language, and then in English, "Take it down when?"

"Sometime last night. Or first thing. I have been awake since two."

"What happened to it?"

"Nothing happened to it. It was read out."

And Priya stopped, in the way of a person who has said a sentence and has heard it.

---

Adrian got to the market at about seven, and the market was the same market it had been on Tuesday, with two fewer stalls trading and a smell of salt and wet rope, and the low shuttered shop with the blue door, and above it, at the height of a grown man's shoulder, a sheet of pressed paper with a flour merchant's invoice printed on the back of it, and beside it, a little lower and a little crooked, a second scrap of the same paper.

He stood in front of it for a while.

It was not a good sheet. The four rules were on the invoice, in his own terrible Latin hand, big, at the top, taking up two thirds of the sheet. The six names and six fears were on the scrap underneath, in Priya's hand, neater, in two columns, with the spelling each person had given, and the scrap was the piece of a page that had come out of his own notebook, which meant that half of what was nailed over a market had been torn out of the pocket of a man who worked for a company that no longer existed.

*Mina Okoye — that I find out I can help and it isn't enough.*
*Dante Kuo — that I'm the one holding the door. I'm always the one holding the door.*
*Priya Shah — that it turns out I did this for content.*
*Jonah Pike — that there's no order here.*
*Noor Haddad — my mother's house. My mother's in it. I'm the only one who's ever left.*
*Owen Park — that somebody walks in and I don't get to do the thing.*

Six names. Six fears. Two hundred and eleven people had read it in a hall on Friday morning and not one of them had stopped on it, and Adrian had stood at the front of that hall for three hours and let it go past a hundred and forty times, because he had been thinking about a seam.

The flour merchant's invoice was dated the eleventh of last month and had somebody else's name on it, and the ink had run in the wet on Tuesday night, and the corner had come away from the nail.

He did not take it down. That was the decision he made on the spot and he made it deliberately, standing in the wet with a market opening around him, and he wrote it down afterwards: *I did not take it down on Saturday morning. Not out of mercy to Priya. Because I understood, four seconds after I stopped to do it, that a sheet that comes off a wall without a reason is a sheet that has been taken down for a reason, and I do not know what that reason is yet, and a man who does not know why he is doing a thing should not do it.*

---

The man who had said the name was called Berren Sill and he was sixty-one and salt-stained to the elbow and he counted salt out of the yard at the tally-house, which was four streets up the hill on the side away from the river.

He was not sorry. Adrian had braced for sorry and got something better than sorry, which was a man who had no idea at all that he had done a thing, and who was annoyed about the hour, because it was early and the counting started early.

"I said it because I was telling him about the paper," Sill said. "The man next to me. He has a daughter at the school at Silt Row and he wanted to know whether the six are going to be at the inspection on Saturday, because his girl is one of nine and there is a thing going round the Reach about whether the water comes. That is what we were talking about. I said the paper is on the wall and there is a name on it that is the one who writes things down, and he said is that the one with the phone, and I said the phone has no bars so how would I know, and that was the whole conversation and it lasted about a minute."

"Who read the paper to you?"

Nobody answered for a second.

"You read it yourself," Adrian said.

"I read it. I can read. I have read a tally for forty years."

"Then you read it off a wall."

"I read *a bit* of it off a wall. The rules at the top." Sill shifted a sack. "But I did not get the six names off the wall on Thursday, because on Thursday the six names were not on the wall in a way anybody could read. The man with the blue door had hung a coat over the bottom half of it on Wednesday night, because people kept standing in front of his shutter and he was losing a morning's trade, and he took it off again on Friday when the rain had it wet through and he wanted his shutter back. He told me about it in the yard and laughed about it. I did not think about it at the time. Nobody did. It was a coat."

Adrian stood very still in a salt yard at seven in the morning and understood that he had made an error and that the error was not in a document.

He had spent nine years believing that information moves because somebody carried it. A document moves because somebody carried it. A leak is a person with a reason and an access. It is the first thing you learn about any system you have to secure, and it had been the frame for every decision he had made since Tuesday morning, and it was the wrong frame entirely.

The coat had been a man protecting a shutter. It had had a reason and it had had an access, and it had not moved a single name anywhere, and it was the only thing about that wall that nobody had arranged, including him.

Names move because somebody *read them out*.

A wall is silent. A wall is the safest thing in the world; a wall has never once told anybody anything. But a lock with a foreign thing in it and a man with a mark in his hand is the single most interesting object in this city, and there are forty stalls under it, and half of them cannot read, and the ones who can read are not the ones who need telling, and so somebody reads it out. Twice a day, in a market aisle, to a knot of people, in a voice, because that is what a market is.

And on Wednesday afternoon, in the rain, standing in that aisle, the person reading it out had been Tamsin.

"It was you," Adrian said.

"It was me," Tamsin said. "You gave me the sheet on Tuesday night and you said read it to anybody who asks, and I read it to anybody who asked, and I read it eleven times on Wednesday in a market because the four rules do not work if you only know them in your own language, and I would do it again."

She said it without apology, and that was the correct thing and it was the worst thing.

"You read the names out."

"I read the whole sheet out. I did not stop at the rules, because I did not think of the bottom half as a separate thing, and because I have never in my life had to think of the thing I am the only person who can say as a separate thing." She looked at the chalk on the wall, or where it was. "Adrian. I am the only person in this city who can say your name out loud in a way that a stranger understands. Every word I have said for four days has gone through my mouth into a market and down a road. I did not know that was a leak. I knew it was a *need*. Those are the same sentence and I have been standing in it."

---

Jonah Pike had got up early and had opinions.

He had them in the yard, in English, at volume, because Jonah's volume was a thing that had simply always been there and could not be turned down.

"Say it. Say the true version out loud and say it where people can hear it, and say it *boringly*."

"Boringly," Adrian said.

"Scared people can be organised. Scared people with a shape in front of them can be *led*. And the second that there is a shape, this stops being a rumour and starts being a story with a man in it, and stories with a man in them get a queue outside the door on Saturday." Jonah was pacing, and his good coat was on, because Jonah's good coat was not a costume, it was a tool. "I have watched a warehouse floor of ninety people turn round in about four seconds, and every single one of them was doing arithmetic about the fire, and every single one of them was wrong about where the fire was, and the only reason they were wrong is that nobody had given them a thing to be right about. Give them a thing. A list, a gate, a date. Anything. A frightened crowd that can see a threat is a crowd that can be *organised*."

"Organised by whom," said Mina Okoye, from the window.

"By whoever is standing nearest," Jonah said, cheerfully, and did not pretend to be wounded, and that was the worst of him and the best of him both. "Yes. By me, if I am standing there. And I would be standing there, and Adrian would not, and I would not put anybody's name in anything, and in nine months the thing I built would have a list with six names on it and I would be the man who reads the list. I know. I said it out loud. That is the difference between me and a man who has not."

"Then we do not do it," Adrian said.

"We do not do *your* version of it. You have been standing in a room since Friday waiting for a date to put in a page." Jonah stopped pacing. "There is a Saltwake market this morning in which a man is telling a story about a list that came out of a wall. If we say nothing, that story has no competitor and it grows. If we say something boring and true on the same morning, the story has a competitor and the story loses about half its power by noon. That is not propaganda, Adrian. That is *distribution*, which is my whole adult life."

---

The tally-house was four streets up the hill and it was the first place Adrian had been in Saltwake that smelled of paper.

Halvard Ketch kept the salt tally and the older records in one long room with a plank floor and shutters, and he was about sixty-five and he had been doing it since he was nineteen, and he was the sort of man who corrected your grammar out of affection. He had been expecting Adrian for an hour; Adrian had sent word up at the market and word had travelled faster than he had, which was the first physical evidence that Adrian was becoming a thing people had word about.

"Marrow sent for this in the first week of the flood," Ketch said, before Adrian had got the door shut. "Marrow. The Crown man. He wanted anything in this room with a wall in it."

"He told me it was a fraud."

"It is not a fraud." Ketch went to a shelf and came back with a flat board, and unwrapped a strap, and inside the strap was a single leaf of pressed paper the colour of weak tea, and he put it down on the plank between them with the care of a man handling a plate. "Three hundred and ten years old, more or less. It is in a customs hand and it is about a light in a wall, and the clerk who wrote it wanted a refund on a salt consignment because his boat had been held eleven days at the mouth of the river waiting for somebody to come out of the hole, and he wanted to know whether the Crown was going to pay for the delay."

"And?"

"And he got it. Look at the bottom." Ketch put a finger on a line. "He has written a list. Not a list of things. A list of *what came out*. Six items. He does not have names, he has descriptions, and the descriptions are the descriptions of men who cannot be described otherwise: *one who could not say the words, two who could not stop shaking, one who went back to the hole three times and had to be held, one who asked for paper, one who asked for a priest, one who was a child.*"

Adrian read it twice.

"It is genuine," he said.

"It is genuine. It is a customs clerk who wanted eleven days off and wrote down the six most upsetting things he had ever seen to justify them, and it went into a file, and the file was not important for three hundred years." Ketch sat down. "And there is not one name in it. There is not one name in it because a customs clerk does not know their names, they are on the manifest, and the manifest burned in the fire at the chandlery in the year of the wet winter. It has never had a name in it."

"And Marrow said it was a fraud."

"Marrow said it was a fraud in a room with nine people in it on the Wednesday, and he was talking about the *word* in it, the word the clerk used for the light, and the word is a bad word, it is a word a man uses when he has been frightened and wants his money back. That is not a fraud. That is a frightened clerk." Ketch folded his hands. "And he let the room believe the whole of it was a fraud, and I do not think he meant to, and I have been turning it over for three days and I still cannot make it come out as an accident."

---

Adrian walked back down the hill very slowly.

It came together in about four minutes, on the slope, in the rain, and it came together the way the bad things in this world always came together for him, all at once, after the fact, with the answer already known.

The dangerous thing was not the mouth.

Berren Sill was a man counting salt. A milk woman reading to two customers. A man on a bench who had said a name out loud because it was in his mouth. None of them were a leak. None of them had a reason. The mouth did nothing; mouths are how information actually moves, and no amount of security has ever stopped a mouth, and Adrian had known that since his first week in an office.

The dangerous thing was the **roll**.

Because the sheet on the wall said *six names*, and a man in the market on Wednesday said there was a paper about the wall with names on it, and Halvard Ketch had a genuine three-hundred-and-ten-year-old customs document in which a list of six things that came out of a hole was written down at the time, by a man who wanted his eleven days back — and if you put those two facts in the same head for five seconds, which is all any human being takes, then the six names on the wall stop being six frightened strangers and become an *item on a list*, and a list in Saltwake is not a list of people. A list in Saltwake is a **count**, and a count is the thing you keep so that you know how many of anything there are.

And a count of what? A count of how many people have come out of that wall. Which means: a queue. Which means: an order. Which means that somewhere in this city there is now a man who thinks there is a position to be had, and that it can be bought, and that Jonah Pike is standing in a market forty feet away with a good coat on and a whole adult life of distribution.

And the reason nobody had stopped it was that Adrian had spent Friday morning in a salt store doing procedure, and Priya had spent Friday night doing courage, and neither of them had spent a single minute on the fact that the group's most persuasive object was a sheet of paper about six people nailed over forty stalls.

And the reason *that* was true, the deep reason, and the one he wrote in the notebook that night as the first line, was this:

*I asked for the names. I asked, on the wet stones by the lock, in front of six strangers who had not asked to be there, for a name and a fear, because it was a good question. They gave me both and I wrote both down and I did not ask one of them, at any point, whether the fear was theirs to be written down. The fear was given to a man on a piece of road. It was not given to a wall.*

---

The pale field came up outside the chandlery, in the rain, and stood there.

> SIX NAMES WERE ASKED FOR. SIX NAMES WERE WRITTEN DOWN.
>
> The paper can be taken down tonight.
>
> Nobody has said that you may.

It stayed nine seconds.

It was not wrong. The paper *could* be taken down tonight; it was on one nail. And Adrian could feel the shape of the thing it was built to do, because he had built a hundred of them, and the shape was always the same: offer the cheap reversible action, and put the irreversible cost of the cheap action two clauses downstream where a tired man at the end of a bad week will not reach it.

The cost of taking it down, tonight, silently, was not that a secret would be kept. The cost was that in about nine days every person in this market would know that the sheet came off the wall at a particular hour, and would know that the hour meant something, and would look for the reason; and the reason was not in the wall any more, because the fears had been the only part of it that a person could not get from a rumour.

He did not take it down.

---

Mina Okoye overruled him at half past one that afternoon, in the loft, in front of the other five, and she was right, and she did it in four sentences, and he has never once been able to reconstruct the argument without the words *and it is worse than that* in it.

"You have been talking about the sheet since seven o'clock as if the problem is the names," Mina said. "The problem is not the names. The names were asked for. Every one of them was asked for by name and gave their name and spelled it, and Adrian wrote the spelling they gave him, and I was there and it was done properly. The problem is the bottom half of that piece of paper, and the bottom half is six fears that six people said out loud on a piece of road in the rain because a man asked them a good question, and nobody was asked whether the answer was for the wall."

"Priya wrote it down," Adrian said. "It was on the sheet. It went up because Noor said—"

"Because Noor said a rule ought to be somewhere a person could reach, which is correct, and the rule was on the top, and the fears were on the bottom, and nobody in that room thought of the bottom as a different kind of object, and you did not, and I did not, and Priya did not, and all four of us are people who make our living out of knowing what a document *is*." She put her hands flat on the table. "So here is the decision, and it is mine, and Adrian is not going to like all of it. The names stay up. The fears come off tonight, in front of people, and it is not a raid and it is not a panic, and the reason they come off is not that they are dangerous. It is that six people gave them to a man on a piece of road and not to a wall, and the wall is not who they were talking to."

Nobody said anything for a while.

"That is going to leave a list of six names on a wall over a market," said Adrian.

"Yes," said Mina. "And it is going to leave a *true* one, and every person who can read it is going to find out that this is the whole of what we will say about ourselves, in public, permanently, and that there is nothing else. And the second the fears come off, a certain number of people are going to say the six have been protected, and a certain number are going to say the six have been made to look innocent, and a certain number are going to say the six are hiding something, and all three of those are correct and all three of them are less bad than the other one." She looked at Priya, who had not moved. "Priya. Do you want the names up or down."

Priya said, "Up."

"And why."

"Because my mother is going to see it one day," Priya said, "and I would rather she read *Shah* and nothing else."

---

They drafted it in the loft from half past one until a quarter to three, and Adrian was not allowed to write any of it, and neither, in the end, was Jonah.

Mina wrote it. Tamsin translated it back and forth three times and refused two phrases in it as *tidy*. Sesh Brack came up the stair at one point, on the way to a shift, read the whole thing standing, and said "you have not said anything about us," and Mina added a clause. Adrian's entire contribution was a list of dates and hours, and it was used twice, both times correctly, and he sat at a table in a room where six people were making a decision he was not the author of and let it happen, and the effort of letting it happen was the largest single physical effort of his week.

It went to a small table in the river market at ten past three, where a man with a board had a board, and it was read out by an old woman with a good voice because she was the only person at that table who could read, and the man with the board wrote it out in a big hand and put it up next to the rules, under the six names, and about forty people stood and read it.

Here is the whole of it, as it went up:

> **WHAT IS TRUE, ON SATURDAY THE FIFTH DAY OF THE FLOOD, SAID BY THE SIX AND BY THE MAN IN THE SEAT.**
>
> Six people came out of a wall in the outer channel of this lock at midday on Tuesday, and a man came out of the same wall at first light the same morning. Nobody asked them. Nobody held the wall. They have no road and no way to send a message and no way home, and they have said so in this market every day since.
>
> The six are named above. There is no other list. There is no order, and nobody is waiting, and no one is being fetched, and no one has a position, and nobody may buy one, and if anybody in this city is told otherwise they should come to the Concord hall and say it out loud in a room.
>
> The names were given by the people themselves and are the whole of what we have said about them in public. The other thing that was on the wall has been taken down. It was not ours to leave up and we are sorry.
>
> The Lock of Salt's gate is open and damaged and is watched. Nothing has been made better by any of the six. A season of the bottom field is owed and is entered in the Concord's book against a named man, and it will be reviewed in public on the last day of the salt month.
>
> Nobody from the wall has asked this city for anything, and nobody from the wall is going to ask for anything this week.

Priya read the last line back to Adrian at about four, in the yard, in English, off a slate, because she was the one who had to be able to say it.

Adrian listened to it twice.

"That is wrong," he said.

"Say the part that is wrong."

"It is the third line and the fifth line. *Nobody is waiting, and no one is being fetched, and nobody has a position.* That is not true, it is a promise, and promises do not go on walls, they go in a book with a date on it, and we have spent a week finding that out." He took the slate off her. "And the last line. *Nobody from the wall has asked this city for anything.* That is false, and I know that it is false, because I asked four dockers to stand under a gate on Wednesday and two of them lost a day of pay and I said it on Tuesday and Vask wrote it in a book as my reason for being allowed to work."

"Then say the true one and I will put it up."

"You cannot. That is the thing you are not seeing. If we say *we have taken a season of your field and we do not have a road home and we are not going to give you one this week*, then that is on a board over a market, and by Monday every man in the Reed Reach knows that the season is gone and that the six who took it have no way to give it back. And on the Saturday after that, when the inspection is to be and the tide turns, somebody in Nine Elms or Corrow is going to use that sentence, and they are going to be right to, and there is not one thing I can say afterwards that will sound like anything other than a man minimising."

Priya looked at the slate for a while.

"So we say *nobody has asked for anything*," she said, "which is a claim about the past, and it is true, and it is the last sentence a person can put on a wall and still be able to stand behind it in a month."

"Yes."

"And we both know what the second sentence means."

"Yes," Adrian said. "And I want it in the notebook that I disagreed, and I want it on the wall too. Not that line. A line under it." He took the chalk out of his pocket, and then put it back in his pocket, because it was not his wall and he had been told so in a yard once already, three days ago, by a woman with a board of lime and clay. "Ask Sesh for a bit of chalk. Put under that last line, in smaller letters, and in the local language, so that it does not look like a legal notice: *What the six were asked for, and what they were asked by, and what they were told it would cost, is in the Concord's book, and any person in this city may come and read it.*"

Priya wrote it out and looked at it.

"That is much worse than the lie," she said.

"That is the only sentence on that board that will still be true in February," Adrian said. "The rest of it is a person being as careful as they can be at six o'clock on a Saturday. That is worth doing. But that line is a door, and it is the only door on that wall."
