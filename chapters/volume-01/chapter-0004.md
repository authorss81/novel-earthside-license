# Chapter 0004 — A Door in the Lock

The stair down into the tunnels was cut into the wall of the lock itself, and halfway down it the water came over Adrian's boot tops, and by the bottom it was at his shin, which was four inches higher than it had been in the yard an hour ago.

"There," said Tamsin, and pointed at a lamp hung on a bracket, and then at the water, and then at Adrian, and waited.

He understood her immediately, which was a thing that kept happening to him now and which he did not like.

"How fast," he said.

"Not a number you can use. The tide does not keep a clock. It keeps a *mood.*" She said the last word the way you would pick up a tool you did not trust. "Watch that lamp for a while and tell me what you see."

He watched the lamp. It hung on a bracket over the water and its reflection lay in a long smear on the surface. Over about two minutes the smear shortened, from a long streak to a compact oval, and then began to lengthen again, and then shortened further than it had been, and the water over his boots went from a tickle to a steady push.

"It isn't just going up," he said. "It comes in slugs. Something's charging it. Something on the other side fills up and then lets go and the water here feels it."

Tamsin considered him for a second. Then she turned to the man in the grey-green coat, who had come down with them and the slate, and said, "Twenty-one years I have been on this lock. Nobody has ever said that in words before."

"It is the shape of it," Adrian said, and immediately wished he had stopped, because he could hear how it sounded, and how it sounded was like a man from the other side of the world reading a gauge on a machine he had never seen.

The man in the grey-green coat was Hettis Roke, and Adrian learned that in the next half hour, in fragments, and mostly learned it by watching him refuse to hand the slate to anybody. Roke spoke Veyran to the room and English to Adrian, in the same voice, without any ceremony in the changeover, in the way of a man who had decided that the man from the wall was worth eight words of study and no more than that.

---

They came out into the lower gallery, which was the bottom of the lock: a long stone room with a low ceiling, three arched bays, a channel running down the middle of the floor that was under a foot of fast water, and, in the far arch, a gate.

The gate was a slab of black timber faced with iron, four feet thick, set in a stone frame with a channel cut around it, and it was up. It was not up because the water was low. It was up because six men were standing in that channel with their shoulders under it, taking the whole weight of the tide on their backs, and Adrian heard them before he saw them properly — a low collective sound under the noise of the water, not words, the sound of people agreeing to a thing they had agreed to a hundred times before.

"That is the near gate," Tamsin said.

"That is not the door I came out of."

"No." She said it without impatience. "That gate is how we work. The chamber above is how the tide gets in. You have been staring at the wrong half of the building."

He took it in. He took in the six men under the gate, the two on the frame with a bar, the wedge, the smell of wet timber and lamp oil, the way the man on the left end kept shifting his grip a few inches every twenty seconds so his shoulder would not lock. He took in the second bay, where two dock workers were kneeling on a plank with their hands flat on the stone floor, doing nothing at all visibly, in a way that was unmistakably work.

"Who are they?"

"Bray and Sarr. They are holding a join."

"Holding it with what?"

"With the oath." Tamsin said it the way you say *with the tide.*

And then, because Adrian had asked and would not have stopped asking, she told him, standing in four inches of moving water in a stone room, in about a hundred words, the thing that would reorganise the rest of his life.

"The lock is not a machine. It is a promise that three places make to each other, twice a day, for four hundred years, and this room is where they make it. When the words are said here, the stone listens — not the whole stone, not a god, not a spirit. The part of it that has heard the words before. Call it a *join*. It is the place where a pattern, a place, and the people who keep it are all still attached to each other."

"And if the words are not said?"

"Then the join gets thinner. It does not vanish. It thins, and one day you find out how thin it had got." She looked at the far arch, where the pale grey light was coming through the wet stone. "There is a place under the lock chapel that has been thinned for longer than anyone alive will say. That is a different conversation. This one is about today."

"And a working?"

"A working is a hand on a join. Words and stone and water and somebody standing there saying yes." She was choosing her words with a care Adrian could feel. "Here is the part you need, and I am saying it once, and then you may use it or not: a promise that nobody accepts is not a working. It is only noise. If you say the words and the people who have to stand in them do not accept them, you have made a sound. The stone does not care how much you meant it."

Roke said something, short and hard, in the direction of Adrian. Tamsin translated it without softening: "He says you were found in a room with a light in the wall and now you want us to say words to the wall. He wants to know what a man who studied this lock so well was doing in a place where nobody studies locks."

The honest answer was: I have never seen a lock. I do not know what this is. I have been awake since dawn in a world I cannot read and I can see a thing in the water and I would like to stop it killing us.

What he said was, "I'm not going to lie to you. I don't know anything about locks. But I can tell you three places where that water is losing, and I know why, and I can tell you in a way that you'll be able to argue with. That's all I've got."

Roke gave him a level, unhurried look that lasted long enough to be uncomfortable. Then he said four words to Tamsin, and Tamsin's mouth tightened, and she said, "He says that's what a spy says."

"Then he's right, from where he's standing," Adrian said. "That's fair. I'd say the same thing."

Bray said something from the floor without lifting her hands. Tamsin did not translate it, and did not translate it for four hours, and by the end of the afternoon Adrian understood that the refusal had been about the payment and not about the stranger, and that she had let him believe otherwise for most of a day because she wanted to see what he would do with it.

---

He got a slate and a stub of chalk and drew the room.

It took him nine minutes and it was the first time in a year he had done anything that felt like work instead of administration. He drew the plan as a pressure diagram instead of a floor plan, because a floor plan told him nothing and a pressure diagram told him where the water *wanted* to be.

He put a mark in the first bay, under the lamp, and wrote three words beside it: *it decides here*. That was the bay directly beneath the chamber where he had woken. It was where the water came first and left last, and the lamp's reflection was short over it right now, which meant the water was already in it and did not intend to leave.

He put a mark in the third bay, against the wall, and wrote four more: *going up, not through*. There was a line of white standing up on the water there, holding a curve against the stone like a bowstring. The water was being pushed sideways and it was not going where the gate let it. It was climbing.

He put a mark on the ceiling of the second bay, and wrote: *fell further*. The water coming down from above had changed its sound. It had been a slap. Now it was a hiss, which meant it was falling a long way into a small space, which meant the leak above had opened into something.

"Here," he said, tapping three marks with the chalk. "And here. And the leak's worse. And the reason is that you're all standing under a load with nowhere to put it, and you're trying to fix it by holding harder."

Bray said something from the floor, flat, without lifting her hands.

"What did she say?"

"She said you have been in this room eleven minutes." Tamsin's voice was hard. "She said that if you were right about the load, then the gate has to go down and come up, and that a gate is a promise and you do not put a promise down in a flood to see what happens."

"I'm not saying put it down—"

"She knows what you are saying."

Adrian stood in the moving water with a piece of chalk and understood that he was about to make a proposal that would be very stupid if it was wrong and would save the room if it was right, and that there was no way on this earth to know which before trying, and that this was a materially different situation from every decision he had ever made in an office.

He thought about it for as long as he could afford to.

"Okay," he said. "Then we don't put the promise down. We add one. Here's what I want, and I'll say it as an instruction because I don't have the words and I'll say it three times so somebody can stop me."

Then he made himself say her three conditions back to her, out loud, in her words, because a rule you have only nodded at is not a rule you have agreed to. *You will not touch a working. You will not use a name. You will ask before you take a thing.* The third one nearly went wrong, because *take* had four different words in this language for four different kinds of taking, and Tamsin made him choose the one that meant *use up* and then looked at him until he understood the difference and was embarrassed by it. Then she said the first two again so that the crew would have them from her and not from him, and Adrian understood that this was not a courtesy.

"What I want," Adrian said, "is for all of you to say the maintenance oath. Not here. Up in the yard, in the open, with the Hearthguard standing there and Nyla Ferrow at the arch, so that it's a real promise and not me making a noise. All six of the men under that gate, all of you, and anybody else who maintains this lock and is willing."

He heard himself say it and felt how it sounded, and then he went on anyway, because the alternative was not saying it.

"Because that gate isn't the problem. The gate is working. The gate is doing the only thing it can do. The problem is the *joins* in the floor and the wall have got thin, and a thin join can be a promise, and it can be a promise only if somebody is standing in it. That's it. That's the whole idea. You don't need strength. You need a witness."

There was a silence in the lower gallery long enough for the water to make a whole decision.

Then Roke said something, and Tamsin translated it, and it was nine words long and it changed the shape of the day.

"He says that is the oldest thing anybody has said under this lock in his lifetime, and that it is also what the gate was for, and that it has been a long time since anybody thought of it as an answer to anything except a flood." She paused. "He says the join in the third bay has been in his family's charge since before his grandfather and he has never once seen it take a spoken promise, and he would like to watch you be wrong, and he wants the oath said on the arch, in the open, with the mark in your hand shown to the Hearthguard first."

"Fine."

"It is not fine, and I am not saying it for your benefit. I am saying it because if you stand on that arch and show that mark to sixty people and the join does not answer, then you will not be a stranger who came out of a wall. You will be a false promise, and false promises in this city are worse than anything else, and I will not be able to get you out of it by being reasonable with you." She looked at the far arch. "So before you do that, you will do something smaller and you will do it where I can see."

---

They cleared the second bay first. That was a real job: two hours of getting a grown man out from under a moving stone channel on a rope and plank, and getting the rubble that had been sitting under the gate's drip-line for a decade out of the way, and it took six people and one extremely bad-tempered Hearthguard woman called Brann who worked like she was angry at the tide personally.

And when the second bay was clear and three of the men under the near gate were swapped for two, and the oath had been said in the yard twice with sixty people standing in the rain, Adrian walked out into the third bay with the chalk in his hand and the pale mark on his palm.

He did not know what happened next, and he would turn it over four different ways afterwards without any of them getting cleaner, but at the time all he had was the sound of the water and a piece of chalk.

What happened was that somebody behind him said a word — a short one, ordinary, an instruction — and the two men kneeling on the plank in the second bay said the words they had said a hundred times, and the six men under the gate took a breath together without meaning to.

And the floor of the lock went quiet.

Not silent. Quiet. The water over his boots stopped pushing. The hiss from the ceiling stopped. The room's whole enormous churning conversation dropped by half, the way a room drops when everyone in it hears the same thing at the same moment, and out of the quiet came something Adrian had no category for at all:

A line.

It ran from the plank where the two men knelt, under the water, up through the stone, out through the third bay's wall and up, and it was not light. It was *awareness*, shaped like a wire, with a direction in it. The direction was the third bay. It was the loudest thing he had ever been inside of.

He put his hand against the wall. He could have told them, right then, in about nine words, exactly where the water was going to fail.

He said, "The first thing is the leak. Second is the ridge. Third is the far arch, and third is where the whole weight is going, and when we push it off the arch it has nowhere to go."

"You are certain," Tamsin said.

"Yes."

"You can see it."

"I can see where it's *going*." He put his fingers flat on the wet stone, and the line under his palm was very cold, and very thin, and the headache came up behind his eyes with a hot nail in it. "Not because I'm clever. Because somebody in this room is holding a promise, and while they're holding it I can feel where the stone is still listening. That's all this is. That's not me. If they let go, I think it stops."

He did not say *I have no idea where I learned this.* He thought it, very loudly, and it did not help.

And then blood came out of his nose, warm, and went down his front into the water without ceremony, and he wiped it with the back of his wrist and said, "Okay. Third bay first."

---

They worked for two hours. They were good at it, and Adrian was not good at anything except talking, and eventually he found his actual job, which was standing in the third bay saying things out loud in the wrong language with his hands while six Veyrans repeated the maintenance oath around him.

He sent the load off the far arch into the outer channel.

It was the correct call. He was certain of it in a way that felt, at the time, like the first real competence he had ever had in his life. The outer channel was a spillway. It was what the lock was *for*, on the days when the river had to put something somewhere. He got the water to accept it. The join under the third bay went quiet and hard and stopped fighting, and the foam ridge against the outer wall dropped, and the ceiling leak began to fall slower, and men who had been standing under a gate for six hours came out and sat down and did not move for a quarter of an hour.

Adrian sat on a baulk with his back against cold stone and his knees up and his nose slowly stopping, and Roke came and stood over him and looked down at him for a long moment, and then crouched, and said four words, and Tamsin translated them.

"He says your join is true."

"That's—" Adrian stopped. "Thanks."

"He says it is true and that it is not his," Tamsin said. "He says you did not bring it. He says you were shown a line that was already there." She looked at him. "He would like that on the record, and he would like you to say it back to me, in that flat voice, in front of these men, because a thing that is said in a working is a thing the stone hears."

Adrian said it back.

It cost him something he did not have a name for, because he had spent six years building the belief that a good system was a thing you could stand outside of, and it had just been demonstrated to him, cheaply, by a man with a stick of chalk, that the moment a thing worked he was inside it.

---

It was Brann who went up to the yard first to call for the next watch shift, and Brann's voice in the yard is the reason nobody was watching the outside of the lock.

"There was a wall coming out of the chamber that nobody had looked at since my grandmother's time," Tamsin said, an hour later, when Adrian asked whether the outer channel had a walk-round. "There is a walk-round. It is two hundred steps, in this water, on a ledge, with a rail that is half rotten. Roke will do it at the change of the watch. You do not send a Hearthguard into a flooded gallery after ten hours on their feet. It is not brave, it is a wage bill."

She was right. He wrote it down. He was not the only person in the world who could learn things and be wrong about the timetable, and the relief of that was so large he had to put his notebook down for a moment.

The sound came at about the fifth hour, from the direction of the walk-round.

It was not a shout in the local language. He heard that later and understood that the local words were very fast and very high and there had been two of them.

It was a man shouting in English, from outside a wall two hundred steps away around a flooded corner, in a voice Adrian had heard in a thousand films and had never once heard outside a film, and it was saying: *—somebody, HEY, somebody, my legs—*

The air that came through the split in the outer wall smelled of asphalt and hot engine oil and dust.

It smelled like asphalt and hot dust on any Tuesday afternoon on any car park in any city he had never been to.
