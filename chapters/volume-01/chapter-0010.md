# Chapter 0010 — The Narrow Mark

The Lantern Concord met in what had been a salt store, and you could still smell it: the walls were white to the shoulder with old brine and the beams were black with four hundred years of smoke, and the benches were new, and the roll of public business was nailed to the north wall in a wooden frame with a strip of oiled cloth over it to keep the damp out of the ink.

There were two hundred and eleven people in the room. Adrian counted them from the doorway and then stopped counting, because Roke had told him at seven o'clock that there would be two hundred and eleven, and had been right, and had been right in a way that Adrian found difficult to sit with.

Sera Vail was at the long table with three other notaries and a man in a very good coat who had introduced himself as the Concord's assessor of strangers and had said the words *assessor of strangers* with a completely level face. The table had the ledger on it, open, and the ledger was the object of the entire morning: two hundred pages of pressed paper in a wooden cradle, chained at one end so it could not be carried off.

Tamsin stood at the back of the room with her arms folded, between Adrian and the door, and Owen Park stood beside her, and Mina Okoye sat down at the front on the end bench with the medical slate on her knees, and Adrian had not asked any of them to do that and none of them had needed to be asked.

The other five of them were on the fourth bench along, in a block, in the good coats they had been given on Tuesday and the smell they had not got out of, and Adrian had to look at them for a moment before he could think about anything else.

They were not a group. That was the thing he had to write down afterwards. Jonah Pike was at the front on his own, six feet from the Concord's table, asking the assessor of strangers a question about the receiving area in a voice pitched to carry, and getting an answer, and looking like a man who intends to be in a room like this for the next ten years. Priya Shah had a notebook out and was writing down the number of people in the room before she wrote down anything else. Dante Kuo had come in early and put himself by the door with his arms crossed, not on guard, just positioned, in the way of a man who has spent his life deciding where a crowd will go. Noor Haddad had found the ledger itself and was standing at the elbow of the long table, unobtrusively, in a position that let her see the pages, and was not reading them, and had not been asked to, and was not going to.

And Owen Park, who had slept about two hours in the three days since Tuesday, was standing up because he had something to say, and had been standing up for six minutes, and would keep standing up on and off for the next three hours.

None of them looked at Adrian for help. He had a long moment of not understanding what that was, and then he did understand it, and it went through him like cold water: they had stopped asking him.

He had slept for two hours and he was still bleeding from the ear, and he had a page in his notebook that he had written at four in the morning and had read eleven times since.

It said:

*What I want: a way home for six people who are not mine, a door that works, and a chair. I want the chair so much it is embarrassing. The chair is the part I must not take.*

And under that, in the flat hand, at the bottom of the page where he could not see it while he was standing up:

*Say yes first, then be honest about what it costs. Do not let them put me in a chair.*

---

Vail read the terms.

She did it standing, in the middle of the floor, with the slate turned so that people at the back could not see it, and she read it in the local language first and then in English, and she did not look at Adrian once while she read.

"Item one. The site. The seam in the outer wall of the channel below the Lock of Salt, closed by five witnesses at half past ten yesterday morning, under the hands of Ottoline Vask, Tamsin Quill, Roke of the outer ward, Ovin Sarr and Nell Bray, dockers of this city. The seam is eighteen inches. It is a socket. It is not a door and it is not a passage and it does not carry anybody. It is the place a door would be built into, if a door is ever built here."

Nobody in the room breathed for a moment, and a hundred and sixty of them were looking at the ceiling.

"Item two. The holder."

And here she stopped, and looked up, and this was the part Adrian had been dreading for eleven hours.

"The holder is the person who has agreed to pull the rope. The seat is named in the ledger, and the name is Adrian Vale, and the office is *holder of the pull*, and it means exactly this and nothing else: if the working is done and the witnesses are satisfied, the person in this seat is the person who is permitted to put a name into the opening, once, and only one name, and only a name that carries a return mark already set."

She let that sit for four seconds, which was two seconds more than it needed, and Adrian knew exactly why she had let it sit, because she had read the clause and she was the most careful reader in the city.

"The holder does not open the working. The holder does not close the working. The holder does not hold the join and does not speak the oath. The witnesses do those things, and only when the witnesses are satisfied, and only after an inspection, and the holder is told afterwards."

"And if the holder is not there?"

"Then the working is not done," said Vail. "Then the five witnesses may close it themselves and the seat falls vacant until the Concord fills it, and a vacant seat is not a fault. A seat that only one man can fill is a fault, and the man who fills it is the fault."

A hand went up at the back, a large hand belonging to a man in a good coat, and Vail nodded at it.

"Does the holder hold the mark?"

"Answer him," Tamsin said, in English, to Adrian, without turning round.

"He holds nothing," Adrian said. "The mark is mine and it is going into the stone and I am not going to have it back. If I have it in my hand, then the seat is a man with a private key, and a private key is a man who cannot be replaced and cannot be refused, and this room should throw me out right now."

"You are not going to be allowed to say what is good for you," Vail said, without any inflection. "That is not how this works either. Item two stands as read. Item three."

"Item three. The witnesses. Four names, each of whom signs for himself and may refuse for himself, and the refusal of any one of them stops the working: Tamsin Quill, anchorwright, keeper of the public bridge register. Ovin Sarr, docker. Nell Bray, docker. Hettis Roke, Hearthguard, of the outer ward, who signs also for the Hearthguard and may refuse the whole of the Hearthguard's share of it."

Four people came forward from the middle of the room and each of them wrote their own name on the slate in their own hand, and Ovin Sarr's hand was not entirely steady, and Adrian found that he was watching a nineteen-year-old docker's handwriting and feeling as though he had watched a man walk a long way to a thing he had not been sure was real.

"Item four. The receiving community."

Vail turned around and looked at the back of the room, and Adrian turned round too, and there were four people at the back of the hall he had not noticed, who had come in late, who were dressed for a four-day journey.

The oldest of them was a small dry woman of about seventy with wet reed-grass in the hem of her coat, and she stood up when Vail said the Reed Reach's name, and the entire room of two hundred and eleven people went completely silent, because everybody in Saltwake had known since Tuesday night that the water going out of the bottom field was going into the Reed Reach, and everybody in the room had known since Tuesday night that nobody from the Reach had come.

"The Reed Commons has read the terms of the site at the request of the keeper of the public bridge register, and has accepted the receiving of one named living traveller per opening, into the Reach, on the following condition." Vail read it. "The receiving is for the shared gates of the Reach and for the hamlets on them: Four Elms, Long Water, Silt Row, Corrow, Black Sluice, Nine Elms and the two gatehouses. Save for the water of Nine Elms, which is not received, and which is not a hamlet of the Reach's choosing to withhold, and which is a refusal made by the household of Nine Elms and reported here at their own word and not at the word of their assembly."

The old woman put her hand up and spoke, and Tamsin translated it in a low voice, and this is what the old woman said:

"Nine Elms is nine households and a school and they have lost a crop and a bridge twice. They are not against your door. They are against being the *only* ones it lands on. We did not know it would land on us until the water went out of our ditches on Wednesday, and we are not a village that can afford to be the one that agrees."

Somebody at the front of the room made a noise. Roke turned round very slowly.

"Is that lawful?" he said.

"That is lawful, Roke," said Vail. "That is the *whole* of item four. A receiving community that has agreed to part of the receiving area has agreed to part of it and not to the rest, and the rest is not received, and the term goes in with the rest of it not received, in the name of the household, spelled out, in the public book, forever, or until that household says otherwise in front of me."

"Forever."

"Not forever. Until they change it, and they can change it on a Tuesday, and if they change it the working stops at the next inspection and does not have to be argued about first." Vail put the chalk down. "Item four is entered. The reduced receiving area is entered. The refusal of Nine Elms is entered, by name, at the word of the household and not the word of the assembly, and I am going to say this once more slowly for the people at the back: Nine Elms is not receiving anybody, and the nine households of Nine Elms have not consented, and this working does not touch their water."

"Item five. The cost. Written and posted, in the same hand as the terms, where anybody in the hall can read it: opened salt, eleven measures a season, out of the Lock's own stores; the season of the bottom field, entered against Adrian Vale as a debt to eleven villages and to the Reed margin; and a shared labour shift, one day a month, taken by the witnesses and by the holder alike, out of the gate crew's rotation, so that the seat is not free."

She closed the slate.

"Those are the five parts. Any person in this hall may now object to any part of them by standing up and saying so, and the objection will be written in this book, and the objection does not have to be right, and an objection that is not answered is a thing the Concord must answer before it rules."

Nobody stood up for a while.

Then a young docker at the end of the second bench said, in the local language, loudly, and Tamsin translated it: "The stranger held this join yesterday and he could not finish it. He went down on the wall in front of us. Why is he the one holding the rope?"

"He went down because he tried to take it off five people," said Tamsin, "and because he had spent two hours being the only one who could see and he had got used to it, and I think that is a thing a man should be frightened of in himself, and so should he. He is in the seat because he is the only man in this hall who has a mark, and the mark has gone into the stone, and that is the whole of the reason, and I would have said no if I had thought it was a reward."

"You did think it was a reward."

"Yes," said Tamsin. "I did."

The young docker sat back down. Nobody stood up again for forty minutes.

---

It was in the middle of that silence, with two hundred and eleven people in a salt store being extremely careful with each other, that Adrian felt the pale field come up in the air in front of the roll on the north wall.

> YOU HAVE WORKED WELL.
>
> The closed place below can be opened and kept. It would carry one living person. It would not need a witness and it would not need a community and it would answer to you.
>
> The cost has been paid. There is nothing further to pay.
>
> This is earned. It is not yet accepted.

It stood there for a while. It was the longest the field had ever stayed.

The thing about it — the thing he would be asked about, later, by people who wanted to know how he had known — was not that it lied. It did not say the water would not move. It did not say the field would come back. It said *the cost has been paid*, and it said it in a voice that had never once, in three days, been wrong about a fact.

It was correct about the cost. The cost *had* been paid. A season of a bottom field, out of a hundred and forty acres, gone on Thursday, entered in a public book under his own name, enforceable, forever, or until the last day of the salt month. It had been paid. It was paid. He had watched Vail write it down.

And the voice said: *there is nothing further to pay.* Which was true, in the way that a lie is true when it is about a thing that has already been paid for. There was no further cost *listed*, because the cost of this was going to be paid by somebody else, in a field he had never seen, in a village called Nine Elms, in about nine months, by people who had not been asked, and whose refusal was sitting in a ledger with their name on it, and who would have no standing to object because they had already used their refusal up on the water.

He understood all of that in about four seconds, standing in a room full of people, and it took him about nine seconds to get his hand up, and it took Vail two full minutes to work out which of the two hundred and eleven people had just asked to speak, because Adrian had stood up and sat down again twice while waiting.

"Name," she said.

"Vale."

"State it."

"I want it in the book," Adrian said, "that I have been offered a thing this morning that would open the closed place and keep it open, and carry one living person, and require no witness and no community, and that it stated the cost had been paid, and that the cost has been paid, and I am recording that I have understood that this is the reason it was said to me, and that I am refusing it, and that my reason is not that it is dangerous."

"Which is?"

"That it is cheap," Adrian said. "That it is the cheapest offer anybody has made to me since Tuesday, and it is the only one that cost me nothing at all, and that is what a person should be most frightened of."

Marrow, on his bench at the back, wrote something down. He did not look pleased and he did not look sorry. He wrote it down, which Adrian found worse than either.

"The offer is entered as refused, and entered as understood, and entered as refused by the person holding the seat and not by the Concord," Vail said. "The seat is vacant for four minutes. Does anybody in this hall wish to fill it?"

Two hundred and eleven people looked at a man who had been in the city three days.

"Nobody," said Tamsin. "Which is the correct number of people and it is the correct answer and it is going in the book. The seat is filled by the man with the mark. That is the whole of the selection procedure and it is a stupid procedure, and I have written in the margin that it is a stupid procedure."

"It is in the margin," Vail agreed. Then she turned to Adrian, and for the first time all morning she looked at him as a person rather than as a line on a form. "Vale. Do you accept the seat?"

He had prepared three sentences for this on Tuesday night and had thrown all three away somewhere around the second hour of the lock.

"I accept it as a duty and not as a privilege," he said. "I accept that four people can stop it and that the receiving community can stop it, and that if I am gone for longer than a day the seat is vacant and stays vacant, and that nobody in this hall can be asked to trust me because I said a good thing in a good voice this morning. I accept the inspection. I accept the cost that is already in the book, and I accept that the season of that field is mine whether or not I ever pay it, because it stopped being anybody else's on Thursday at ten o'clock in the morning."

"Mine," said Vail. "Say the word the law uses."

"Enforceable."

"Good." She dipped the chalk. "The seat is filled."

He had not been given a chair. That was the thing he noticed afterwards, and wrote down, and underlined: he had been given a duty, a name in a chained book, a review date, and a debt, and he had to go and stand at the back of the room with the other six of them, and there was no chair anywhere in that building with his name on it, and there never would be.

---

Then they did the working, and Adrian did not do it.

That is the part of the account that every person who was in that hall gave afterwards, in three slightly different versions, all of them agreeing on the shape of it.

Tamsin Quill and the two dockers went out to the seam at eight o'clock in the morning, in daylight, in front of nobody but the four of them and a Hearthguard on the wall, because a working in this city that is worth entering in a public book gets *done*, and gets done by the people whose names are on it, and not performed for an audience. Vask went with them and the two masons. Roke took the inspection line: a rope with a weight on the end, held in his hand, running up and down the join the way a man runs a hand up and down a wall to feel whether the mortar is dry, which is what he said he was doing, and which may even have been true.

And Adrian stood on the walk-round with his right hand against the wall four inches to the left of the seam, and read the join out loud, in English, in the flat voice, so that Vask could hear a thing she could not see.

He could see it. That was the new part. He could see it, and nobody was owing it to anybody — no arch, no oath, no masons on a promise — and the wire was simply there under his palm because he had earned it on Wednesday morning by falling down on a wet wall in front of the whole of the Hearthguard of the outer ward, and the earning had not made him any more able to do it. He still could not make a working. He could only make the pattern visible enough for four other people to put their hands on.

"Six inches lower. No — hold. Hold. Left by a finger." He had it for ninety seconds and then it went, and he said, "It's gone," and Tamsin said, "Then it's gone. Vask, you have it?" and Vask said, "I have it," and they finished it without him.

The whole thing took eleven minutes.

At the end of it, Sesh Brack's cousin brought up a tray, because Sesh Brack was a woman who understood that a working that is entered in a public book has to be followed by something, and there was bread and salt and a pot of something hot on it, and two hundred and eleven people ate it in a salt store in the middle of the morning, which is not a thing Adrian had ever been part of before in his life and which he did not entirely know how to survive.

And at eight twenty on that morning, with four witnesses on the wall and Roke on the line and Vask's hands on the stone, Sera Vail did the last thing, and she did it herself, and she did not ask Adrian's permission because it was not his.

Adrian put out his hand. He turned the palm up.

And Vail took the heel of it in her left hand and pressed, once, firmly, with a small tool like a die that had a branching line cut in its face, into the lime and stone of the seam, in front of the witnesses, and said, in the local language, the words that go with a thing being put into a public place so that it stops being private.

The palm went cold. Not painfully. It went the way a thing goes cold when the attention that has been on it since Tuesday stops being on it.

He looked at his hand.

The pale mark was gone. The branch, the fork, the spur, the small clean absence in the middle: all of it, the whole shape of a permission that had no site and no witness and no receiving community anywhere in the world, was in the wall of the outer channel of a lock in a city he had never heard of, cut into lime, under a slab, with four names beside it.

What was left on his palm was a trace. You could see it if you knew what it was: a thin pale ghost in the heel of the hand, most of the forks gone, running out toward the wrist and stopping. It would be there in a year. It would be there in ten.

The black line was not part of that, and it had not gone into the stone. It was where Vask had found it on Wednesday night, two inches of it along the long axis of the heel, a hair's width across, exactly as wide. She had told him it would not move until somebody put a hand to it, and he had understood at the time that the word she had used was *until* and not *unless*, and that the whole of the difference between those two words was the content of his week.

It would be there when the field came up for review. It would be there when Tamsin checked, which she had told him on Wednesday in the rain she was going to do. He was not going to be able to sand it out, and nobody in this city was going to step over it and notice it, which was exactly as Vask had described, and he had thought at the time that she was being dramatic about a mason.

He could pick it up. That was the thing he understood with total clarity, standing on a wet walk-round in the rain with four exhausted people holding a rope he had not been holding.

The mark was the mark. It had never been in his pocket; it had been in his hand since Tuesday at half past two in the morning, and what was in the wall now was *his*, and it was the only thing in this world that was his. And the pale field in the hall had said, in as many words, that the closed place could be opened and kept, and would not need a witness and would not need a community.

It was a lie. Vail had said so in front of two hundred and eleven people an hour earlier, in the plainest sentences she had: a permission with no site was a fragment, and a fragment could not hold a door, and everything the wall had ever given him had arrived by accident, on Tuesday, in his own body, in a suit. He believed that completely. He had believed it in his chest.

He also understood, standing in the rain, that this was the part nobody in this city was in a position to check. A legal crossing has a site and four witnesses and a receiving community and a woman with a chained ledger, and if somebody opened one that had none of those things, then the two hundred and eleven people who would be standing in the water afterwards would be standing in the water arguing about it, and the whole enormous machinery this city had taken nine hundred years to build would collapse into a second-hand market stall and a man with a key.

And the wall in front of him was not even evidence. It could not be read by anybody else, could not be carried to a magistrate, could not be photographed, and could not be produced in any room at all except the one it had appeared in. He could have it back, and nobody here would be able to tell afterwards, and he had been in this world three days and had already watched two people lose things for want of a witness.

And if he took it back, the seat in the ledger would be a man with a key, and Vail had said so, out loud, in front of two hundred people, forty minutes ago.

He put his hand down.

---

The last thing that morning was not a working.

Vail read back the entry, and Tamsin read it back the second time, because Vail is a Concord notary and the anchorwright has been running a public bridge register since she was twenty-two and between them they could get a thing into a book that no single person could get into a book, and Adrian stood at the front of the hall with his notebook open and listened to his own name being read out in the past tense of a form he had not written.

"The Lock of Salt, outer channel seam, called the Narrow Mark. One named living traveller per opening, and only with a return mark already set and reviewed. A fresh inspection of the seam by the witnessing group before any further use. The receiving community is the Reed Commons, for a reduced area, save for the water of Nine Elms, which is not received, and the refusal of the household of Nine Elms is entered here by name and may be withdrawn by them at any time. The cost is posted: opened salt, the season of the bottom field, one shared labour shift a month. The holder of the pull is Adrian Vale, and the seat may be suspended by the receiving community and may be filled by no one but the man whose mark is in the stone."

"And the recognition," Vail said, "is *incomplete*."

The hall made a noise.

"So say the whole of it," said Roke, from the middle of the floor, in the voice of a man who had been in hearings before.

"So I will." Vail put her hand flat on the open ledger, which was a gesture Adrian had watched her make three times in three days and had come to understand as the single most reassuring thing he had ever seen. "A site: yes. A holder: yes. Witnesses: four, all signed, all standing here. A receiving community: the Reed Commons, for part of the Reach, and not for the whole. A cost: posted. So the instrument is whole except at one point, and at that point the community has not accepted the whole of the receiving area, and I am told by the law I keep that a community which has accepted part of a receiving area has not accepted a license at all. It has accepted a smaller arrangement, and a smaller arrangement is not a license, and this thing is not a license."

"Then what is it?"

"It is written, argued, costed, posted, and signed by four people who may each refuse," Vail said, "and it cannot be used."

She looked up.

"The Concord has opened the record on the question whether a first license may exist in this city at all. That question is open from this morning. It will be heard. It has no date, because I am not going to give one of you a date to wait for. When the Concord has heard it, you will know, because it will be in the book." She closed the ledger and put the chain through it. "And in the meantime there is a shape in a wall, and four names beside it, and a debt with a review date, and not one single person can use any of it. That is what we have. I did not want it. I am obliged to tell you that I did not want it and that I have read every argument for it twice, and that I am the person who will have to be asked to rule on the license, and I would like it written in the book that I did not want it."

"It is written in the book," said the Concord assessor of strangers, who had been taking notes the entire time and had not looked up once, and whose pen had not stopped moving even while Vail was reading the terms aloud.

Then Owen Park stood up in the aisle.

He was bad at it. He was six foot two and out of a factory and he had a scalp wound and he stood up in the middle of two hundred and eleven people and said, in English, with a horrible accent and total sincerity:

"Does anybody in this building have, in writing, anywhere, anything at all about a light?"

Nobody laughed, which surprised him.

"Because I've got one," he said, "and I know nobody else sees it, and I know what that makes me, and I have thought about it for two days straight, and I would like it in the book that there is a light and that I am not calling it a miracle or a relic or whatever you want to call it, I am just saying there is a shape behind the stone and I have seen it twice and it's the same shape both times, and if I am wrong then I am wrong and it is in the book that I said so."

Vail looked at him for a moment. "Your name?"

"Owen Park. I came through the wall. I was the second—"

"You were the first," said Mina Okoye, from the front bench, without turning round. "Englishman, tell them."

"You were the first," Adrian said. "I had your collar. You came out through one shoulder and you were shouting about your legs and there was blood in your hair, and you weighed about what I weigh now."

"Right," said Owen Park. "First. Put that in the book, because I will get it wrong again."

"Your statement is entered as *unverified*, by the person named, in his own words, and it stays in the book unverified, and it is not taken out of the book, and it does not become more true because two hundred people have heard it." She dipped the chalk. "There. If it turns out to be real, the book will be the reason anybody believes you. Do you understand that? Not me. The book."

"Yeah," said Owen Park, and sat down, and put his face in his hands for a second, and then took his hands away and looked around the room at two hundred and eleven people who had all just been told, in a cold voice, by a woman with a chain on a ledger, that a thing they had not seen was in the record anyway.

And that, Adrian thought, standing at the front of a salt store with a thin pale trace in the heel of his hand and a thin black line under it that nobody else in the building could see, was the entire difference between this world and his own, and it was already winning.

---

Everyone left at once, the way two hundred people do.

Adrian got out into the yard, into the rain, and did not get ten feet before Tamsin came after him, and she did not say anything for a while, and then she said:

"You are going to be very careful now, and everybody is going to call it humility, and in about five weeks one of my people is going to come to me and say that the man in the seat is different from the man who came out of the wall, and they will be partly right, and I want you to know that I will be checking, and that checking is not distrust, and that you have made it possible for me to check by agreeing to be checked, and that this is the first time in seven years of keeping this register that anybody has made it possible for me to check anything."

"I know," Adrian said. "I wanted the chair."

"I know you did. Marrow told me you would and Marrow was right about that as well, and I have written that down too, in the same book, in a hand that is not his, so that it is on the record that a man told me in advance that I would want to be permanent, and that I wrote it down, and that the record of a prediction made for a reason is worth more than the record of a thing done without one."

She stood in the rain in front of him and looked at him for a long moment, and Adrian understood that this was the entirety of what she was going to give him this week, and that it was a great deal more than he had any right to expect, and that he was not going to get anything else from her by asking.

"Nine Elms," she said. "Do you understand what has just happened to those nine households?"

"They have refused, and it is in the book, and nobody can use their water without their say."

"They have refused and it is in the book, and in about four months one of us is going to be desperate enough to argue that a refusal given in a hearing is not a refusal given in a *commitment*," she said, "and on that day I am going to get up in a room and read the date and the words back, and it is going to be you in the seat, and you are going to have to decide what you do."

"I know."

"You do not know yet. You know the shape of it. It is a different thing." She put her hood up. "Dinner is at the loft at seven. Owen's scalp is not healing and Mina is angry about it, and she will be angrier, not less, if you come down and tell her it is probably nothing. Bring the notebook, because I want the timeline out loud where the others can hear it, and bring the page with the five items on it, because the first person who is going to try to move the whole of this in one direction instead of five is a member of this room, and it is not going to be a man from the other side of the world."

---

Marrow was waiting at the bottom of the stair to the lock, in the rain, in the good grey coat, with his book in his hand.

"You gave up the mark," he said.

"I put it in the stone."

"You *gave it up*." He said it with something that was not quite admiration and not quite grief. "Vale, in eleven years I have watched two men give up a mark. One of them was a liar and one of them was a coward, and I have spent a great deal of my professional life being able to tell the difference, and I would like you to know that it took me some time and that I have now put you in the second category and I do not think you belong in it." He opened the book and did not read from it. "There is one thing I am going to say and then I am going to leave you alone, because you have had a very long two days and you have earned an evening with no lessons in it."

"Say it."

"The mark in that seam is a socket," Marrow said. "A socket is a shape. And a shape is a promise that somebody, somewhere, one day, in a city that is not this city, is going to want a door. And when that happens — and I have lived long enough to be sure it will, because there is always somebody — you will be the man in the book. Not the man who holds the rope. The man *in the book*, next to the name of the rope-holder, in the only volume anybody will ever open to find out what happened here."

He closed the book.

"You will be the narrow mark in the seam," he said. "That is all. I merely wanted you to hear it from me first, so that whatever you do about it, you do it knowing that somebody has already written it down."

He went up the stair into the rain.

Adrian stood at the bottom of it in the wet, alone, with a thin pale trace in the heel of his hand, and did the thing he had been doing since Tuesday and that he would go on doing for a very long time, which was to open the notebook to a clean page and write, in the flat hand, at the top:

*Held, not solved. One empty socket. One debt. Two hundred and eleven people, one of whom can see something I cannot see. The gate is wider than it was on Tuesday.*

And then, lower down, in smaller writing, the honest version:

*Nobody in this world can get home. Including me. And the first thing that has actually been given to us is a list of names, and the second is a book with a chain on it, and I would trade every permission anybody has ever offered me for the second one, and I do not think that is humility, and I do not think it is modesty, and I think if I write it down somewhere nobody reads, then it is at least true.*
