# Chapter 0040 — Nobody Is Going Down to Fetch Anybody

There is no such thing as a dead man in a market for more than about two days in Saltwake. It took the morning of the fortieth and it took four markets and a number, and the number was nineteen, and nobody had put it there, and by the ninth hour of the morning of the fortieth day of the flood there was a version of it in about six mouths in this city and it was not the same version in any of them.

---

It began on the Saturday morning, in a market, on a board, and Adrian Vale did not find out about it until about the seventh hour of that same morning, and the reason he did not find out about it is that he had been in a hall in a converted salt store since the first light of the Friday, being read four pages of his own record to about three hundred and fifty people, and then put in a room and made to sign a page about three rooms he did not choose, and he had slept about two hours on a bench behind the salt store and had not been outside the building.

The board in the river market on the twenty-sixth day of this flood had carried the number **eleven** for thirteen days, in a market of forty stalls, and Adrian had never been able to get it off, and on the Saturday morning of the fortieth day somebody had taken it down.

What was on the board at the seventh hour of the Saturday was a different number, and under it, in a different hand, four words.

**19**

*how many are there*

Nobody in Saltwake put that number there. Adrian established that within about four hours and it is in the book, and the finding is this: the board belongs to a man who sells candles, the man has had a board for eleven years, and there were four markets in this city where a number appeared on a board in a flood month without anybody being able to say who wrote it, and the third of those four is on the twenty-sixth and the fourth is on the fortieth, and the man who sells candles has entered in his own words that he did not do it either time and that he has stopped asking because the answer is always the same and the answer is *it is not me and it is on my board*.

Four versions of the number were in the city by the ninth hour and all four of them are in the book because a person wrote them down and a person read them back, and they are:

**One.** That nineteen is the number of people who come out of a floor. This was in four mouths and no board. It is wrong and it is not the fault of anybody who said it, because the arithmetic is available to anybody who has been in a market: eleven came out of the chapel floor on the twenty-fifth, and eight are of the second group, and eight is the number of items on a numbered paper of eight things that a boy of nineteen brought back out of a wall in eleven seconds and whose numbers run 1 to 4 and then back to 1 and 2 and 1, and there is a man of twenty-four in this city who has said out loud in a yard that eleven people are going to come out every time the thing wants something, and the number on the board has gone up by eight and nobody can say who added it.

**Two.** That nineteen is the number of stones on the upper road above the salt works that carry writing nobody living can read.

**Three.** That nineteen is a number of graves.

**And four** — and this one was only in two mouths and one of them was a man who does not sell candles — that nineteen is the number of people who are going to go down, when somebody finally opens the thing properly, and there are four of them saying it and none of them can say where they have got it and all four of them say they heard it in a market.

---

The yard under the lock chapel on the morning of the fortieth day of the flood had about two hundred and forty people in it at about the ninth hour, and the reason it had two hundred and forty people in it is that a city in a flood month puts everybody who has lost somebody in the one room where the thing happened, and there was only one room, and it was a yard with a tarpaulin on four feet of floor in it.

Nyla Ferrow had the boards up over the hole at about the seventh hour. It is the first time since the twenty-fourth day of this flood that the ground has been closed, and the boards are the same boards and there are two new ones because Vask would not have boards that had been up over that hole go back down, and the boards are chalked with the date on the underside, and the underside is what will be looked at in four hundred years.

Vask did not come down. She went up the two hundred steps of the walk-round at about the sixth hour of the morning of the fortieth and sat on a step with a bar across her knees, and she was still there at about the ninth hour, and about nine people from this city went up and sat with her and about nine went away again, and nobody has ever established which nine, and Vail entered that.

The binding is said twice a day at six and at six and it has no name at the top of it, and on the morning of the fortieth day of the flood the yard was empty at ten to six for about ninety seconds, which is the first time in twenty-eight days that it has been empty, and about nine people in this city know the fifth of the five words properly and two of them were not near the yard at ten to six because a city that has lost a man does not stand in a yard at six in the morning.

Nobody stepped into it for about ninety seconds.

And then a woman of fifty-eight with a bowl in each hand came up the market steps and stood under the arch and said the four words and the fifth, in the local language, and put the fifth in the middle, and it took on the first attempt, and about thirty people who were in the yard at the time said afterwards that they had not known she could do it, and that they had seen her do it once, in a porch, in the second week, with nine people in it.

And after that about six other people said it, in a yard, in the open, in the rain, within about ten minutes of each other, and nobody made a list, and Vail entered the number in the yard and entered that no list exists and entered the reason, which is her own: *and this is entered so that nobody in nine years can point at a page and say the rule was broken. The rule was not broken. The rule was met by a person who had learned it.*

---

They had been at the top of the two hundred steps since about the ninth hour.

**Sabra Vint** is sixty-one, of the upper road, in a house with a burnt lintel, and she has kept a sheet of paper for nine years on which she records the shapes of the nineteen stones every year, and she walked down two hundred wet steps in the rain on the morning of the fortieth day of a flood with the sheet in a waxed case, and she had not been asked to come, and she came because nineteen had been said twice in a market in nine days and a man had died, and in nine days it would be a third time, and the third time is the time a thing becomes a number somebody can act on.

She is a Veyran, she is sixty-one, she has lived above the salt works for forty-one years, and she has never once said in this city that this is a game or that anybody is a player, and the order in which this city finds out what is on the nineteen stones is going to be decided in about nine days by about six mouths in a market, and that has to be somebody's fault, and it is not going to be hers, and she knows that.

She got as far as the top of the stair and Roke was standing in the archway with his waxed slate and he was not ready.

That is the fact. **Hettis Roke**, a Hearthguard of the outer ward, nineteen years in, forty-four years old, broad, grey-bearded, who has said more true things in yards in five weeks than anybody in this city in ten years, and who has a live debt of his own that he has not told anybody about and has not been asked about and has not been able to pay in five weeks, and it is this: there are eleven graves in the ordinary ground behind the ordinary wall where his grandfather is buried, and his grandfather has no name stone, and eight of the eleven have no name stone, and Roke has never once asked why, and Sabra Vint has nineteen stones with writing on them that nobody living can read, and she has never in nine years been able to make nineteen and eight be one number.

He was not ready because he has eight stones in a ground and a sheet of paper in a waxed case, and because a man died on Thursday and he was in the room, and because the two things have been eleven feet apart in his own head for nine years and a woman of sixty-one has just walked down two hundred steps with the other one.

"Ma'am," he said, in the local language. "You will want the yard and the yard is full and there is a man in it who died on Thursday and about two hundred and forty people are standing about nine feet from a tarpaulin. Come back on Monday."

"No," said Sabra Vint.

"It is not a refusal, it is a building."

"Mr Roke." She did not raise her voice. She had a waxed case and a sheet of paper and forty-one years above a salt works. "I have been coming down to that yard nine times in nine years and I have always come on a Sunday and I have always come when there is nothing in it, and I have come nine times and asked nine times and been given a woman with a bowl and a page, and I have never been refused and I have never been given the sheet.

"And there is a number on a board in the river market this morning and it is the number of the stones and there is a man dead who went into a floor in that city and every person in this yard knows which number came first and in which mouth. **I am not here about the man. I would not dare come about the man. I am here because in about nine days this city is going to be able to say that a man went into a hole because of nineteen, and I have been writing nineteen on a piece of paper every year since I was fifty-two, and I am sixty-one, and I am not going to let that happen to a number I have kept.**"

And Roke stood in an archway and did not have an answer, and about forty people on the steps behind her were waiting to see what a Hearthguard of nineteen years did, and he did not have one, and he said so, in front of them, in about nine words, and that is in the book:

"I have not got an answer and I have had a live one of my own for nine years and I have never asked it, and I am not going to stand in an archway and ask a woman of sixty-one in the rain about my own eight stones while there is a tarpaulin in this yard. I am going to say the true thing and then I am going to do the other thing and the true thing is that I do not know what is on your paper and I have wanted to know for nine years and I have not asked, and the other thing is that you are going to stand in this yard at about the seventh hour this evening with about two hundred people in it and I am going to read your paper out, and I have never read it and I am going to read it, and if I cannot read it then I will say so in a yard and it will be entered."

He read it, at about the seventh hour of the evening of the fortieth day, in a yard, with about two hundred and ten people in it, and it took about nineteen minutes, and it is nineteen shapes, and Adrian Vale was there, and did not understand one of them, and the paper has been to a man in a good coat and to a woman of twenty-nine who reads Crown Law, and to a mason of thirty-four, and to a notary, and four of them have said they cannot read it, and one of them has said that she can read two words of the nineteenth and would rather die than say so, and that sentence is not in the book because she would not let anybody write it down, and Roke entered the fact that she said it.

And the shapes are not writing in any language anybody in that yard has, and the entry says so, and the entry also says — in Vask's words, in her own hand, and she came down off the walk-round for it and went straight back up — **that nineteen of them are the same shape and eight of them are not, and that the eight are further down the road, and that the eight are in a different order, and that this is a fact about stones and not a fact about a man's handwriting, and that a mason would read a joint in a wall in nine seconds and cannot read a joint in a stone from this road, and that this is the first time in thirty-four years I have been asked to look at something I cannot get my hands on.**

---

Roke said the sentence at about the eighth hour of the evening of the fortieth day, in a yard, with about two hundred and ten people in it, and about half of them had watched a man go into a floor on the morning of the Thursday, and about a hundred and forty of them were there for a woman of sixty-one's nine-year sheet of paper and had come for that and were still there.

"**Nobody is going down to fetch anybody.**"

It was the same four words and it was the third time anybody in this city has heard them and the first time they have been said about a man who died.

"Twice before now," he said, to the yard, "and both times I have said it and both times I have been asked who I was saying it to and I have had an answer, and I have had no answer tonight and I am going to say the sentence anyway because I said it on the twentieth day of this flood in a yard with a bar and a dig in it and I was right and I have never taken it back and I am not going to take it back on a Saturday.

"It is the worst possible use of that sentence and it is the only correct one. I have been a Hearthguard for nineteen years and I have said that sentence in a yard twice about a hole and I am saying it tonight about a man, and if you would like me to be embarrassed about it then I am, and I am going to keep saying it, because the alternative is that this city works out a way of making a sentence that will let somebody go down, and the sentence that lets somebody go down is always the sentence with a man in it, and there is a woman of sixty-one standing at the front of this yard who has just spent nineteen minutes reading a paper that four people in this city cannot read and one of whom would rather die than say so."

Adrian Vale stood up in the second row and said, in English, and then in the local language, and got it right, and Tamsin Quill did not correct him:

"I am the holder of the pull. I want that sentence in the book tonight, in his words, with the hour, and I want it entered that the man in the book stands in this yard and agrees with it, and I want it entered that I have been trying to say a version of it to a room since Wednesday night and I have not managed it in two days.

"And I want the entry to say the other half as well, because he said the first half and everybody in this yard heard the second half and nobody wrote it down. **The reason that sentence is the only correct one is that this city has known for nine days that a man could be sent and did not stop it, and the only thing standing between this city and a man with a rope and a reason is four words in a yard, and there is no instrument in this world that can be relied on instead of four words in a yard, and I have spent thirty-nine days trying to build one.**"

And a docker of about sixty in a felt hat stood up in the fourth row and said the thing that is in the book under nine numbers of its own, and Nell Bray is the docker who defined what a seat is, and who made a crew say the maintenance oath at its ordinary volume in a gallery on the twenty-fifth day of this flood so that the day could not later be called a thing a crowd did, and who asked Adrian Vale in the rain in a yard in front of a hundred and forty people what the mark in the wall was for, and who has not been thanked for either of those things and has not asked to be.

"A man is dead," she said, in English, and then in the local language, and Tamsin translated it and said afterwards that she had not needed to and did it anyway because it is the rule and the rule is not a courtesy. "And the two men who have just said that sentence are the two men in this yard with the most to lose by it, and I have been in this city fifty-one years and I know what a man's face looks like when he is being careful in a yard, and both of those faces are being careful, and I am not having it.

"You have both said a true thing and neither of you has said the other true thing, and the other true thing is standing at the front of this yard and it is a man of sixty-one with a piece of paper, and it is *three*.

"It was the breach and it was the door and it was the lock and there is a fourth thing and the fourth thing is that on Thursday morning at about ten to four there were two men on a floor in this city and one of them was in the terms and one of them was not, and the one that was not in the terms is a boy of nineteen who is standing at the back of this yard with his right hand in his pocket and who voted for it and who has never once been asked by anybody in this city to explain why he put a hand on a stone he was not named in.

"And that is not *nobody is going down to fetch anybody*. That is *somebody was sent, and a second somebody went because a thing is held at two ends, and the first somebody was sent because two hundred and forty people voted and one of them was asked and the man who was asked did not say the word.*" She turned round. "I am not saying the man in the book is the reason. He has been in a book for thirty-nine days and there are four hundred people in this city who are angry at him and about nine of them are right and I am one of the nine and I am not going to say it out loud tonight because a man died and it does not help.

"I am saying that a sentence about the future is not a rule, and a rule is a thing that can be argued with, and a sentence is a thing that gets repeated, and this city has had four days to repeat it and about nine people in six markets have already said *somebody is going to go down and fetch somebody*, and that sentence is going to be here on Monday.

"So I am against both of you and I am telling you why in a yard instead of a market. And the thing I am telling you is that a man in this city is dead and the record of why is four pages long and it is the only thing anybody in this city has got, and **a record is not a fence.** A record tells you afterwards. A fence is there before. And what this city has got is four pages, and the four pages did not stop a man from going into a hole, and if somebody stands up in this yard in nine months and says *I want to go and fetch somebody* then the four pages will be read and the four pages will say *he was sent, and a second man went because a thing is held at two ends, and the man in the book did not say the word*, and the four pages will be *true*, and about a hundred people will agree with the four pages and the man will still go, because the four pages are true and a true thing has never in my life stopped anybody from doing anything."

And at about half past nine in the evening, in a yard, with about a hundred and forty people still in it because a docker in a felt hat had said a thing and a man with a waxed slate had read nineteen shapes off a sheet of paper and nobody had gone home, a pale field came up in the air in front of Adrian Vale in the colour of a paper form, and stood there for about twenty-two seconds, and about eleven people in that yard saw it happen.

> **AN ENTRY THAT IS WRONG MAY BE ENTERED AGAINST ANY ENTRY IN THE BOOK.**
>
> **The person named must answer it in public, or withdraw it, or stand by it in public and say that they can say no more.**

It was almost word for word the procedure this city entered on the thirty-first day of this flood and has used three times, and the third clause was in the book in a notary's hand and a woman's phrase, and it had been in a room with about four hundred and ten people in it nine days ago, and it was the single most careful thing anybody in this city has ever built, and it had arrived in a yard in the rain, in a pale field, in a language built out of Adrian Vale's own.

"No," said Adrian Vale, out loud, in English, in a yard, to about a hundred and forty people, in under a second.

Tamsin Quill was standing four feet away and she said: "You said that very fast. Say it slowly and say what it is."

"It is a record procedure," he said. "It is a version of what a notary in this city invented nine days ago and it has been used three times, and it arrived in this yard tonight in a field in my own language, which means the thing that produces it has been in a room with about four hundred people in it, and it has read the book, or it has been in this city longer than anybody in it."

"And the cost," said Tamsin Quill.

"The cost is in the third clause, and it is a beautiful clause and I want to say that before I say anything else about it. *Or stand by it in public and say that they can say no more.* That is the best piece of drafting anybody has done in this world in five weeks and a woman with a bowl and a man who writes in a small hand did it between them. And it means that a person may finish. It means that a record may be closed, by the person it names, in public, and that after that the entry stands and nobody may move it, and everybody in the room may go home, and the thing has been *had*.

"And there were three accounts in this yard this morning about why a man died and all three of them are an entry that says *no more*." He looked at the tarpaulin over the boards. "A man was sent. A door was opened. Forty men said four words. Every one of those is a sentence that lets a city stop asking, and every one of them is a sentence that is going to be said in a market by Monday, and the reason they are going to be said in a market by Monday is that this yard has just been offered a procedure for saying them officially and putting them in a book and being finished.

"Four hundred people in this city found a procedure nine days ago for being wrong in public, and it has been used three times, and tonight a thing arrived in a field in my language with a clause in it that lets a person say *I have said everything I have*, and I am not letting it in. Because a record is not a fence, and a notary in this city has been saying that since the thirty-first day of this flood without knowing it, and the sentence I want to keep on the page is hers and it is *say it out loud again*, and *again* is the whole of it, and **a procedure that lets a person say they can say no more is a version lock with better manners.**"

Nobody in that yard could check any of it. Ivenn Marrow was eleven feet away with a slate under his arm and he had been in this city for eight weeks and he said, in English, in a level voice, to a man who had just refused something in public in front of a hundred and forty people:

I know what you are all doing and I have been doing it too, and I have been in eleven rooms this month where a sentence of mine was the reason a man did a thing, and I have entered every one of them, and I am not going to be the last man in this city who says *not me*. I have no idea whether it is true and I have told you for eight weeks that I have no idea and I have been getting more use out of that sentence than anybody should be allowed to get."

"Then say the other thing," said Adrian.

"I cannot," said Ivenn Marrow, "and that is the first true sentence I have said in this city in front of a hundred and forty people and I would like it entered if anybody has a slate."

Two letters were written on the fortieth day of the flood and neither of them was sent, and both of them are in a notebook and neither of them is in a book, and the reason neither of them is in a book is that a book is a thing other people can act on and neither of these is yet.

The first is to a woman of twenty-five in Camden, in the flat above a locksmith's, who runs a rota on Tuesdays and Thursdays and Saturdays at the Camberwell Larder and who does not know.

The second is to a woman called **Hattie Park**, of Dunedin, in another hemisphere, who is a sister and who does not know either, and whose brother asked in a room by a salt wharf on the afternoon of the thirty-eighth day of this flood for somebody in this world to write to her, and a man in a good coat offered, in the yard, at about the ninth hour of the evening of the fortieth, in about thirty seconds and entirely reasonably, to have it carried through a Crown channel out of Aurel.

"He is very good at this," Adrian wrote, "and it is the most frightening offer anybody has made me in this world and it is the ninth time he has been the most useful man in a room in a month, and there is no version of refusing it that is not also a way of letting it stand. I did not refuse. I said I would think about it and he said *of course*, and he has not mentioned it since, and that is the correct thing for him to do and it is the reason I have written it down."

Neither letter can go. There is no route in this world that a letter can travel, and the road goes about four miles to Verrow and the road does not go to a ship, and the ship goes to Aurel and Aurel is the other end of the Registry's demand, and a letter in a Crown channel about a man who went into a floor in a yard in Saltwake is a letter that a clerk reads before it goes on a boat.

And the Registry's demand is live and dated to a quarter, and it is not about a dead man and it does not mention a dead man, and it requires either a registered holder with a Crown-recognised workway and a Crown advocate, or a statement of permanent closure, and the answer this city gave on the tenth day of the new month was neither and was in its own words and not in the form required, and the next quarterly sitting is in about a hundred and ten days, and a man in this city has a debt with no figure on the end of it and a registry with a date on one, and has not worked out yet which of the two is going to arrive first.

And then at about ten minutes past midnight on the fortieth day of the flood, Adrian Vale went down two hundred steps to the lower gallery of the Lock of Salt, alone, with a lamp and a folding steel rule, because he had been going down there on and off for thirty-nine days and was not going to stop on the day after a man died, and because there is a number chalked on the wall of the second bay that is the only number in this city that nobody argues with.

The tide of the thirty-third was last Saturday and it was a small one and it was an ordinary one, and forty men of this city's own crew said four words under the near gate twice, at their ordinary volume, out of the line and not together, and the box in the last column of the tide table was filled in on the Saturday morning by a docker with a barrow, and the word in it is the ordinary word, and the *k* is pressed too hard, and nobody has ever asked her what she decided and she has told about nine people in a market that she decided nothing and that on Tuesday she will not be able to say what she wrote.

**Eight and five-eighths** was the number on the thirty-third.

He put the rule into the crack and read it, and it is **eight and five-eighths**, and it is the same, and the four chalked marks have not moved, and the number on the wall of the second bay under the docker's nine is in his own hand and is going to be there in the winter, and he wrote the twenty-second reading underneath it and the twenty-second reading is the twenty-second number in a series that began on the second night of a flood and has a ruled line through every night he did not go down, which makes it the only honest record anybody in Saltwake has kept of anything at all, and a crack does not step, and the number does not move on an ordinary night, and there is no tide on the fortieth and the fortieth is a Saturday and there was no working at the lock today and there has not been a working at the lock since the twenty-fifth.

The next tide is on the forty-third day, which is a Tuesday, and it will be a working one, and the last column of the tide table has an empty box against it, and the box against the thirty-third stood empty for six days and a woman with a barrow filled it in on the Saturday morning, and the box against the forty-third has been empty for seven since, and nobody asked her and she will not be able to say why on Tuesday.

Adrian Vale stood in the lower gallery of the Lock of Salt at ten past midnight on the fortieth day of the flood with a rule in his hand and read a number that had not changed and looked at an empty box on a board four feet above his head, and did not write anything in it, and did not fill it in, and there is a note in the book at the twelfth hour of the fortieth saying that a man came down two hundred steps at ten past midnight and read the number and did not write in the box, and that this is entered because a date in a book is a place a plan has to be legible in, and that an empty box on a tide table is the only place in this city where nothing is claimed by anybody, and that on the forty-third day of this flood a man is going to come down those steps with a stick of chalk and it is not going to be me, and it is not going to be a woman with tweezers, and it is not going to be a man in a good coat.

It is going to be whoever is holding the chalk.

And the last act in this city is not going to be his, and it is not going to be a wall, and it is not going to be a road, and it is not going to be a book.

It is going to be a man with a stick of chalk, on a wet Tuesday morning, in a gallery under a lock, writing one word in a box, because a lock is a thing that has to be worked, and a field is a thing that has to be paid for, and a man who died in a yard on Thursday is a thing that has to be entered in a book and read out at a grave and not summarised, and there is no instrument in this world that can do any of it, and there is a woman of thirty-one from Nine Elms who asked in a room over a boat-yard on the seventh day of the new month whether there is a person on the other side of a floor, and there is not one anybody in this world can name, and she is right to have asked, and she will ask it again, and it is going to be the question the whole of the rest of this is about, and a man in a good coat has said in a yard, reasonably, that the only way to close a join in a gate is to give it to one person, and a notary has written on a page that the two documents that say so do not agree with each other, and a docker in a felt hat has said in a yard that a record is not a fence, and Hettis Roke has said four words in front of about two hundred and ten people and been told by about forty of them that it was the worst possible use of the sentence and that they were keeping it, and a man who asked for nothing in a room for thirty-eight days is under a board in the ordinary ground with a real name on him and a four-page account of his own death in his own dictation, and the injury that killed him is the injury that was never set, and it is written down, and it will be read out on the Sunday, and nobody will be able to make it shorter.

*works*, says the box against the thirty-third, in a chalk hand that is not his.

The box against the forty-third is empty.
