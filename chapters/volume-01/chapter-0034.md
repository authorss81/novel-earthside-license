# Chapter 0034 — The Man Who Was Available

There are about two hundred things a city at a lock has to do on an ordinary Tuesday in a flood month, and Adrian Vale did eleven of them, and got two of them wrong, and neither of the two was a thing anybody noticed until the Thursday.

---

He came up the bank at about half past four on the thirty-sixth day of the flood with a sixteenth-year-old girl and a woman of twenty-nine and a paper of flour-merchant's writing signed by himself, and the first thing that happened to him in this city was that nobody was waiting for him, and the second thing was that this was an improvement on the twenty-eighth.

The binding in the yard is said twice a day, at six and at six, in the open, in any weather, by whoever is nearest, and at about ten to six on the Tuesday evening the nearest was Owen Park, who said the four words and the fifth, and put the fifth in the middle, and it worked on the first attempt, and about sixty people were in the yard and about a hundred of them did not know they had watched anything.

Nyla Ferrow, at the arch, with the lime bucket, said: "It took you twenty days to learn where the word goes and you have said it eleven times since. Nobody has thanked you and nobody is going to. I am the only person in this yard who is going to say anything about it and I am saying it once."

"That is more than I expected."

"It is more than anybody in this yard has got. Go and do your page." She put the trowel down. "You are the holder of the pull and it is the second item of the cost of your own seat and there is a page in the book that has not been written up as well as any other page in it since the twenty-sixth and I have stopped mentioning it, and I have stopped mentioning it four times, and I am mentioning it now."

---

The page is the eleventh page of the book of the Lantern Concord and it has been open since the twenty-sixth day of the flood and it is the second item of the cost column of the seat, and the item is this, in the words Sera Vail read out in a hall of three hundred and ten people on the thirtieth:

> **A new page in the ledger, opened for the eleven persons who came out of the ground under the yard of the lock chapel on the twenty-fifth day, and kept in the same book and by the same notary, and the holder of the pull may not sit in that seat if that page is not written up as well as any other page, and may not move it to a different book.**

Adrian had understood what it was when it was read, and had written in a notebook that it was the best of the three items, and had gone to a hall and a counter and a ledger about eleven times in seven days. It is the only one of the three he has ever been able to do, and it has cost him about four hours a day for a week, and the page is still behind every other page in the book.

It is the page with the eleven names on it, entered as each person gives them and not as a neighbour gives them, with a refusal clause in the second paragraph and an express bar on the words *order, company, party, class, cohort, settlement, faction, rescue* in the third, and four columns.

The four columns are these: **name as given. What the person is owed by this city, being four things, and the day each was answered. What the person has said about themselves, in their own hand or by dictation, and where it is. And whether the person wishes the entry to stand.**

And at the bottom of the page, in the eighth week of a flood, there is a fifth column, which Vail entered on the twenty-ninth day because Teodor Blaz could not spell the local language and Priya Shah would not have a row of a page that eleven people could not read in a room where they might be asked about it.

The fifth column is a **thing the person said they saw**, and the column exists because the form on the loft wall says that a person is allowed to enter one, and nobody in this city was ever going to use a form on a wall and so the form came to the book instead, which is a thing Sera Vail did on purpose and which she has not explained to anybody and which Adrian has thought about for six days.

**Nadia Brandt**, 29: a room, no window, the light from everywhere, no shadow. She has not struck her name.

**Kirsty Lund**, 16: the sound, like a page, every forty seconds, from above and behind, and nobody could find where. She has not struck her name. Beside it, in another hand, in a school exercise book, is the account of a question on a road at the sixth hour of the evening of the eleventh day of the new month, and Vail entered that too, and the entering of it took nine minutes and no explanation.

**Gareth Mbeki**, 27: took a docker's shoulder without being asked, and apologised to it for two hours.

And on the bottom line, on the Tuesday, in the fifth column, in a hand that is not one of the eleven and is not Adrian's, and entered by Sera Vail from a dictation taken in a room by the salt wharf by Hesper Saye, who does no private work and who took the dictation because the man would not come to the notary:

> **A light in the water at the bottom of a reed channel below the second gate at Long Water, on the thirty-sixth day of the flood, at about the second hour of the afternoon, seen by one man, named Gwil Merrivale, forty-four, of Silt Row, a reed cutter and a ditch hand, who has no stake in this city's book, who is not of the eleven, who has never been asked about anything by anybody from another world, and who said: "I have been in that channel a hundred times since I was nine. There is a place at the bottom of it where the water goes the wrong way on a still day, and it has always done, and my father said do not go there and I did not go there. On Tuesday I went there because a man asked me to and I did not know why. And there was a light in it the shape of a thumb, about the size of my thumb, and it was on the far side, and it was not on the water, it was under it, and it did not move and I stood in it for about a minute and a half and then I came out and I have not been able to say a whole sentence about it since Tuesday."**

And then, underneath, and this is the part that matters, in the notary's hand, in the flat clerk's voice, and it is in the book because Vail put it in and read it back in the hall the next morning:

> **Entered on the eleventh page and not on any other, and entered because it is the first statement of a thing a person said they saw that anybody has entered in this book in a month, and it is entered on this page because the person who saw it was sent to the channel by a man of the six and because the only form in this city that could hold it is a form on a wall, and because the holder of the pull asked for it to be put in a book where it could not be got at by one man.**

That is a lie, in one clause, and nobody ever found out for about nine days, and it is the last thing Adrian Vale wrote in that city that was a lie about a document.

---

Here is how it happened, and it happened in about four minutes, at about the seventh hour of the evening of the thirty-sixth day of the flood, at the counter in the hall, with Sera Vail on the other side of it.

He had come in from the Reach road with eleven hours' walking in his legs and had sat down for about nine minutes without saying anything, and the notary had let him, and the four witnesses of the seat were not there because it was not a sitting.

"Your page."

"I know. I have done nine of them."

"You have done nine of them and there are eleven people on it." She squared the ledger. "I am going to say a thing and I want you to hear the shape of it before you answer, because I have said it twice to you already and you have heard it as a complaint and it is not one.

"There is a man called **Gwil Merrivale** in this city. He is forty-four. He cuts reed and banks ditches at Silt Row and he has been in this city every day of his life and he has never once been in a room where a thing was decided. And this afternoon a woman with tweezers took down from him, in my presence and on my slate, the account of a light at the bottom of a channel, which he saw because somebody sent him to look at it, and he has asked to have it kept, and he wants it kept properly, and he has asked me — " she looked up — "he has asked me whether the page it goes on is the same page as the eleven."

"And what did you tell him."

"I told him the truth, which is that it does not go on the eleven, and that there is nowhere else in this book for it, and that the page about the eleven is the only page in this book that anybody from another world has ever been allowed to be on, and that I have been putting strangers' words on that page for six days because it is the only page I have that has a place for them." She put the pen down. "And he said: *then put it on that one, because that is where the other ones are, and I am not anybody.* And I said I could not do that without the holder of the pull, and he said, *he is the man out of the wall, does he not decide everything*, and I said that he decides nothing, and he said, *then who does*, and I did not have an answer, and I have not got one now, and I am forty-eight years old and I keep a book and I did not have an answer to a man with a reed hook in his hand."

She turned the page round and put it in front of him.

"It is at the bottom. It is the fifth column and it is the only line in it that is not in one of the eleven hands and I have entered where it was seen and by whom and that the man is not of the eleven and has no stake in this book, and I have entered that he was sent by a man of the six and I have entered why I am putting a reed cutter's sighting on a page about eleven people from another world." She turned a leaf back. "And there is a line at the foot of the entry for the countersignature of the holder of the pull, and the holder of the pull countersigns the daily page of the eleventh, because if he does not sign it then it is not a page of this book and it is a piece of paper, and I have done it that way since the twenty-sixth and I have asked him for it nine times and he has done it nine times and he has read every one of them."

She stopped.

"It is nine lines and it has been nine lines for about a minute and a half and I want to say, out loud, in this room, that I have put a man's sighting on a page it does not belong to in order to get it into a book, and that I have done it in a form that is otherwise correct, and that I would like him to read it before he signs it, and that I would like him to understand that I have wanted him to ask me one question about this page in six days and he has not asked it once, and that I am saying so at the fourth hour of the evening of the eleventh day of the new month, in a room with one man in it, and entering it."

"And if I do not sign it," Adrian said.

"Then it is not in the book. It is on a slate in a room by the wharf in a dead man's—" he caught it and stopped. "In a woman's hand. And in a month it will be a thing four people repeated, and a thing four people repeated has no name on it, and a thing with no name on it can be used by anybody about anybody, and there will be a man in nine days who says *a reed cutter saw a light* and there will be no page in this city that says whether he is a liar."

He signed it.

It took about four seconds. He took the pen, and he signed at the foot of an entry, and he did not read the entry, and he did not read the line above it, and he did not read the column, and he gave the pen back.

He did not notice himself doing it.

That is the finding and it is in the notebook on the Wednesday morning in about ninety words, and the ninety words are the whole of it, and they are in the flat hand and they are not folded and they are not funny:

> *The eleventh day, the seventh hour, the counter. I countersigned a page without reading it.*
>
> *It is the first thing I have signed in this world in thirty-six days without reading it, and I did not notice myself not reading it, and I want it entered that the not-noticing is the finding and not the signature. There was no decision in it. A person had asked me for one and a person had stood there and not answered a reed cutter's question, and I sat down on the eleventh day of the new month with eleven hours' walking in my legs and I signed a piece of paper about a light at the bottom of a channel that I did not know existed, and I signed it in a hall, in front of a notary, under a rule I wrote myself in a worse mood, and I broke it in about four seconds and nobody stopped me because nobody was watching me for it.*
>
> *A page that the holder has not read is not a page. It is a piece of paper with a signature on it, and a signature is a thing a Crown clerk can hold up, and there is a letter in a building in Aurel that says a position may be filled by a person who can hold it, and I have just made myself, in four seconds, with a wet pen, on a stool, a person who can hold it.*
>
> *Ask the people who maintain the gate. Ask the keeper. Ask the woman who keeps a count.*

The next morning, at about the tenth hour, in a hall of about two hundred people, with the reading of the whole page taking eleven minutes, a woman of forty-one of Silt Row stood up on the third bench and entered a **statement of error** against it, in her own words, without giving a reason and without a cost, and it is the second statement of error in the history of the Lantern Concord and it is against a page and not against an entry, and the person it named was Adrian Vale, and the terms bar him from entering one against anything on his own seat and did not bar a person from entering one against him, which is the first time that has been tested and it worked.

Wenna Cray's statement is four sentences and it is the longest thing she has ever said in a room and it does not accuse him of anything:

> **I do not say the account is not true. I say that a man of Silt Row who cuts reed has been in that channel a hundred times since he was nine, and that he says his father told him not to go there, and that there is a place at the bottom of it where the water goes the wrong way on a still day, and it has always done.**
>
> **I say that a thing which has always done is not a discovery, and that the man who saw a thumb of light in it on Tuesday was sent there by a man who has been going to that place since the eighteenth day of this flood at six in the morning before anybody was up, and nobody sent him, and I have known about that for five weeks because I have a man who goes out with him.**
>
> **I say that the page this is on is the page about eleven people, and that a man who saw a light has been put on it so that his sighting is on the same paper as theirs, and that if that is a fair thing to do then I want somebody to come and tell me what it is to be done to, because I have been in this market for four weeks saying what happens to people in a city that keeps a count, and I have been saying it about a number on a board.**
>
> **And I do not know that the man in the book knew. I want that in as well. I have never once in this month known that a man in this city knew, and I have never once in this month known that he did not, and that is the whole of what I have to say and it is not an accusation and I am not going to make it one.**

Sera Vail read it out. It took two minutes and eleven seconds and the room did not make a sound in any of it, and Adrian Vale did not answer it, which is the second time in eleven days he has been asked something in a room and not answered it, and Vail entered that too.

---

Everything else that happened on the thirty-sixth day of the flood is entered in the book and four items of it are here.

**The yard, at six and at six.** Owen Park said the binding, twice, and put the fifth word in the middle both times, and it worked both times.

**The flagstones.** At about the fourth hour of the afternoon of the thirty-sixth day, eleven flagstones came out of a path in the yard under the lock chapel, one at a time, and were carried to the far side of the boards and stacked. Nine of them are the ordinary size of the stones in that path and weigh about thirty pounds. Two of them are bedded stones and weigh about forty-two, and the last one is a corner stone with a mason's mark cut in the underside of it and weighs about fifty-one, and it is entered in this city's book as an object and not as a paving stone.

Owen Park took all eleven with his right hand, one at a time, and set them down with his right hand, and he did the corner stone last and he did it alone and he did not use the bar. A man from Silt Row called **Gwil Merrivale**, forty-four, a reed cutter and a ditch hand, who was in the yard that afternoon because he banks the near ditch twice a week, held the far end of nine of the eleven and thought nothing of it, and said afterwards that the last one was heavy and that the man with the sling should not have done it, and that he had said so at the time and the man with the sling had said *all right* in a voice that ended the conversation.

The eleven flagstones are entered, with the hours, because the stones are the stones and because on the Wednesday morning a woman of fifty-eight took the number out of Owen Park's own mouth in a room by the wharf and would not have taken it out of anybody else's, and entered the number and the day and the fact that the number of stones and the weight of the corner stone were both stated by a person who was asked and not by a person who was watching.

**The miller's flour.** Noor Haddad has a ledger with a column in it with a small square round it, which is *who gave it to us and what they expect*, and on the Tuesday morning there were three barrows of flour on the bank of the second ditch and nobody had given them, and the miller is at Silt Row and has been doing it since the day the water came up and has told nobody and has told them now, in one sentence, to Noor, in the market, and what he expects is nothing.

"He expects nothing," said Noor Haddad. "I asked him three times and he said *nothing* three times and then he got annoyed, which in this city is a man telling you the true answer. So it goes in the column, and the column says *a miller at Silt Row, three barrows, twice a day, and nothing*, and that is a different sentence from a cost and Marek Osley is not going to be able to have a conversation with me about it, because I am not entering it in a cost column, I am entering it in a column about what people expect, and what he expects is nothing, and I have got that in my own hand and I am not giving it up."

**The ditches.** Wenna Cray of Silt Row brought the thing to the hall on the Tuesday morning in one sentence and it is in the book, and she said it the way she says everything, which is as a fact about a number:

"Four villages in this Reach cannot promise their gate this week and did not promise it last week either, and one of them is Long Water, and the man who holds the second gate at Long Water entered a statement of error against this city's book on the fourth day of this flood and was right, and nobody has gone to tell him that he was right, and I have been thinking about that for two days and I am going to say the whole of it out loud: a village that cannot promise a gate-opening on a date does not keep its share of the water, and that is not a fine, and there are nineteen people putting flour on ditch banks at four in the morning who were not asked and have told nobody, and eleven of them are in this hall, and this city is going to keep using a field as a cost column for as long as a field can stand it, and on the day it cannot stand it the last four villages will find out about the whole arrangement from a market."

**And the thing nobody noticed.**

Nobody asked Owen Park to volunteer for anything, on the thirty-sixth day of the flood, or on the thirty-fourth, or on the thirty-third.

There was a man in that city who was not available. He had a shoulder that went on the twenty-fifth day at the eleventh hour and eleven minutes, it had gone back in twice, it had been put in at two minutes past two by a woman with tweezers, and it could not be set, because there was nothing out of place. A rope had been asked to be a wall and had taken the wall's job and would take it again and would not be able to tell him afterwards and neither would he. Hesper Saye had said that to about forty people in a yard on the twenty-fifth and to four hundred men in a month and been wrong about ninety of them, and it is in the book in the same form in every place, and the book is a book that Sera Vail enters contradictions in on purpose.

And so when a thing needed doing on the thirty-sixth day of the flood — because a man from another world needed the eleventh page countersigned, and a stone had to come out of a path in the yard because eleven people from another world have been walking over the same eleven flagstones for eleven days and four of them are worn through — nobody said *Owen*. Everybody knew. Hesk, the foreman, has known since the twenty-fifth and voted against a working and had his name entered for it. A mason of fifty knows. A woman of about sixty in a felt hat has known for eleven days and has said nothing about it, and the not-saying is deliberate, and Nell Bray has entered that she is not saying it, in a room, to a man with a waxed slate, in the form of an entry that has the word *refusal clause* in it.

Adrian Vale had been carrying it since the twenty-fifth. He was so relieved on the thirty-sixth that he did not look at it, and the reason he did not look at it is written in his own notebook on the Wednesday morning, in nine words, in the flat hand:

> *A man with a shoulder is a man who is not available.*
>
> *Availability is a thing this city has been quietly redistributing for a month. I have not given a single person in it away, and I have watched about nine people do it, and I was the one who was supposed to be watching, and this afternoon I was so glad he was not going to be asked that I went and had a good meal.*

And what Owen Park did on the thirty-sixth day of the flood, at about the seventh hour, in the yard, is this. He found a man. He asked a man. He got the man to do a thing he had not told anybody about, and the thing was a walk, and the walk was at the bottom of a reed channel, and the man was **Gwil Merrivale**, forty-four, of Silt Row, who cuts reed and banks ditches and who has never in his life been asked to do anything by anybody from another world, and who was in the yard that evening because he banks the ditch on the near side of the lock twice a week and had come to see about a barrow.

Owen asked him pleasantly, in a doorway, over a barrow, the way he had asked a mason over lime on the Thursday of the third week, and the pleasantness is the method, and Renna Ord named the method on the twenty-fourth day in a yard in front of about nine people and wrote it down.

And before that, on the same afternoon, at about the fourth hour, he had got Ottoline Vask to go down and sound eight feet of the outer wall of the outer channel under the cap of the old landing, which she had already done once for him on the Thursday of the third week and about which she had said in a yard in front of six people, *there is a hollow under the cap and I will not say what is in it and you are not going to make me say what is in it.*

So: three in three weeks, and four in a month, and the fourth was on the thirty-sixth and the fourth was the one where Renna Ord was not there.

Renna Ord was at Long Water. She went up the Reach road on the Tuesday morning with four men and a boat and did not come back until the Wednesday afternoon, because a miller at Silt Row had sent a message up the road on the back of a barrow of flour that four villages could not promise their gates and that if a salt factor wanted to hear about it he could come and hear it at the well-head, and she had not been asked and had gone, which is what she does.

And Adrian Vale was the man in this city who was standing in a yard with a notebook on the evening of the thirty-sixth, and it is on the page that he did not read, and he has written the whole of it up himself, four days later, in a room above a market, and it is nine lines and it is the worst thing in that notebook, and the nine lines are these:

> *On the eleventh day of the new month I was the person whose job it was, in a city of eleven hundred people, to notice a man making other people go.*
>
> *I did not notice. I was on the road. I was on the road for a reason that was a real reason, and the real reason took me off the road for eleven hours, and I have written in this notebook four times that the true cost of a thing is not the thing.*
>
> *And what I am going to write is this, and it is the sentence I have been avoiding for four days, and I am going to write it and then I am going to go and see him and he is going to be in the loft and he is going to be fine.*
>
> *I put a man with no page, no shift, and no standing in this city at the bottom of a reed channel to look at a light, for a purpose of my own that I have not told him, and a man with a reed hook who has been in that channel since he was nine has seen something and cannot say a whole sentence about it, and I have signed his words into a public book in four seconds without reading them, and a woman of forty-one is going to stand up in a hall tomorrow and say that the man in the book may or may not have known, and she is right, and I do not know, and there is a version of the next four days in which I never ask him what the light was, and I have been telling myself for a month that a thing nobody has tested is a thing that must be protected, and I protected it, and the protection was me not asking one man one question, and that is a rule about people made by a man who did not ask, and it is the worst rule on a wall, and I wrote that sentence myself nine days ago.*
>
> *Ask the people who maintain the gate. Ask the keeper. Ask the woman who keeps a count.*

---

Hesper Saye will not look at a head before she has looked at a child, and there were no children in the low room by the salt wharf on the evening of the thirty-sixth, and so she looked at the head, which is what she had been not doing for eleven days.

"There are eleven on that page," she said, in English, to Mina Okoye, "and there is a sixteenth-year-old girl in your loft who has answered a question in a market four times today and who has answered it in her own words, and I want you to hear me say that I have been watching her since the twenty-ninth and I have not said anything about it because I did not want to be a woman with a foreign word for a thing that happens to children."

"She is remarkable," Mina said.

"She is *available*," said Hesper Saye. "And there is a difference and it is a difference you wrote on a wall, and you wrote it in the second week about six people and it has been true ever since and you are the only person in this world who has noticed that it is now true about a sixteen-year-old, and I have watched three men in this city ask that girl what it is like and I have watched her answer all three of them, and she is not lying, and that is the part I cannot get past. She is not lying and it is still costing her something and she has not asked anybody for anything and nobody has noticed because she is *remarkable*."

Mina Okoye went up the stair to the loft at about the ninth hour that night and did not give an order, which is not what she is good at, and sat down at the end of the table next to a girl of sixteen with a school exercise book and said:

"Right. I am going to read clause six of that form out loud and then I am going to sit here and you are going to do nothing at all for two days, and if anybody asks you anything, in any market, in any language, you are going to say *ask the woman with the slate in the market*, and Priya is going to be the woman with the slate in the market, and that is not a punishment and it is not a kindness and it is the correct instrument and I have been a nurse for nine years and I am using it."

"I said yes to the chandler twice," Kirsty Lund said.

"I know. I was there for the second one." Mina turned the exercise book round. "You are going to write down what he asked you and what you said, in that book, tonight, and tomorrow we are going to take it to a woman with a bowl who is going to ask you three questions and you may strike your own name out at any point, including after, and if anybody in this city is unhappy about it the clause says they have done it in the open in front of witnesses and it is entered as a fact and not as a fault, and it is the fault of whoever allows it to continue. That is not comfort. It is the only thing anybody has built in a month and I am not going to let a sixteen-year-old be the one who finds out it is only paper."

And at about the eleventh hour that night the girl did write it down, four lines, in a school exercise book, and then went to bed, and Mina Okoye sat in the loft for about forty minutes after that with the four lines in front of her and did not do anything at all, which is the hardest work she did that week and the only thing in this city anybody has ever made for her that worked.
