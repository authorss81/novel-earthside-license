# Chapter 0038 — Unclosed

Hesper Saye said the word *unclosed* in a porch on the fourteenth day of this flood, to about nine people, about a scalp that had opened on the first morning of a flood in a city of eleven hundred people, and she said it meaning something particular, and she has never taken it back, and on the thirty-eighth day of the flood at about the tenth hour of the morning she explained, in a room with about eleven people in it, what she had meant, and it took her about a minute, and it is the shortest thing anybody said in that room and it is the reason a man of thirty-eight days in this world died in the afternoon of it.

"A thing with a witness on it stays open until the witness comes back," she said, in English, to a room. "That is the whole of the word and I have never had a better one and I have had thirty-four years to look for a better one.

"And it does not mean the wound is not killing him. I want that said in this room in front of a notary, because a thing with a witness on it does not look like a thing that is killing anybody. It looks like a thing that has been left alone. It looks like a man with a scar who is being patient.

"And it means the thing keeping it open is the thing that is killing him. Not a second thing. **The same thing.** Whatever is standing in that wound is the reason it has not closed, and it is the reason it goes on, and it has been going on for thirty-seven days and it is a third of an inch into the bone now, and I have looked at it every day since the fourteenth and I have not been able to do one single thing about it, and I have known exactly what I have not been able to do it with since the fourteenth, and his name is on it, and there is a form on a wall in a room above a market with three names in a chain on it and the three names are not there, because he had not told anybody and the form does not allow a witness who has not been asked."

---

He came up out of the floor at twenty past the sixth hour of the morning and was in the room by the salt wharf by the eighth and he was in that room for nine hours.

The room is the low room by the salt wharf. It has a table in it, two benches, a stove, a shelf of jars, a window that faces the water and a door that a woman with a bound hand opens with her hip because the latch has been broken since before any of them arrived. Hesper Saye has kept that room for thirty-four years and does no private work, and Mina Okoye has worked four days a week in it since the fifteenth day of this flood, and about nine people were in it between the eighth hour of the morning and the seventh hour of the evening of the thirty-eighth day.

Owen Park was on the table from the eighth hour until about a quarter past two in the afternoon, and then he was on the table until about half past four, and then he was on the bench, and then he was on the table again, and the reasons for the two moves are entered in the room's book by two different women and the two reasons are not the same and neither of them is wrong.

He was conscious the whole time. That is the fact about the thirty-eighth day that nobody in this city has ever had to establish about anybody before, and it is entered first and it is entered in the flat voice of a notary who has been writing for twenty-six years and who wrote it at about the ninth hour and read it out at about the tenth:

> **That on the thirty-eighth day of the flood, at about the eighth hour of the morning, and until about the fourth hour and forty minutes of the afternoon, the man Owen Park was conscious, and spoke, and could be understood without being spoken to twice, and that this is entered as a fact and not as a mitigation, and that it is entered in this book at the request of nobody and because a book that records that a man was not conscious is a book about the room and not about the man, and about eleven people were in this room and every one of them has entered something about the room.**

He spoke mostly in English and mostly to Adrian, and about a third of what he said in nine hours was in the local language, badly, learned from a woman with a bowl in the second week of this flood, and he had about two hundred words of it and used them all in about four hours and never once used one twice.

---

Here is what the room knew at the tenth hour, and it is in the book, and it is in this order, and the order is Saye's and not anybody else's:

**One.** The scalp. Thirty-seven days. Opened on the first day of the flood, in a wall in another world, in a city Adrian Vale has never been to, in a lane four hundred yards from a lock. Nine days of clean skin over it by the fourteenth and nothing underneath at the end of the month, and at about the fourth hour of the morning of the thirty-eighth day, after a minute and a half of a floor being open, it is about a third of an inch further into the bone than it was in the second week. It is not bleeding. It is not cut. It has the exact same shape it has had since the first morning.

**Two.** The shoulder. The right one. Went on the twenty-fifth day of this flood at the eleventh hour and eleven minutes, in a yard, in front of three hundred and ten people, when a mason came down flat on the edge of a standing flagstone and a boy of eleven was on it. Went back in twice. Put in at two minutes past two by a woman with tweezers. Cannot be set, because there is nothing out of place. The second injury went into the same place, which is the place that has been asked to hold twice in thirteen days, and Saye said in the yard at the seventh hour on the morning of the thirty-eighth that it was the only part of him that was worse than it was on the twenty-fifth and that she was not going to say anything else about it until the Friday.

**Three.** The mark in the heel of the right hand, which is a scar now, and which is the first scar in this world that is in a person.

**Four.** And the thing that Saye found at about the eleventh hour and which she entered herself in her own book and then had Vail enter in this one, and it is this: **the scar is in the hand and it is going to be in the head by morning.**

"That is not a figure of speech and it is not a metaphor and I am not a woman who uses figures of speech," Hesper Saye said, at about the eleventh hour, in English, in a room, with her hands in a bowl. "A line in a place can be mended with work and money and I have mended one and it took me two years. A mark in a person cannot be mended, and the reason is that nobody steps over a hand, and that is a mason's reason and I am a bone-setter and I am using it because it is the correct one.

"And I have been looking at a scalp for thirty-seven days and I have watched it not close, and I know why it has not closed, and I said the word in a porch nine days after the first morning and I meant it, and what I meant was that a thing came through this man on the first morning of this flood and did not finish, and it has been standing in the one place in him that was open ever since, and it has had thirty-seven days to go out through a wound that a woman with tweezers cleaned every morning for thirty-seven days.

"And it went out through the wound at about a quarter past four in the morning, because that is what it does, and because he put his hand on a floor at four o'clock in the morning and about a hundred and forty people had read a list on a chapel door nine minutes beforehand that says *anything that answers*, and I read that list, and I would read it again, and the thing that went out through his head is not going to be got back in by me or by anybody in this world and I am going to write that down in my own book tonight and it is the first thing I have written in thirty-four years that I have not been able to do anything about afterwards."

**Five.** And a thing that was on the table in that room all day and that nobody brought there on purpose, which is that **Anwen Kroon, Registrar of Crossings and Tides, at Aurel, opened a file on the twenty-eighth day of this flood and is not represented in this city**, and the second letter of that office is a sheet of oiled cloth on the table of the Lantern Concord, and item four of it has been read out in two rooms in a week, and it is in this room, on the table, at about the second hour of the afternoon, upside down, because Mina Okoye put it there.

"It is a document from a Registry about the ground under a chapel floor," she said, "and it is in a room with a man in it, and I did not bring it. I brought a sheet of oiled cloth because the table is wet. And I am going to leave it there and I am going to read item four out loud at some point this afternoon because he can hear me and I do not think anybody in this city has ever read a Crown document to a dying man and I would like to do it, and the notary can strike it out of the record if it is improper."

She read it at about the second hour of the afternoon, to about eleven people, in English, standing up, in the flat voice she uses for documents:

> **The Registry notes that the holder has described himself in the roll of strangers as *Unbound*, and that a seat with no workway is not a form this office is able to accept, and that a holder who is unable to hold the thing he holds is not a holder but a position, and a position may be filled by a person who can hold it.**

And Owen Park laughed, which nobody in that room expected, and it took about a second and a half, and it was the last thing he did that anybody in that room found funny.

"That is not about me," he said. "That is about his hand. And it is not about his hand yet, because in order for that to be about his hand somebody in Aurel is going to have to know that there is a hand, and they are not going to know that for about eleven days, because it is in a book in this city and the case goes on a river boat four times a year." He shut his eyes for a moment. "And when they do know, they are not going to be talking about a man who died. They are going to be talking about a man who has a mark in his hand and a *seat*, and a scar in a person is the best thing that has ever happened to that office, and about four hundred men have just walked into it and cannot get out, and one of them is on this table."

---

He used the time he had. That is in the book in his own words, dictated, and it took about two hours and forty minutes, and there were about forty people in the room across that time and about nine of them stayed the whole time, and it is entered as *a person was lucid and used the time he had, and did not waste any of it, and the people of this city have no practice at being in a room with a man who is using the time he has, and the two most common things that happened in this room in nine hours were silence and a person being fetched for something that could wait.*

**The first thing was the light.**

It came at about half past eleven in the morning, out of nowhere, in the way it always came, and it was about a minute and a half long and it is in the book because a mason entered it, because Vask was in the room at about the seventh hour of the evening and asked for it to be entered, and it is in her words and it is the only thing in the ninth clause of a form about a thing a person said they saw.

"Then the fourth reading," said Owen Park, from the table, with his eyes shut, and he did not open them for the sentence. "Tell me you have written the fourth reading down. The four readings of where that sentence came from. And that it is in a notebook, and that a woman with a slate has got a page of it, and that nobody has read it but the two of us."

"I have written the fourth one down."

"Read it to me."

"*I wrote it down, and I have been carrying it, and I did not know it was the same thing.*"

"That is the one." He opened his eyes. "I have had a version of that for a month and I have not said it out loud to anybody because there is nobody in this city who would know what I mean, and there is one person in this city who would know what I mean and he has not asked me once, and I have been furious about it for eleven days, and I have not been able to be furious on paper because of clause six."

"Owen—"

"It is not a relic," said Owen Park.

It was the quietest thing anybody said in that room in nine hours and about nine people heard it and about four of them understood it and one of those four was a woman of fifty-eight who had said the word *unclosed* in a porch in the second week and who has told four hundred men things in her life.

"It is not a relic. It is the same shape as the mark on your hand. It is the same proportion — a thumbnail, and every branch of it in the same ratio, the way a coin fits the die it was struck from. It has been that shape every single time I have seen it, in the wall, in the channel, in a wet field at four in the morning, at the second stile in the dark on a road in this country, and I have known that for about eleven days and I have not said it, because I have spent eleven days working out what it means, and what it means is that I have been looking at *you*."

He stopped. About four people in that room were not breathing.

"And that is either the truest thing I have ever said or I have a hole in my head the same place as the rest of it, and I have got no way of telling you which and neither have you, and I am going to say the rest of it anyway because I have about two hours and I would like somebody in this world to have to decide about it, and deciding about it is a thing this city is good at, and I have watched it do that to about nine people in a month."

Adrian Vale was sitting on a stool at the foot of a table in a room by the salt wharf with a notebook in his hands and had the only perception in this world for a join-line and could not see a single thing Owen Park was describing and had failed to see it for thirty-eight days.

"Write it in," he said. "Not as a claim. As what a person said."

"It is already on the wall. It has been on the wall since the twenty-fourth and it is on the eleventh page of this city's book in a man's words with a reed hook, and you countersigned it on the eleventh day without reading it." Owen said it without any weight at all. "I am not asking you to write it. I am telling you what it is, because a document that says *unverified* has been sitting in a book for two weeks waiting for somebody to say a word about it, and the word is not *relic*. There is a word for what it is and I do not think anybody in this city has got it, and I have been carrying it for eleven days, and the person in this world who can corroborate a light is a man who reads a join, and you have not asked me one question about it in eleven days, and that has been the single most generous thing anybody has ever done to me, and I would like it entered, and by the man who did it, in his own hand, in this book, and the entry is: **he did not ask me, for eleven days, and he could have, and he had a form with a chain of three on a wall and he did not use it, and he has not told anybody why, and I am the only person in this world who knows that he had the form. And I am dead or about to be, and the reason is not that he did not ask, and I want the not-why written down as well, because the reason is that he had a document that would have made a thing of a person and a person cannot be a thing, and that is the reason he wrote a form for things that cannot be looked at again, and it is the only good thing anybody in this world has done in thirty-eight days and I would like it in the book with my name on it.**"

**The second thing was the road.**

It came at about half past one in the afternoon and it is the shortest of the three and Saye has said since that the man said eleven sentences in that part and that about half of them were the same sentence.

"The road and the person to send are the same thing," said Owen Park. "I said that in a room on the twenty-eighth of this flood and it was the truest thing I said in a month and it went in a book and a docker in a felt hat went and argued about it afterwards and the argument was not wrong.

"I have been in this world thirty-eight days. I did not ask for anything. Everybody in this city knows that about me and I have built it, and it worked, and I have watched about nine people notice that I had not asked, and I want to say the true thing about that, because I have had nine hours to look at it and because I am not going out of this room having lied in it.

"**A man who has asked for nothing in a room is a man you can send.** That is not a complaint. I have been building that for thirty-eight days and it is the only thing I have ever built that worked. And on Wednesday evening I stood in a yard in front of two hundred and forty people and I used it. I stood there and I said *I have asked for nothing in a month* and two hundred and forty people looked at a man who had been offered a mark and had not asked, and a woman of thirty-four from Long Water said yes, and a boy of nineteen said yes, and the terms were written and the mark was set and I was sent into a floor of a building in a city I have never been to, and I did that. I did that with the thing I built. That is what the thing was for.

"And the second part is the part I want in the book and it is this. It is not *I got what I asked for*. I did not get what I asked for and neither did anybody else. **The road went the other way and took the man who had not asked, and it did not bring anybody back, and it cannot, and it never could.** And that is the whole of what a hold is, and a hold is a working without a witness, and the residue of one goes into the person who did it, and that sentence is a woman of mine from another world's and not mine, and I have been using it as a description since the twenty-fifth day of this flood, and it is a rule, and the rule is that the person who does the holding pays it, and I have been doing it since the eighteenth day of this flood, in this city, with a lamp, on my own time, and I asked a man with a reed hook to walk a channel for me on the eleventh day and a man of thirty-one to smell wet stone in a room for me, and a boy of nineteen with a flask of tea, and a mason of thirty-four years in this city with a bar, on her own time and not asked, and I spent four men in a month and every one of them has paid for it and one of them is on this table.

"That is the road. It goes the other way and the man who has asked for nothing is the man it goes through. And I would like that in a book, with my name on it, and I would like it to be the first entry in this city of a man saying out loud what he built and what it was for, and I would like Hesper Saye to read it out at my burial, and I would like to be buried under a real name, and I would like somebody to write to my sister, and my sister's name is **Hattie Park**, and she is in Dunedin, and she does not know, and I have had no way of telling her for thirty-eight days and there is not going to be one, and I would like somebody in this world to try."

"Say the reason for the name," said Adrian Vale.

"I did not have a false one and I am not going to be given a good one." He said it the way he said everything, at the speed of a man reading a list he had been keeping. "I have been whatever this city needed me to be since the second morning of this flood and it has been the most useful thing I have ever been and it happened in about nine rooms and a market, and the man in the register is a name on a list and he is not a man, and I have known that since the eleventh day and I did not want to be that in the ground. A man who is sent into a floor goes in under a name or it does not count, and I do not have one, and I am not going to be handed a good one, because the day somebody hands me a good one is the day this place starts handing them out, and you are the one who would be handing them out, because you are the one who could."

Adrian Vale wrote it down at about the eleventh hour of the morning of the thirty-eighth day, in a school exercise book with somebody else's name on the cover, in his own hand and not as a summary, because he had asked for the reason and not the fact and had not known which of the two he was going to get.

**The third thing was the sentence on a board.**

It came at about a quarter past two in the afternoon and it is the one that Adrian Vale has never been able to write about except in the terms that are in the book, which are the terms of a man who was asked to write it down.

"Marrow's chalk," said Owen Park. "On the thirtieth day, in the yard, in daylight, in front of about thirty people. He wrote that you would do it. He wrote that you have never yet not written down the true reason. And he asked for the true reason for refusing the workway to be written under his own withdrawal in your own hand on the keeper's board, and you wrote it on the seventh day of the new month, and it is on the door, and Peth Lomas put his own name under it, and I have read it every morning for six days because the yard is where the binding is said and I have been in that yard about forty times in five weeks."

"I know you have."

"You wrote about yourself. You wrote *I refused it because it worked, and I liked saying no, in a room, in front of people, in the rain.*"

"Yes."

"I refused the workway too," said Owen Park, "on the twelfth day, in a room, in front of six people, and my reason is in the book, and my reason is: *if I take a workway I will never know whether I am seeing the light or doing it.* That is a good reason. It is in the log. Priya has it in her own handwriting and it is the best-sounding thing I have ever said in my life.

"And it is not the true one, and the true one is on a board in a yard and it is a man's own hand and it is about a man, and I have known that for about forty minutes, and I would like you to write it down, and I would like to be the one who says the sentence, because you have been the one who says the sentence in this city for thirty-eight days and I would like to be it for about four minutes.

"**The true reason I refused the workway is that a workway is a duty somebody else is already doing, and the somebody else is not in the room, and I had spent two weeks finding out exactly whose, and I did not want to be a man who said the duty was somebody else's problem.**"

The room was very quiet and there were about eleven people in it.

"That is your sentence," he said. "That is the sentence you wrote on a board on the seventh day of the new month, and it is a true sentence, and it is a bad sentence, and it is mine, and I read it every morning for six days and I did not know it was mine until about a quarter past two this afternoon, and I have had a mark in my hand since last night and a scar in it since this morning and I am not going anywhere, and I have about an hour and a half, and I would like the true reason for my refusal written down in my own words and not the fair one, and I would like it written where it was written, and I would like a mason to read it out.

"And Adrian — I want to say the other half while I can, and it is the half that matters and it is about a man in a good coat and I am not accusing him of anything, because he has been nothing but useful for eight weeks and he sent a bonesetter to a yard before anybody knew there would be a yard.

"He asked you for the true reason. He did not say *yours*." Owen Park's eyes were open and there was nothing in them at all except the business of saying a thing correctly. "He said *the* true reason. And you wrote *your* true reason, in your own hand, about yourself, on a keeper's wall, where anybody who comes up that yard has to read it, and it is a rule, and a rule is a thing that stands there after the man who made it has gone. And I do not think he asked for that. I think he asked for a thing in a book in a good coat's hand, and a thing in a good coat's hand is an argument, and an argument can be answered, and a thing in a keeper's hand on a keeper's wall in this city is a **record**, and a record is the only thing in this world that has ever survived anybody.

"And I have worked that out in about four minutes in a room by a salt wharf and I would like somebody in this world to be angry with him about it, and not tonight, because he has not done anything wrong, and I would like it to be written down that I do not think he has done anything wrong, and that he asked a question that four hundred people in a city of eleven hundred are going to want answered, and that he asked it of one man, and that the one man wrote down his own."

---

He dictated the entry of himself at about half past three in the afternoon, in about eleven sentences, in a room with about nine people in it, and he invented the procedure and it took him four minutes and it is the last thing he did in this world that was not about a wound.

"Nobody in this city has ever asked a man whether the thing that has been written about him is the thing he would have written," he said. "There is a clause on a form about a person striking their own name and there is a clause in a book about the eleven and there is a page on the fourth column of a wall upstairs that says what the work is for and not what the person is for. And there is not one single instrument in this world that lets a man read back the entry about himself and say no to the words.

"Write this. **This is the account of Owen Park of the thirty-eighth day of the flood, and it is given by me, in my own words, on the thirty-eighth day of the flood, at about the fourth hour of the afternoon, in a room by the salt wharf, to about nine persons, of whom three cannot understand the language it is being given in and I have asked for it to be entered in the book and read back in the language of the room on the thirty-ninth day. I was conscious. I have not been in pain that I could not say out loud. I struck nothing. I have not asked anybody for anything today and I have asked for this.**"

There is a boy of nine in this city called **Tam Crane** who follows Owen Park at six feet and has done since the second week of this flood, because his mother owns the building that the loft is the top of and holds the ground floor on a rent-free lease and sold him a key, and because a man who does not talk to a nine-year-old is a thing a nine-year-old talks at, and Sesh Brack, who is a market woman and a landlady and a widow's-box administrator and who has a bound hand, stopped him doing it in a market once and then said *let him, he has not got a father in this city and neither have you, and at least he has somebody to follow about and you have a shutter.*

He came in at about the first hour of the afternoon and stood at the end of the table for about twenty minutes and nobody stopped him, and at about a quarter past four, which is about a quarter of an hour before it happened, he said to Adrian Vale in the local language, which he has picked up from a man who follows him about for a month, in a voice with no consonants in it:

"Is he going to be all right?"

And Adrian said, in the local language, which he can now manage in about ninety seconds without a correction, and Tamsin Quill did not correct him and he noticed and wrote it down afterwards and it is in the notebook and it is four words on a page that has nineteen pages of the worst day of his life in it:

"He is going to be asleep by tonight."

And Tamsin Quill, who had been standing in the doorway in the local language for two hours, said in English, to nobody, in a room: "That was a lie."

"It was," said Adrian.

"You will go back and fix it."

"Not to him. Not today. He is nine and he has been following that man at six feet for a month and he has a right to a sentence out of me today that he can use on a Tuesday, and I have got about an hour and I am going to use it for a man on a table."

"Do as you like. It is not mine and it is not his and it is going to be true either way in about an hour." She did not move from the doorway. "I have watched you do this for thirty-eight days. It is the same thing as the four rules and the post and the page. You build a small true thing for a person so that the person has a thing to stand on, and the small true thing is not the world and it is not what you are doing and it is not going to hold, and you do it anyway because you cannot bear to hand a nine-year-old nothing."

"Yes."

"And on Friday you are going to be in a hall with four hundred people in it."

"Yes."

"Then say the true one in the hall," said Tamsin Quill, "and say the small one here, and do not tell me which order those go in."

---

There is also a document in this city about Owen Park that has never been signed and is never going to be signed, and it is in a book, and it is on the page opposite the entry of the second group, and it is four lines long and a widow wrote it and a man did not meet it and died.

It is a **twelve-month lease** on the two upper rooms of a building in the river market, in Owen Park's own hand, with two conditions attached to it that have not been met in thirty-eight days: **tell Sesh in her kitchen with her in the room, and go up the street and ask Sesh whether the roof over her head is the roof being sold.**

Delya Crane offered it in a doorway on the fourteenth day of this flood, calmly, in a way that cannot be handled as a fight, and she wrote the two conditions herself and she has never once asked him about them and she was not in that room on the thirty-eighth and she came at about the seventh hour of the evening and read the entry from the outside of a doorway, standing up, in about forty seconds, and then said four sentences to Adrian Vale that are in the book at the seventh hour of the thirty-eighth and that nobody in this city has ever said in a better order.

"You did not ask me for a thing today. I heard about that from four people before I got here, and I have been a widow for nine years and I have been owed things by men in good coats for nine years, and I would like it in the book that I heard it.

"The lease is a paper and the paper does not care and I am not going to pretend that it does. Two conditions. He has not met either of them and I am not going to say he has, because he has not, and I have watched him for thirty-eight days and I know he has not because I have watched him every day going past my door and not up it.

"And the second one is the one I am going to have to live with, and it is this: I wrote that condition because I did not want this building to be a roof over somebody and then a price, and he has not asked me, and I am going to have to be a woman whose building had a man die in a room four streets away and who never found out what he thought about her roof.

"So write this down, because a woman with a slate will write anything you say and I would like it in the book in my words: **the conditions stand, and the paper stands, and I am going to keep the ground floor and I am going to keep the two rooms, and there is no man in them now, and nobody from another world is going to sleep in them.**"

And then she said, in English, which she can do badly, and looked at the floor:

"And I am going to want to go and stand in my own kitchen and be angry for about an hour, and then I am going to go up the street and ask her, because the second condition was *her* condition as much as mine and I wrote it in eleven minutes because I did not want to be a woman who had thought about it for a month."

---

And then he said the last thing, and Adrian Vale has it in a notebook and it is four lines and it is the last thing that was said in the room by the man on the table, and it is in the book in his own words and it is under everything else:

"And write down that I was not on a game. I do not know what the word is and I do not think it means what they say it means, and I have known that since the first morning and I have watched about forty people in this city find out slower than I did. Write down that there is no game, and that nobody was fetched, and that a man walked into a room in another world on the first day of this flood and came out of it thirty-eight days later with two marks on his hand and eleven hundred people who had never heard of him, and that is the whole of what happened to me, and it is not a tragedy, and a tragedy is a thing that happens to somebody else."

Nobody washing a man's hands is the last thing that happened in that room on the thirty-eighth day of the flood and it is not in a book, because Renna Ord is not a notary and does not keep a record and had not been in the room until about a quarter to five in the afternoon.

She had been on the Reach road since the fifth hour of the morning of the thirty-sixth and had come back at the fifth hour of the afternoon of the thirty-seventh and had got about three hours' sleep in a chair in a boat-coat, and she had gone up the road again at six in the morning on the Thursday because a boy of nineteen had come into the yard and said four sentences and a man of fifty-eight had a mark on his hand, and she was back inside the wall by the eleventh hour and had been in eleven rooms since and had not been in this one until a quarter to five.

She was not a friend of Owen Park's. She had been the first person in this city to say what a man who has asked for nothing in a room is, on the fourth day of the flood, over a barrow, in a yard, and she had said it about him, and she had said it as an insult, and she had entered it, and it had been in a book for thirty-four days.

She came in at about a quarter to five and Mina Okoye was at the table with a basin and Owen Park was on the bench against the wall with the stool behind his head and about four people in the room, and the woman with the bowl was outside on the step with her back to the wall and had gone out and had not come back in and had left her bowl on the sill, which is a thing that has happened about four times in thirty-four years.

Renna Ord did not say anything. She took the basin off the table and the towel off the nail and washed his hands.

Both of them. One at a time, and the right one first, and she got the palm open and there was a scar in the heel of it about the size of a thumbnail and it was black and it was not a mark any more, and she had heard about it in a yard and she put her thumb on the edge of it the way a docker puts a thumb on the edge of a thing to find out whether it is still moving, and it was not.

She washed the dirt out of the creases of a hand that had been on a dressed joint at four in the morning. She washed the black out from under the second knuckle where there was a graze he had not reported. She washed the four fingers and the thumb and the back and the wrist, and she was not fast about it, and she did not speak, and there were four people in the room and about two hundred and forty people in the yard outside and a city of eleven hundred people behind that, and Renna Ord washed a man's hands for about nine minutes.

He died at about half past four in the afternoon of the thirty-eighth day of the flood, of a head wound that opened on the first day of this flood in another world, on a shoulder that had been out of its socket since the twenty-fifth day of this flood, in a body that took a working's residue on a flagstone in front of three hundred and ten people, and Saye was not in the room and has said since that she was on the step with her back to the wall because she had sent herself out of a room at about a quarter to five because she is fifty-eight and has told four hundred men things in her life and has been wrong about ninety of them and has known since the fourteenth day of this flood which ninety this would be.

Mina Okoye was there. She said it, in English, in a room, to four people, and she said it the way she says things: *He is gone. That is the time. I have got the time and I have got it right, because I have been counting since the sixth hour of the morning of the twenty-seventh and that is how I know.*

There is no second time and there is no coming back and there is no mark for anybody else and the scar in a man's hand does not go to a person or a boy of nineteen or a stone or a boat. He is not a casualty of a monster and he is not a casualty of a door; he is a man from a security contractor's firm in Dunedin, in another hemisphere, who learned four words of a maintenance oath in the rain on the second morning of a flood and said them every morning for the better part of a month so that a courtyard in a city he had never visited would not become a room with a hole in it, and who could not lift forty pounds with his right arm for the rest of his life and did not know it, and who was a scar in a person on the evening of the thirty-eighth day and the first one anybody in this world has ever had to bury.

And Adrian Vale sat on a stool at the foot of a table in a room by the salt wharf and did not say anything for about an hour and a half, and Hettis Roke wrote nothing and read nothing back, and Tamsin Quill stood in the doorway in the local language, and Peth Lomas came up from the walk-round at about the sixth hour and had not been told and could not be told, and Ottoline Vask, who is a mason and has been in this city for thirty-four years and who was still sitting on a step two hundred steps up with a bar across her knees, had not been told either, and was stopped in a doorway by a docker at about six in the evening and did not understand a word of what was said to her and stood in a doorway for about ten minutes and then went down two hundred steps and sat on the step again until it was dark.

He has written up the thirty-eighth day of the flood in nineteen pages, and the first line of the first page is the one that is read out in a converted salt store at the first light of the thirty-ninth, and it is this, and it is the whole of what a man came out of the wall for, in a book, in the flat hand, and it is four lines long:

> *Nothing gained. It is the thirty-eighth day and a man is dead and it was in the book on the seventeenth, in a form, with an empty chain of witnesses, because he had not told anybody, and a form does not allow a witness who has not been asked.*
>
> *I have spent thirty-eight days learning that a thing nobody has tested is a thing that must be protected, and I protected it by not asking one man one question, and the protection was me not asking, and a thing that is held open by not asking is a thing that is being held open by me.*
>
> *I am the reason and it cannot be proved to be. That is the ninth clause of a statement of what a person said they saw, and he wrote it himself on the twenty-fourth day of this flood about a mark in his own hand, and it is the only clause in this city that has ever been true of anything, and it has been true of him for two weeks and he did not know it and it has killed a man.*
>
> *Ask the people who maintain the gate. Ask the keeper. Ask the woman who keeps a count. And do not ask me anything about this.*

The basin was washed at about the eighth hour of the evening by a docker who does not keep a record and had not been in the room until a quarter to five in the afternoon, and it was put back on the shelf, and the towel was hung on the nail, and the stool was put under the table, and the room was left the way a room is left when something has happened in it and the people who were in it have gone out through a door and come back in through a door and are not going to talk about it for about a year.
