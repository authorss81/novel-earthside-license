# Chapter 0015 — What the Anchorstone Holds

Low water on Saturday came at twenty past five in the morning and the yard under the lock chapel was a different yard from the one Adrian had been working in all week, because there was mud in it you could stand in without falling over, and that turned out to matter more than anything anybody said for the next six hours.

Nyla Ferrow came out of the chapel at ten past six with her board of lime and clay under her arm, and she did the daily binding at the arch before she would speak to anybody, and Adrian stood well back and watched her do it, and understood about four seconds in that he was watching the oldest continuous piece of work in his life and had no business near it.

Chalk. Three marks. A pause with a short sentence. Clay, pressed flat, smoothed with the heel of the hand until it was the colour of the stone. Water out of a brass cup, touched to the middle. And then the words again, a little longer, and her right hand flat on the stone for the length of them.

He got four of the words. He had had four of the words on Tuesday and he had four of the words on Saturday morning, and one of the four was the word that Nyla Ferrow used to begin every binding, and it was not one of the four words of the maintenance oath, and on Tuesday he had assumed it was a kind of local flavour.

"May I say something and be wrong," he said, when she had finished.

"You may say it," said Nyla Ferrow, "and you will be wrong. Ask."

"I want to ask her to say the maintenance oath in this yard," Adrian said, in English, to Tamsin, "all four words, in the old form, the one they stopped using sixty years ago. Not to say it. To *count* it."

Nyla Ferrow, who had not been told the question in the local language and who clearly did not need to be, said one word and made it very clear that she had understood the question anyway, and Tamsin said, "She says: you want the four words of the old oath said out loud in a yard, in front of eleven people, in the cold, by a man who does not speak it."

"Yes."

"Why."

"Because on Wednesday night a voice said four words and put a fifth one in the middle, and Tamsin caught it, and it was Tamsin who told me the word was *answer*, and Mina, who cannot speak this language at all, was the one who noticed it was not the same four words, and I have been carrying that around for three days like a stone in a shoe." He turned his hand over and looked at the heel of his palm, where the pale trace was and the black line was not. "And I want to know whether the fifth one is in the oath or whether it is in the arch. Because those are two different sentences and they have been two different sentences for four hundred years, and something in the lock join has been saying both of them in one breath."

---

Tamsin said the four words.

She said them slowly, in the yard, in the ordinary voice, to an audience of Ottoline Vask and two of Vask's masons, Ovin Sarr, Nell Bray, Roke, and nine people who had come down off the walk-round because word travels, and Adrian stood at the edge of it and counted.

Four words. And then, in the middle of the second one, Tamsin said the fifth.

And Nyla Ferrow, who had been standing in the chapel doorway with her hand still flat on the arch, said the word first, in the flat patient register of somebody who has said one word every morning for thirty years and has never had to say it twice at once.

And then, about half a second later, out of the black line in the threshold stone under her feet — the raised pale slab every person in this city steps over on the way in — something under the lock said the same word, in a man's accent, as though finishing the phrase for her.

The whole yard went still.

"That is not the oath," Tamsin said.

"No," said Nyla Ferrow.

"That is your word."

"It is my mother's word," said the keeper, and came out into the yard, and did not go towards the arch and did not go towards the yard wall and stood in the middle of the mud in the middle of everything, the way a person stands when they have decided they are going to be asked in public and have decided in advance to answer in public. "It is my mother's word and it is her mother's word and it is not in the oath. It has never been in the oath. I have said it here every morning since I was nineteen, standing where I am standing, and it has never once been *said* — it has been *kept*. Those are not the same word in my language and I cannot give you the difference in yours and I am not going to try."

"Then what is it," said Roke, in the local language, flatly, and Tamsin did not translate for a moment, which Adrian noticed, and which was the first time in five days that she had let a sentence sit in a room in the language it was said in.

When she translated, her voice was the worst it had ever been.

"It is *we keep the third thing*. It is what the keeper says at the arch and has said since before the chapel was a chapel, and it is not the gate and it is not the boundary. It is the promise that the room is a place where somebody can be asked." She looked at Adrian. "On Wednesday night a man in a join said the old oath with a keeper's word in the middle of it, and there are two families in this city whose words those are, and both families are standing in this yard. Somebody down there is holding the two oldest things this gate has and he has them at the same time and he is saying them in the right order. That is not an echo. That is a man who has been *taught*."

---

The pale field came up in the yard, in the mud, in front of the arch, at about twenty past seven, and it was different.

Not bigger. Not brighter. Different in the way a familiar face is different when it is being kind to you.

> THE OLD BOUNDARY IS HELD IN TWO PLACES.
>
> One is the stone. One is the table.
>
> A holder may be shown which one the water is using.
>
> Nobody is offered a road.

It stood there for eleven seconds and Adrian counted, because counting was the only thing he had.

Then it went, and he felt the specific physical nausea of a man who has been offered the one thing he has wanted for eleven days in a form shaped like the absence of the thing, and who has noticed, about four seconds too late, that the last line is not a consolation. It is a *price tag*. Six offers in eleven days, and every one of them had a *shape* in it and a place for a yes in it and nothing whatever in it for a no. Five of the six had mentioned a road. All five had mentioned it as a caution. This time it appeared in the grammar position that a threat occupies, and Adrian could hear the sentence around it: *you want to know which boundary the water is using, and here is the last time anybody will mention the word road in connection with you.*

And the thing it had offered was the most valuable thing anybody had offered him since Tuesday, and Adrian understood within about nine seconds exactly what it had left out, and it took him until half past nine that morning, standing in a hole with his hand on a bar, to be able to say it out loud.

It would have told him, and told nobody else.

That is the whole of the offer and it took him four seconds to see. There is no page. There is no second pair of eyes, and no chalk, and no witness, and no argument that anybody can make afterward. A thing the wall shows the holder is a thing the holder is the only record of, and a man who is the only record of a river boundary in a city with eleven villages and a Reed margin downstream of it is not a witness. He is a singleton, and Adrian had learned what a singleton was in front of two hundred and eleven people eight days earlier, and he had learned it by failing at it.

He did not tell them at seven in the morning. He wrote it in the notebook on the bar, in the mud, with a stub of chalk from Ovin's pocket, and he wrote:

*It offered to show me which boundary the water is using. It would have been true. It would have been true and there would have been no page and no witness and no argument, and the man who knows would have been me, alone, in a city where being alone with a fact is a way of being wrong permanently. Refused, and the reason is not that it was a lie.*

And then, underneath, in smaller writing:

*Eleven days. Six offers. Every one of them is a way of making me the only person in the room who can see.*

---

Ottoline Vask had a bar out of the shed and forty minutes' worth of opinion before anybody had agreed to do anything at all.

"First: this is possible. I want that said in front of the masons, because in four days somebody is going to say I said it was not." She leaned the bar against the jamb. "Second: it is not a hole. It is a *floor*, and the floor is the only thing in this building that is older than the arch, and when you open a floor you do not find out what is under it, you find out how much of the floor you have to take up, and I have been doing that for thirty-four years and the honest answer is that I do not know, and a mason who says *I do not know* and goes ahead is a mason who has decided to be the person it happened to."

"Third," she said, "is the price, and the price is the floor. There is no other version. The binding at the arch is held in the flagstones. The moment there is an open cut in the flagstones there is a thing in this room that cannot be done in this room, and the *thing* does not care, and the room does not care, and the arch is the oldest working this city has and it will go on being the oldest working this city has while it is standing on a whole floor and it will stop being anything at all the day the floor is in pieces."

"How long would the floor be in pieces?"

"That depends on what is under it, which is what we do not know, which is why I have a bar."

She went in alone, with a lantern, and she was under the smallest of the long flagstones at the north end, three feet from the foot of the arch, and she was out again in nine minutes with her knees ruined and a scratch on the left side of her face that she did not notice for an hour.

"There is a void," she said. "Not a chamber. A *void*. That stone is bedded on nothing for about two feet in every direction, and the nothing goes down further than I would like, and it is straight down, which is the part that bothers me, because a void under a floor is either a drain or a thing that was taken out, and I have never in this city seen a drain that somebody dug and then stopped digging."

---

Tamsin read the table out in the yard at half past nine, and she had brought it, and everybody understood that she had brought it on purpose and against somebody's advice.

"I keep a copy of it," she said, "because my name is on it, and I have read every line of it, and I have read it every year since I was twenty-two, and I have never once read it out loud in a yard, and I am doing it now because on Wednesday evening a man from Aurel told a room that a stone under this chapel is out of its place, and I have discovered that the man from Aurel is the first person in six years to put a *what if* in front of that table, and I would like to explain the table."

She had a single sheet, folded into eight, soft along the folds from seven years of being carried in a coat.

"It is a table of every attempt this city has made to move the old river boundary," she said. "That is what it is. It is not a history. It is a *list of failures*, and there are eleven lines, and the columns are: the year; who proposed it; what the proposal was; what the new boundary would have done to the water; and who paid."

"Whose name is on line seven."

"Mine," said Tamsin. "I was twenty-one. I proposed that the boundary be moved four hundred yards upstream, which would have given the bottom field another season and taken it out of the Reed margin, and the Reed margin is lower than the bottom fields, and it is lower because somebody made it lower in the first place, and the cost column on line seven says *the Reed margin, the whole of it, and the eleven villages that take water from it, and nobody knows which until it is wet*."

She turned the sheet round.

"Line one is the year the boundary was last moved. It is a year in living memory. The proposal column says: *the old river's edge is written in the stone set in the yard-side landing of the chapel, and the stone is to be lifted and set one hundred yards downstream, and the flood to be allowed to find the new edge itself.*" She stopped. "It was carried. It was carried by the three jurisdictions together, at a public act, with witnesses, and it is the only line on this table that anybody ever carried out. And the cost column says: *the bottom field failed in that year, and four hundred people were rehoused, and the boundary was not moved again.*"

"Which means the stone got moved," Adrian said, "and moved *downstream*."

"Which means the stone got moved," said Tamsin, "by a decision, taken in public, by three jurisdictions, with witnesses, one hundred yards downstream, and the cost went into a column, and the column is the reason the city knows the price. It is the best-documented failure in the history of this city and it cost four hundred people their homes and it is the *only* thing anybody has ever done successfully to that boundary."

"And Ivenn's route said *find it and bring it back before the next tide*."

"His route was true in every particular," said Tamsin. "There is a stone. It is under this chapel. It is out of its place. It is four steps left of the yard at the foot of the yard-side landing. It is dry, and it has been dry for a long time, and it holds the shape of a boundary instead of holding water. Every single part of that is correct and I have been able to confirm every single part of it in eleven days, and Vask has confirmed the last of it twice." She folded the sheet over her arm. "The one part that is not in his route, and the only part that matters, is that he did not say *bring it back*."

"He said it on Wednesday. I have it written down."

"He said *find it and bring it back*, and the sentence has no place in it, because there is no *back*." Tamsin's voice did not rise. "That stone has been at the yard-side landing since before this chapel was a chapel. It was set there by the same three jurisdictions that own the lock, in a public act, in a year in living memory, at the end of a line on a table in a book. If you lift it and put it where it was before, you are not repairing a thing. You are moving the boundary back a hundred yards and letting the water find the edge itself, and the water will find it, and it will find it four hundred people's rehousing again, and it will find it in about nine years instead of about nine minutes, and there will be no table, and no witnesses, and no cost column, and no man from Aurel in a good coat to be blamed for it." She looked at Adrian. "And it will be done by the man in the book, in the mud, on a Saturday, with a bar, because a man in a good coat told him in a chapel that a stone was out of its place."

The yard was very quiet. Roke had the slate on his knee and had not written anything on it for eleven minutes.

"Which is why," Adrian said, "his route was true in every particular except the one that mattered."

"Yes," said Tamsin. "And you refused it in a room with nine people in it on Wednesday night, and you refused it for the wrong reason, and I have not corrected you, and I would like to correct you now, in a yard, in daylight, before we do anything else." She looked at him levelly. "You refused it because it was cheap. It was not cheap. It was the most expensive thing anybody has put in front of you, and it had a *useful* cost in it, which is the only kind of lie that works, and if somebody offers you a lie with a useful cost in it, then the lie is not about the cost. The lie is in the fact that it was offered at all."

---

The keeper named the price at ten past twelve, and she named it standing in the mud in the yard with the board of lime and clay under her arm, and she named it to the yard and not to Adrian.

"You have asked me for my ground," said Nyla Ferrow. "My mother's ground. It is not mine in the way a lease is yours. There is a woman in this yard whose business is a word and a decision, and it is not money and I am not going to hear a number."

"Say it," said Vask.

"For as long as the ground is open, the binding is done outside." The keeper said it in the ordinary voice she used for everything, and it took about four seconds to arrive in English, and when it arrived it went round the yard twice. "The arch's promise is in the floor. The floor is in pieces. So from the day you put the bar in until the day you make the floor good again — and that is a season, and I will tell you now that it is a season and not less — the words are said in this yard, in the open, twice a day, in whatever weather, by whoever is nearest."

"That is a rota," said Dante, from the wall.

"It is a rota, and I am not the one who makes it, and you are not the one who makes it, and the man in the book is not the one who makes it." Nyla Ferrow looked around the yard at about twenty people. "It is the nearest person. That is the whole rule. A person walks through this yard at six in the morning and there is nobody else here, and the words get said or they do not get said, and if they do not get said then the arch is a doorway to a room with a hole in it, and a doorway over a hole is how you get a hole that is not a drain."

"You cannot do it yourself," Adrian said.

"I cannot do it myself," she agreed. "I will be in the hole. That is what my ground is for. I have been the one who said the words in this room for thirty years and I have said them in a warm room standing on a good floor, and the difference between that and saying them in a yard in the root month with the wind coming off the river is the difference between a promise and a *cost*, and if the cost is not there then the promise is a habit, and I have watched two arches in my life stop being arches because nobody could remember what they were costing."

"And the price is that somebody in this city says four words twice a day in the rain for a season," Adrian said, "and it is not a fee and it cannot be a fee, because a fee can be paid once and this cannot."

"Yes."

"Then it goes in the book as a labour and not as a payment," said Sera Vail, who had come down at eleven and had been standing in the archway with a slate under her arm for an hour, and who now came out into the mud and held the slate against her chest like a woman carrying a table. "I will want it in writing whose words they are, because the words matter and the hand matters and a man saying my mother's words in my mother's yard in the wrong voice is a different promise from a woman saying them." She looked round. "I will enter the labour as a rota, on the wall, in the yard, in the open, to be read by anyone who has to stand in it, and the first name on it will be the name of a person who has agreed to learn the four words and the fifth and say both. And there will not be a money column on it, and there will not be a name on it who is in charge of it, and if anybody tries to put a name at the top of that page I will strike it out in front of the yard."

---

The first descent was set for the second Saturday from now, at low water, with the terms entered, and the yard emptied at about half past two, and about forty people went up the stair to the lock and did the ordinary work of an ordinary Saturday in the rain.

The terms were the shortest and the strangest set of terms Adrian had ever read for a piece of work, and they took forty minutes and Vask insisted on every one of them.

One: the cut is in the floor, at the hairline in the flagstones, four feet beyond the threshold slab at the foot of the arch — the line that opened in this room on the first morning of the flood and carried water uphill and has been dry and still ever since — and not in the threshold slab itself, which every person in this city steps over and which is not part of this job and which nobody touches.

Two: the dig stops the moment the void is found, and it does not go further into the void on that day, and the bar goes in no further than Vask's own judgement, and Vask's judgement is not appealable by anybody including the holder of the pull.

Three: it is not a rescue. Adrian said this out loud in front of the yard because he had noticed that nobody else had, and Roke had written it on the slate: *nobody is going down to fetch anybody, because there is nobody to fetch, and if anybody starts saying they have somebody down there, they are wrong, and I will come up and say so myself.*

Four: it is not a door. Nobody goes into the ground to come out of it somewhere else, and anybody who says otherwise in a market is to be brought to the yard and shown the terms.

Five: the binding is done in the yard from the first day, and the binding is done before the bar goes in, not after.

And then Vask went and got a board and chalked the keeper's four words and the keeper's fifth on one side of it, in the keeper's own hand, because Nyla Ferrow had written them out before anybody asked her to, and asked for them to be legible from four feet away, and had not been thanked for it and did not appear to want to be.

On the back of the board, in a mason's square hand, was a list.

> **WHAT MAY NOT COME OUT OF THIS HOLE**
>
> 1. **The stone in its place.** It is not in the hole. It is in the footing, four steps left of the yard, and it is not ours, and nobody lifts it, and if a man in a good coat comes with a sentence about putting a stone back, he is read the first line of this list.
>
> 2. **Water, in any vessel, for any reason.** If it runs in, it runs in. Nobody carries it out in a jar and nobody is going to be told it is a curiosity.
>
> 3. **Any writing.** If there is writing down there it is somebody's, and it stays where they put it until we know whose, and "until we know whose" is not a date.
>
> 4. **Anything that answers.**

Somebody at the back of the yard — a docker Adrian did not know, forty-odd, with his arms folded and rain running off him — said, "What's that mean, anything that answers?"

And Vask, who was thirty-four years in this city and had been down in worse holes than this one under a worse flood, looked at the board, and then at the man, and then at the arch.

"It means don't take a voice," she said. "It does not mean there is one. I have never heard one and I have never been told there is one and I have read the register of this chapel from the year it was built and it does not appear in it once." She chalked nothing else. "It is on the list because I put it on the list, and I put it on the list because I have been in a hole once in this city with three good men and a bar, and we came up with four hours of something in our heads that belonged to somebody else, and it was not a voice, and it was worse than a voice, and I am not going to explain it to a man in a wet yard, and I am not going to take it off the list either."

---

They put him in the hole.

That was the last item of the morning and Adrian had not expected it and never forgot it. Ovin Sarr, who was nineteen, came off the walk-round at ten minutes past one and said that if a man was going to stand in a void under a floor then the man who could see where the void stopped would be the man they would ask, and Roke said yes, and Nyla Ferrow said *no*, and then said yes, and the yes came with a condition, which was that Vask held the ladder herself and nobody else touched the rope.

He went down on a rope at half past one with a lantern tied to his belt and Vask on the ladder above him, in a void two feet across at the top and a foot and a half at his shoulders, and he put his right hand flat on the north wall of it, and blood came out of his nose about forty seconds later, warm, and went down the front of his shirt into the dark, exactly as it had gone down the front of a shirt in a stone room under a river on his first day, and he was glad of it in a way that would have appalled him in a different week.

There was no join.

That was the thing he had to say afterwards, and he said it in the yard, in front of eleven people, in the flat voice, and it was the sentence he would be most proud of in a year and it cost him more than anything else that happened that day.

"I can see the line four inches to my left, running up into the north wall, and it is clean, and it is the boundary of the void and it is not a damage. I can see it about two feet up. And above that there is nothing." He took his hand off the wall. "I can see the *edge* of something. I cannot see into it, and I have been in a hole for four minutes and I have been able to feel the line, and it went about a minute ago and my nose has been bleeding since, and I would have gone on for another hour if you had let me, and you should not have let me, and I want it said in this yard that I would have gone on, because that is the answer to the question all of you are going to ask me in about a fortnight. And I cannot tell you what is past the top of that line, and I am not going to stand in a yard and describe a shape to you that I have got from guessing."

"Can you find out where it is safe to stand?" Vask said.

"I can find out where the *edge* is. And I can tell you where the edge is three times in a row now, and I was right three times, and the fourth time I did it I was wrong in a way that put me on the floor in front of five men on a walk-round, and that was on the Thursday of the second tide, and it was nine days ago, and I was wrong in public and I have not been right in a better way since." He came up the ladder on shaking arms. "I am a lamp. I am not a road. I can show a mason where to put a hand. I cannot tell anybody where a floor stops being a floor, and if anybody in this city wants a man to tell them where a floor stops being a floor, I am the wrong man, and I have been the wrong man since Tuesday and I did not know it until this morning."

Ottoline Vask looked at him for a moment, and then said, in the local language, "Good," and Tamsin translated it as *that is the first thing you have said this month that I am going to be able to use in a book* — and then, before Adrian had worked out what that meant, she sent him out of the yard, on the grounds that he had been in a hole and the hole was finished and there was no reason on earth for a man with blood on his shirt to be standing in front of thirty people while she chalked a board.

Adrian wrote it in the notebook that night, at the top of a clean page, and the last line of the page was the one he did not cross out:

*The twelfth day. It is not a rescue and it is not a door, and the man who wrote line four is the only person I have met in this world who is frightened of the correct thing, and I have a feeling I am going to be frightened of the correct thing for a long time.*
