# Chapter 0043 — The Man With the Chalk

On the eighteenth day of the new month, which was the forty-third day of the flood and a Tuesday and the next tide, forty men of this city's own crew said four words under the near gate twice at their ordinary volume, out of the line and not together, as they have done every morning and every evening since the twenty-fifth day of the flood, and a mason of thirty-four years took a fresh reading of the pale edge in the main breach and it had moved a quarter of an inch, and a crack does not step, and a boy of nineteen with a stick of chalk wrote the ordinary word in the last column of the tide table in a gallery under a lock, and the man who has been going down those two hundred steps for forty days stood four feet back with a folding steel rule in his hand and did not write it, and was not asked to.

---

The tide of the forty-third day of the flood was a working one and it was at the top at about the fourth hour, and the box in the last column of the tide table had been empty for ten days, and a docker with a barrow wrote the word against the thirty-third on a Saturday morning in a hand that is not hers and not a clerk's and pressed the *k* too hard, and nobody has ever asked her what she decided and she has told about nine people in a market that she decided nothing and that on Tuesday she would not be able to say what she wrote.

She was right about the Tuesday.

---

Forty men went under the near gate at about the sixth hour and again at about the sixth hour of the evening. Nineteen of them are the day crew and twenty-one of them are the flood crew, and the foreman of the day crew is a man called Hesk, and he did not go under it either time, and he has not gone under it once since the twenty-fifth day of the flood, and about nine people in this city have worked that out and about four of them have said so in a market.

They said the four words at the volume they say them and in the voices they say them and out of the line and not together, because a docker in a felt hat made a crew promise it in a ledger on the eighteenth day of the flood, and the promise was read back four times, and the reason it was made is entered in the book and is the reason that the twenty-fifth day of the flood cannot later be called a thing a crowd did.

The oath was not said louder today. Nobody chanted. A man on the third step of the gallery had a slate and wrote down the hour and the number of men and the volume, and a docker at the top of the second stair had a slate and wrote down the hour, and there are two figures in the book about what was said under that gate on the morning of the eighteenth day of the new month and neither of them is a number of words and both of them are the ordinary figure, and Vail has entered that the two men were not asked to agree and does not intend to make them.

---

Adrian Vale was in the lower gallery at about half past four in the morning with a folding steel rule and a stub of chalk and nothing else, and he was put four feet back by a woman with tweezers who has put him four feet back four times in forty days and who does not have a name in this book that anybody uses, and the reason she gives is not about him.

"A keeper's whole job is giving a man a place that is not the hole," Nyla Ferrow said, at about the seventh hour of the morning on the sixteenth day of the new month, in the yard, to about nine people, when somebody in a yard asked her why the man with the rule is never where the work is. "It is not the hole. That is the whole of it. He has been in this yard and in that gallery for forty days and I have never once let him stand where the ground is and I am not going to start, and if you want to know why it is because a keeper is the only person in this city who gets paid in not being anywhere, and there is a woman of this city who has spent a month putting a man's name in the sentence to keep somebody out of a hole and I will not do it twice."

The staging was up in the second bay and it was up because the pale edge had to be read at a falling tide, and a rule and a man with a rule are the reason a staging goes up, and a man with a rule is the reason Vask has gone up on it four times in forty days.

She called the number at about the seventh hour, from eleven feet up, with a bar across her knees.

"Eight and five-eighths, and it is the same as it was on the fortieth, and the marks have not moved, and I have chalked a new one and it is in the same place as the other four, and if it is not in the same place as the other four then I have put my hand wrong and I would like to be told."

It was in the same place.

That is the twenty-third reading, and it is the first one in eleven days, and there are three ruled lines in the column between the twenty-second and this one, drawn in a man's own hand, and a ruled line is a fact and a missing line is a fight.

And then, at about the eighth hour, when the water had come off the staging and Vask was down it and the tide was on the turn, a boy of nineteen who has had seven watches in three weeks asked her for the rule.

---

**Ovin Sarr** is nineteen. He came into this city on the second morning of the flood out of a hole in a wall and was pulled out of it by a man from another world, and he has never had a watch in this city that anybody could say was a gift, and he has had seven of them in three weeks, and on the thirty-third day of the flood he stood on the sill at the top of the second stair and said *it is not the tide* and it was entered and believed, and on the fortieth day of the flood a man in a good coat put a man on a roof to watch him for one morning and he has not been able to say in a room what the watch is for and tried for an hour.

He asked for the rule at about the eighth hour of the morning of the eighteenth day of the new month and nobody gave it to him, and he asked twice, and the second time Ottoline Vask gave it to him and held the chalk for him because the stub was wet, and the reading is his and the number is his and he wrote it on the wall of the second bay under the docker's *nine*, and the *nine* is a different number and has never been this number, and he wrote his number in his own hand about four inches under a docker's and about nine inches to the left of it, and the two of them are on that wall together and no one has moved either.

He read it back in the local language and got it out in the wrong order and read it again the right way, and then he said: "It is not the same."

"It is not the same," said Vask.

"It moved."

"It moved a quarter of an inch and a crack does not step and I have been a mason for thirty-four years and I have never in my life seen a crack step in a wall of dressed stone unless a man has taken something out of it or put something into it, and a man has done one of those things in this gate twice today, and I did it, and here it is, and I put it in the book at the seventh hour this morning before I came down the staging, and it is in my own hand and it is the hour, and it says that at the turn I took the old near-gate prop out and put a new one in at the sill because the staging leg had to go where the old one was, and that I ordered it, and that it took eleven minutes, and that I did not think about the pale edge at all when I did it because I have not thought about the pale edge since the twenty-fifth day of this flood and I have thought about the pale edge every day since the twenty-fifth day of this flood, and those are two true sentences and the second one is the one I would like in the book."

Nobody in that gallery said anything for about four seconds, and about nine people in that gallery had already decided what the number was.

---

It was a woman of forty-one from Silt Row with a slate.

**Wenna Cray** is forty-one and she is the first complainant this city has and she is on page one against Adrian Vale's name, in her own hand, with the words *and he was asked in public*, and she entered the second statement of error in the history of the Lantern Concord on the twelfth day of the new month against the eleventh page and against the holder of the pull, and it was correct, and he did not answer it.

She came into the lower gallery at about the eighth hour with a slate and about four people and stood in the water to the knee in the second bay and said:

"Two questions and I want them answered in front of the crew and not in a book.

"**One. Are you going to enter that as a fact or as a fault?**

"Because there are nine people in this gallery who have decided it is a fault and one of them is me, and I am not going to pretend I did not decide it in the first four minutes, and the reason I decided it is that a fault can be given to somebody and a fact can only be carried, and I have watched this city for forty days and I have watched what it does with a fact.

"And if you are going to enter it as a fault then say so now, in front of nineteen men, and say whose fault, and I will write it down and it will go in a book and it will be somebody's to answer for in March, and that is a thing this city can do. And if you are going to enter it as a fact then say that too, and I am going to be one of the nine who does not believe you, and I want to be able to point at the hour."

Adrian Vale said: "**A fact.** And I am going to say the rest of it in front of the crew because you asked me in front of the crew, and it takes about forty seconds.

"I read a number. The number is eight and seven-eighths and it is a quarter of an inch wider than it was on the fortieth and the marks are in the same place, so the stone did not move by itself; a man moved it, and the man is standing on this staging in front of me and has written down the hour and the thing he did in his own hand before I came into this gallery.

"**And I am not going to enter it as a fault because it is not a fault and because I have entered three things as faults in this city in forty days and two of them were not faults, and I would like that said in the same entry.**

"In a yard in the rain on the eighteenth day of the flood I wrote the site of a rescue as *a dressed joint, described on the twenty-fourth day and not named*, and a mason had said on the twenty-fourth day that a quarter of a thing is not a thing, and that description is true of two places in this city, and the crossing went to the one that had never been named, and a man is under a board eight feet behind an ordinary wall. **That was a fault. It was mine. I have not entered it as one and I have been asked about it by a woman of forty-one and I am going to enter it as one on the day this city has a book that can hold it and not before.**

"This is not that. A mason took a prop out and put one in at a sill at the turn of a tide because a staging leg had to go somewhere, and she entered it before I read the number and I did not ask her to, and if you want a fault then there is one and it is a working staging on a working tide, and that is a thing about this city and not about a person, and if it is entered as a fault it will be entered against about forty days of this city's ordinary maintenance of its own lock, and that is a fault a city can be angry at, and a city that is angry at itself has a thing to do in the morning."

"And the boy is standing here," said Wenna Cray.

"And the boy is standing here," Adrian Vale said, "and nine of you decided about four seconds ago that the number was his, and none of you had said so and all of you had decided it, and he is nineteen and he has had seven watches in three weeks and he did not touch a stone in this gate this morning and I would like the first of you who decided it to say so out loud in a room, because he is going to hear about it in a market on Thursday and he is going to hear it as *somebody thought it was me* and that is a different thing from *somebody thought it was me and said it*."

Nobody said it out loud in a room.

Ovin Sarr said, at about the eighth hour and a half, in the local language, badly, and it is in the book in his own words because he asked for it to be:

"Somebody is going to say it was me. I have been in this gallery seven times and I have never taken a stone out of it and I have been thinking about it since the fortieth day when a man in a good coat put a man on a roof to watch me and I could not say in a room what the watch is for, and today I asked a woman for her rule twice and the second time she held the chalk for me because it was wet, and that is all I did.

"**And I would like the two things entered, and they are both true and they are in the wrong order for me: that I asked for the rule twice, and that nobody gave it to me the first time, and I would like the first one left in.**"

---

The man on the wall was two hundred steps from the gallery and he was not a docker and he was not a gate hand and he had no standing in that building at all, and he was on the wall of the second bay at about the seventh hour of the morning of the eighteenth day of the new month with his right hand on a hauling line, because the staging comes up on a line and the line has to be held.

**Jonah Pike** has been in this city thirty-eight days and he came out dry and has stayed dry, and on the thirty-fourth day of the flood he proposed in a yard in front of about a hundred and forty people that the seven of them have a dry building on the upper road and shared equipment and work rights at the lock, for exclusive access, and about thirty people walked out at item five, and the sentence that would have ended it was one Adrian Vale could not say to a man who had been his ally for three weeks.

He did not say anything on that morning except one thing, on his way past, at about the eighth hour, to a man standing four feet back from a wall with a rule in his hand.

He said: "**The man who cannot be replaced is not a holder. He is a bottleneck with a name on it, and you have been one for forty days, and the only reason nobody has broken it yet is that nobody has wanted to.**"

And then he went back to his line and said nothing else, and he held the line for about two hours, and he did not say anything else, and Adrian Vale has entered that he did not say anything else and has entered the reason: **that I have been waiting three weeks for that man to say one wrong thing so that I could be finished with him, and he said a true thing instead, and I have got a book with a hole in it where an argument was going to be, and I do not know what to do with a true thing from him, and that is twice.**

---

**The word in the box.**

The last column of the tide table on the wall of the lower gallery has one word in it for every tide of the salt year and the word is *works* or it is blank, and whoever holds the chalk that day writes it, and Adrian Vale has been going down those two hundred steps for forty days with a stub of chalk in his hand and had not written in that box once, and on the night of the seventeenth day of the new month he stood under it at one in the morning and did not write in it, and there is a note in the book at the second hour of that morning that says he was asked to and did not, and it does not say he was asked.

He was not asked.

The box had been empty for ten days and about nine people in that gallery had noticed the ten days and about four of them had said so in a market, and at about the eighth hour of the morning of the eighteenth day of the new month a boy of nineteen came down the two hundred steps with a stick of chalk in his hand because he had been sent for it, and nobody sent him, and he says he was sent for it, and there is a docker in a felt hat who has entered that she sent him for it and that she sent him because the box was empty and it was a Tuesday and somebody had to be in the box before the tide turned.

He stood under the board and he looked at it for about a minute and a half, and about nine people in that gallery watched him look at it, and then he wrote one word in the box against the forty-third day of the flood.

He wrote **works**.

He wrote it small, and he put the *k* on too hard, and he stood back about four feet and looked at it, and then he turned round and said, in the local language, to about nine people:

"**Is that the right word?**"

"It is the word that goes in it," said Adrian Vale, in the local language, in about four seconds, without hesitating, and Tamsin Quill did not correct him.

"It is not the only word," said Ovin Sarr.

"That is true," said Adrian Vale. "And it is the only one anybody has ever written in that column, and there are about four hundred tides in that column and about nine of them are blank for reasons that are in a book somewhere, and the box was empty for ten days and a woman with a barrow has told nine people in a market that she decided nothing when she filled in the one before it, and I have not written in that box once in forty days and I was standing right here this morning at about one in the morning with the chalk in my hand and I did not write in it, and I would like all of that in a book, and I would like it in a book now, before the tide turns, and not on Friday."

And Vail entered it at about the ninth hour of the morning of the eighteenth day of the new month, in the flat hand, with the hour, and it is on the page: **that the box against the forty-third day of the flood was written in at about the eighth hour of the morning of that day by Ovin Sarr, nineteen, of this city, out of the day crew, with a stick of chalk, and that the holder of the pull was present and did not write it and was not asked to and did not offer, and that the box had been empty for ten days, and that this is entered because an empty box on a tide table is the only place in this city where nothing is claimed by anybody and a box that has been empty for ten days is not a claim anybody can make about it, including the man standing under it.**

---

And then, at about the ninth hour, in a yard, Ovin Sarr was given the bottom field.

That is the whole of it and it took about four minutes and it was given by a docker of about fifty-eight in a boat-coat who has not kept a record of anything in her life and who stopped a rota from existing in the first week of the flood and who named the chain of asking and who told Adrian Vale on the fortieth day of the flood who actually decides.

He has wanted it since the second week of the flood. It is entered in the book, in his own words, at the twelfth hour of the night of the fourteenth day of the flood, and it is four words long: **that I want the field.**

The bottom field is a hundred and forty acres four miles down the Reach road and a season of it was spent on the twenty-fifth day of the flood and has been entered five times in a month in this city's book, and the debt is on Adrian Vale personally, and the ditching of it has been done since the twenty-seventh by nineteen unnamed people putting flour on ditch banks at four in the morning, which is a number that is not a season.

"There is no crew for it and there is no money for it," said Renna Ord. "There is the flood-creel shift and the reed-cutting and the ditching and the four o'clock work at Corrow, and the day crew is nineteen men and they do a lock, and the lock is a lock, and if you take nineteen men off the lock to dig a ditch then there are no men under the near gate at six and at six, and I have said in this city for forty days that a crew is a man who has owed somebody a shift and I am not going to now be a woman who sends a boy to a field and empties a gate.

"So it is not a crew's work. It is a day's work and it is yours and you can hand it back."

"Then it is not the field," said Ovin Sarr.

"It is not the field," said Renna Ord. "Say why."

And the boy of nineteen said, in the local language, in about nine seconds, and it is in the book in his own words, and Vask read it back in English afterwards because there were about forty people in the yard and about half of them would not have had it:

"**A field is not a day's work. A field is a thing a man is asked for again next season, and a day's work is a thing a man hands back, and I asked for the field, and I have been in this city eighteen days and I asked a woman for the field because I thought the field was a thing you could be given like a watch, and it is not, and I have been watching a man in a good coat for a month and a half from this city and I have learned that the difference between a thing and a duty is whether anybody can send you back.**

"And I would like it entered that I said that out loud, and that I am not going to take the day's work, and that I would like to be asked again when there is a season of it and not a day of it, and if there is not a season of it then I would like to be told that there is not going to be one, and not asked every winter."

And Renna Ord said: "That is the fourth line of a term sheet backwards and you are nineteen. **Ask Elsa Skell about the season in the spring and put it in a book and not in a market**, and I will ask her with you, and I will not promise you that there is one."

And the boy said, "All right," and went down to the field at about the ninth hour and worked it until about the fourth hour of the afternoon with about nine other people and a mason of fifty who went along, and it is entered that he was there for about six hours and that nobody asked him to be and that about four of the nine were there for the flour and not the ditch.

---

**The measurement is entered, and the debt is enlarged, and nothing is resolved.**

The twenty-third reading is **eight and seven-eighths**, and the width of the join in the main breach is a quarter of an inch greater than it was on the fortieth day, in one step, and the four chalked marks on the wall of the second bay have not moved and there are five of them now, and the step fell on the eighteenth day of the new month at the turn of a tide, and a crack does not step, and a man took a prop out of that gate at the seventh hour and entered it in her own hand before anybody asked her to.

And the cost column of that day went into the book at the ninth hour along with the reading, and a season of the bottom field was entered against it for the sixth time in one month.

Sera Vail read out her own sentence again, in a gallery, in the flat voice, and she said it in the same words she said in a yard in the rain on the twelfth day of the new month, and about nine people in that gallery knew it by the first four words.

> **A cost entered five times is not a cost any more. It is a notation, and I am the notary and it is my column.**

And then she said, and it is in the entry, in her own hand:

"**And it is now six times, and I am not going to say that sentence a second time in a room, and it stands, and I am going to say the other half of it once and then I am going to stop saying it for the rest of the flood: a cost column that has been re-entered at its new size six times in a month is a column this city can afford, and the reason it is a column this city can afford is that the re-entries are all about the same field, and the field is four miles away, and about four hundred people have never met the man the column is in.**

"And I would like it entered that I have said that in a gallery with nineteen men in it and that about nine of them have heard it and that none of them works at that field, and that is not an accusation, and it is a fact about a column."

Nothing gained. A number, a boy, a word in a box, a day of ditching, and a sixth entry in a column that a court will like the look of.

And in the notebook, at about the tenth hour of the morning of the eighteenth day of the new month, in his own hand, Adrian Vale wrote the two things he was taking off the page and putting on the wall, and they are these: **that the step is a fact and the decision behind it is entered, and that a fact is only cheaper than a fault for the person it happens to.** And under that, in a different ink, because he had found the stub: **that the box is not empty and I did not fill it and he did, and I have been standing under that board for forty days with a piece of chalk in my hand waiting for a day that would make it mine, and there is not going to be one, and that is the best thing that has happened to me this week.**
