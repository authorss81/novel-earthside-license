# Chapter 0013 — The Crown's Handwriting

The letter came up from the river boat on Tuesday night in a leather tube and lay on the Concord table on Wednesday morning with a salt stain on it, and it was four pages long, and every word of it was courteous.

Adrian had spent a night with it and had got to the point where he could recite the third page, which was the page that mattered, and on Wednesday morning he was in the hall at a quarter to eight with the assessor's table cleared in front of him and Tamsin on one side and Sera Vail on the other, and Sera Vail was not reading the letter.

She was reading a piece of paper about a salt page, which had come in from a village and which was a problem, and which was apparently the more urgent of the two things on the table.

"You are going to want me to help you answer it," she said, before he had said anything. "I am not going to help you answer it, and I would like that established at the start, because in about twenty minutes somebody is going to say that the letter is a civic matter and I want it on the record that I said no to that before it was said."

"Understood."

"I am going to read the *letter*. A letter is a thing that arrived. Reading it is the whole of my trade and I will do it in both languages and I will read the enclosure as well because the enclosure is a document and documents are what I do." She squared it. "And then you are on your own."

---

The letter was from the Office of Crossings and Tides at Aurel, and it was signed by **Anwen Kroon**, Registrar, who was a person and not a department, which Adrian had already decided was the most frightening kind of person in any civilisation.

It opened by thanking the Lantern Concord for its attention. It described the damage to the Lock of Salt in language that was so accurate that Adrian read three pages of it with a cold feeling going down his back, because the Crown knew things about this lock that nobody in the hall had told them: that the gate's third arch had been repointed in the year of the wet winter, that the outer channel's wall had been scabbed three times, that the near gate's counterweight was original. Somebody had read the maintenance roll at Aurel. Somebody had been paid to read it.

The third page was the turn.

> It is not the concern of this office to determine whether the arrangements concluded at Saltwake are proper, nor to disturb any question of local law which the Lantern Concord has opened. My office notes only that a crossing, once it has occurred, is thereafter a fact that will be administered by somebody, and that the administration of it may reasonably be expected to fall to a single authority, since a thing administered by several is, in the experience of this office, a thing not administered.
>
> The Crown therefore finds that the question *who is answering for the bridge* must be answered, and must be answered in writing, and by a name.

And the enclosure was four hundred words long and it was called **Schedule Four: Answerable Party**, and it had a header and a reference number and a date of issue, and it had exactly one field in the middle of it, and the field was a line, and above the line it said *name of the person answerable*, and below the line there was nothing at all.

"There is no other box," Adrian said.

"There is no other box," said Aldis Kerrow.

"There is no box that says *no body is answering this*. There is no box that says *the answer is held by four named witnesses and a receiving community and may be suspended by nine households*. There is no box that says *nobody yet*."

"There is no other box," Kerrow agreed, "and I want to say, as the officer of this Concord who has read the Crown's forms for twenty years, that this is not laziness. Crown forms are not lazy. A form with one field and no exit is the most expensive kind of form there is and it takes a committee two sessions to approve, and this office is very good at its work, and I say that as a man who has spent twenty years being signed things."

---

Marrow came in at half past eight and was extremely useful, which Adrian was beginning to find the most frightening thing about him.

He read the letter standing up, in about ninety seconds, and put it down.

"Well," he said. "Then that is the Crown, and the Crown has taken four days over a letter, which means there is somebody in Aurel who is frightened. Not Kroon. Kroon is a hand. There is a person behind the hand who has read about Tuesday morning and has not slept, and that person has done the sensible thing, which is not to send soldiers."

"What would you have sent?"

"I would have sent what was sent," Marrow said. "A form. A form is what you send when you want a question answered without appearing to want it answered. It is the most courteous instrument ever devised and it is the reason this kingdom has survived for nine hundred years." He tapped the enclosure. "Register the crossing. The Crown recognises the site, the witnesses are entered in the Crown's roll, the cost becomes a Crown cost, and a Crown engineer stands in the join under a Crown warrant. The officer in the join would then be obliged to answer to a person with a warrant rather than continue to spend the river in a field nobody has agreed to. He would be *stopped*, Vale, or at least he would be *asked*, in a form, by men who can be replied to."

"That is a good outcome," Adrian said, before he could stop himself, and heard himself, and went cold.

"It is a good outcome," Marrow agreed. "It is also the fastest possible way to remove the last thing in this city that nobody can do alone. You will have a Crown answerable party, and the answerable party will be a clerk in Aurel, and the clerk will be a man, and the man will be permanent in the way that only men with a warrant are permanent."

Vail looked up from her paper for the first time in an hour.

"I am going to say the part that matters," she said, "and then I am going to go back to my salt page."

---

"I want you all to understand that I am not an officer of Aurel and I am not defending anything," Kerrow said. He had not sat down all morning. "The Crown's registration would do four things, and only the fourth is the one anybody is frightened of.

"It would give the crossing a legal existence outside the Concord. It would enter your six names in a Crown roll — yours, and Mr Park's, and Miss Okoye's, and every one of the rest, in a building in Aurel, indexed.

"It would attach the cost to the Crown purse. The enclosure offers a labour crew of eight for the season, at the Crown's charge, and a stipend against the verified damage to the bottom field, drawn on the harbour fund and paid into the eleven villages before the ember tide."

He stopped, and looked at the floor, and then said the fourth thing.

"And it would *receive*. That is the technical word. A registered crossing is received for the whole of the territory in which it stands. Saltwake, the Reed Reach, the eleven villages, the two gatehouses, and Nine Elms. All of it. A Crown registration does not have a reduced receiving area, because a Crown does not have a border. It receives everywhere, and the refusal of nine households in a wet village is a matter for their own assembly, whom the Crown will write to separately, and who will be told that their water is received."

The room did not make any sound at all.

"That is how it is done," Kerrow said. "It is not a threat. Nobody has threatened you. It is *procedure*. The Crown's form does not contain a provision against Nine Elms because the Crown's form does not contain a provision against anything. It contains a field for a name, and everything that is not a field is a matter for a later letter, and the later letters are always more expensive than the first one."

---

Adrian got up. He said, "May I speak," and got a strange look from a man in the third row, and then understood that in this building you did not ask, and sat back down.

He sat with his hands flat on the table for about forty seconds, and he looked for the field.

That was the thing he would write about afterwards. He had been, since Wednesday, waiting for the pale panel the way you wait for a lift. It had come on Wednesday night in the chapel and on Thursday morning in the rain and on Friday in the hall, and on Saturday morning outside the chandlery, and it was simply *not there*. Nothing in the air, nothing at the edge of his vision, no form, no consequence, no choice. Four days of offers and warnings and one outright lie about a cost, and on the morning a piece of paper from the capital put the whole of his week in front of him, the thing that had been feeding him the prompts had nothing whatever to say about it.

He found that the silence was louder than the panel had ever been, and that it told him something he did not like: that the messages and the paper were not coming from the same place, and that the man who had been quoting his own sentences back at him through a wall was not the man who wrote the Crown's forms.

He wrote that down later. In the hall, on Wednesday morning, he did not write anything down. He said:

"Then we do not fill it in."

"I am aware," said Kerrow, "that the hall may wish to speak."

"No," said Adrian. "I know. Sorry. I know that is not how this works and I am going to say it anyway and then I am going to let you run it properly. I want it in the book, in my words, before anybody else says it: I will not fill in that field. And the reason is not the Crown, and it is not Aurel, and it is not pride. The reason is that the only thing I have found out in eight days in this city that is worth anything is that a thing has to have a way of being refused in it. Nine Elms refused, and it cost them, and it cost us, and it is in a book in a chain and nobody has been able to argue it away. That refusal is the only part of this week that I would put my name on. And there is no box in that form that lets anybody refuse it, and the whole of my answer is that there is no box in that form that lets anybody refuse it."

He got to the end of that and heard how it sounded, and Kerrow said:

"Moved. Is there a second."

"There is a second," said Tamsin, in English, to nobody, and then in the local language, to the room, and Adrian sat down and let her do it.

She was better at it than he was by an order of magnitude. She did not argue about the form. She read out the four costs again in a flat voice, one at a time, and let the hall have the Crown advocate, and the Crown purse, and the eight men at the Crown's charge, and the harbour fund, and let the hall notice for itself that the fourth item was the one that was doing all the work and that the hall was about to lose it because of it.

And then she read the fifth thing, which was not in the letter at all, and which she had put there herself.

"And a Crown registration is a person answerable," she said. "Which means a Crown clerk will be the man in the seat. And a Crown clerk is not in this room, and cannot be got at, and cannot be got at by nine households on a Tuesday. So the thing we have spent four days arguing about — whether nine households can withdraw a refusal, and whether that refusal is a hearing or a commitment, and whether the four witnesses can stop a man with a mark in his hand — would stop being a question, because a registered crossing cannot be suspended by anybody in Saltwake at all, ever, and I do not have to read you a law to know that the hall would miss that in a season."

---

The reply was drafted, read, and entered between two and four in the afternoon, and it took two hours and a half because Sera Vail insisted on entering the *reasons* as well as the answer, on the grounds that a refusal without reasons is a stone in a wall and she had seen what those do.

It went in the local language, in a hand that was not Adrian's, witnessed by Quill and Roke, with the twenty-third page of the ledger and the chain through the ring.

> To the Office of Crossings and Tides at Aurel.
>
> The Lantern Concord acknowledges Schedule Four and states that it will not complete the field named in it.
>
> The instrument entered at Saltwake on the day of the last tide is a site, a holder, four witnesses, a receiving community for part of an area, and a posted cost. Its recognition is incomplete and it cannot be used. The Concord has opened the question whether a first license may exist in this city and that question is open and undated.
>
> The Concord further states, for the avoidance of doubt and at the request of the receiving community, that no body at Aurel may receive for the water of the nine households of Nine Elms by writing, and that any such attempt will be read at the next sitting of this table and entered.
>
> No name is entered in Schedule Four. The holder of the pull will not complete it. The four witnesses will not complete it. This Concord will not complete it.
>
> The costs of the instrument, including a season of the bottom field, remain as posted on page one, and remain payable by the person named there, and the Crown's offer of compensation is declined with the offer of registration, and the Concord records that it understands that this declines eleven villages money that has been offered them.

And then Vail read the last line back, and Adrian said, "That last sentence is mine and it is the only one I care about."

"It is mine too," said Vail. "I wrote the words *and the Concord records* and I would like it noticed that I am the one who wrote them, in a reply to the Crown, in a public book, on a Wednesday, and that I have been a notary for twenty-six years and I have never once written a sentence into a royal reply that a regent could use against me." She dipped the chalk. "I decided that this morning. I have been waiting since Friday for somebody to give me something worth the risk."

---

Ivenn Marrow left at about four, and said, on his way out, one thing to Adrian in the doorway that nobody else heard.

"You have just refused the only authority in this kingdom that could protect you, in order to protect a village you have never been to. I would like you to sit with that for about ten years and not write it down." He put his hat on. "And Vale — nothing came to you this morning. I watched your face at the table for four hours. Whatever that thing is, it is not a letter-reader."

---

The courier was in the porch.

That was the part Adrian nearly missed, and he only found it because he was in a bad mood and did not want to go and find Tamsin, and walked the wrong way down the hall for eleven paces.

She was sitting on the step of the porch with her back against the jamb and her boots up on the opposite bench and a mail bag between her feet that had the Crown's stitched sigil on the flap, and she was about twenty-four, and she had the look of somebody who has been walking since before light and has decided to stop being tired about it.

"Peth Lammay," she said, before he asked. "I carry the second table's mail on the upper river and the Reach run. Eleven years. I brought the tube."

"You brought the letter."

"I brought the letter, and I brought the letter before the letter," she said, "because there are two of them, and one of them is for a place that is not Saltwake, and I am not permitted to be the one who decides which is which."

Adrian stopped.

"Who is the second one for?"

"For the Reach." She took the bag off the bench and undid the flap. "Addressed to the meeting-ground at Long Water, and to the gatehouses, and to Nine Elms by name, and to the four other villages of the shared gates. It is a formal notice. It is a form. It says the Crown office has opened a file concerning the crossing at the Lock of Salt and requires that the receiving community be informed, and it has a line for a person to receive it, and it is due to be read out at the next meeting, and the next meeting is tomorrow morning, which is in about thirty hours." She looked up. "It has my mark on the front. Which means it goes in a book when it is read, and I stand there while it goes in."

"Then nobody will know it is here."

"No," said Peth Lammay. "Nobody will know it is here, and I have been sitting on this step for an hour working out who to give it to, and I want to tell you that it is not a small thing, and I want you to understand that I am not asking you for anything and I am not being clever."

"Then what are you doing?"

"I am asking you a question nobody in that hall asked me," she said. "Because everybody in that hall has been thinking about who answers for a bridge. Not one of them has thought about how a piece of paper about a village gets to a village. And the answer is *me*, and I am twenty-four, and I live in a room over a boat-yard at Long Water, and I have carried four hundred of these in eleven years and I have never once been asked what happens when the answer on the fourth page is no."

She put the bag back on the bench.

"So I am asking. Does your Concord know that letter exists? Because it does, and it is in my bag, and tomorrow morning it goes in a book in front of the nine households of Nine Elms, and if your answer is written in the Concord's book before the Reach has the letter, then in about a year there will be a hundred people in this city who believe that the village was told, and there will be nine households who know that they were not." She shrugged. "That is all. That is the whole of my question. It has taken me eleven years to work out that a person can be a channel and not a person, and I would like somebody in this building to have noticed."
