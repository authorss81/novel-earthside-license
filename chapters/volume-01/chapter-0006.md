# Chapter 0006 — No Respawn

The dry-goods loft above the river market had two rooms, four beds, a stone stair, and a woman named Sesh Brack who owned all of it and had decided by noon to rent it to them, which Adrian suspected she was going to regret for the rest of her life.

The rooms smelled of mothball and cedar and old wool. The windows faced the river and the lower market, and from the sill you could see forty stalls, the awnings going up in a staggered line, and the whole flat brown width of the Vey with the lock's dark stone shoulder standing out of it in the middle distance like a tooth. Priya had taken the window seat and had not moved from it in three hours, narrating into a phone with no signal, and had got to the point where the narration had become almost entirely logistics: who had water, who had eaten, whose thigh needed re-dressing at the hour.

Adrian came up the stair with the notebook open and a list he had made in the yard, and he stood in the middle of the room with everyone's eyes on him, and for about six seconds he was so happy he could barely hold it.

They needed him. Not in the large way — nobody was calling him a saviour and no one had asked him to lead anything — but in the specific, daily, structural way that made him feel like a load-bearing wall: they needed somebody to know what the flood words meant, and how the lock's working was supposed to be said, and which dock worker had been in the water on Tuesday, and where the linen was.

He had spent nine years building systems that made it unnecessary for anybody to need him, and it had worked beautifully, and nobody had ever once thanked him for it.

"You have got a face on you," Mina said.

"Have I."

"You have. You are enjoying this."

"I'm enjoying being useful."

"That is not the same and I have known you for four hours." She did not say it unkindly. She was re-dressing Priya's forearm — the gash from the wall, cleaned and closed with four stitches she had put in herself with a sewing needle and boiling water — and she did not look up while she said it. "Eat something, and then help me write this list, and stop looking pleased about it, because in about two days you will not be able to do any of this and you will not be able to stop us, and today is the only day the being-useful feeling is going to be free."

---

Here is what Adrian had found out in the twelve hours since the wall, and it came down to one flat sentence: the things they had brought with them were useful, and none of them were anything else.

He had spent the morning's first hour, before the stair, going through it with Sesh in her own yard, because Adrian could not stop making a list and a list was the only thing he had.

A folding spanner. A good steel blade; the dockers had wanted it, badly, and had given a coin for it and then been shocked and then been insulted when he insisted the coin go into the market's common box, which Sesh explained to him was a system for feeding widows and he had, for about nine seconds, wanted to cry.

A multi-tool. Folding, eleven implements, indestructible, and the single most dangerous object in the building, because it was *small* and it *fitted in a pocket* and it was therefore on the rail by every hand in the market, and everyone in Saltwake had already discovered the four ways it could be used and none of them were the way it was meant to be used.

A retractable tape measure, which nobody understood and which Owen had used, memorably, to demonstrate to Dante that the width of the market aisle was "not up to code, my guy, it's not up to *code*," which had produced a silence and a request, from a Hearthguard, that he not use that word again.

A phone. Seven of them. Two of them with a cracked screen, all of them with no signal, no bars, no roaming, and — this was the part that made Priya put hers face down on the sill and go quiet — no clock. The time in the corner of the screen had stopped at 11:52 and would not go forward, and every one of them had tried all of them, and there was no way on earth to know whether that meant the world had stopped or the *watches* had stopped.

A cigarette lighter, which worked, and which was the most valuable object any of them had, and which Adrian put on the market's common box ledger in his own hand under the column for things that were not for sale.

And no magic. None. There was no point in any of them where a thing became a different sort of thing.

Veyran work was bound to relationships. Adrian had watched it all day and had it now in one sentence, which he wrote at the top of a clean page: *A working only works where people are already standing in it. Everything here is tied to somebody. That is not a limitation to be hacked. That is the only reason it works at all.*

He wrote that, and then, underneath, in smaller writing, the sentence that had been trying to get out of him all day:

*Everything here is tied to somebody. Including me. I have been doing this for one day and I am already the most tied thing in the room.*

---

The repair that worked happened at eleven, and it went well.

The market's flat cart — a wide, low, four-wheeled thing for hauling sacks down the aisle — had a cracked hub that had been going for months, and the dockers had wanted it, and a ropemaker had said the hub could be bound with wire, and Owen had produced a folding spanner and Dante had produced a bicycle brake cable from a bike they'd both been looking at rather than at each other for six hours.

They did it in eleven minutes. It was, Adrian conceded privately, magnificent. The hub came apart, got cleaned, got re-packed with grease from a tallow tin, got re-torqued with an Allen key, and got bound in a proper figure-of-eight with brake cable and a crimp. The cart rolled like a thing on rails. Four dockers, two market women, and a child of about five all made a noise.

And then the residue arrived, and it took the rest of the afternoon to understand.

The hub stopped. The wheel was fixed. But the cart was a *joined* thing — the ropemaker had bound its axle into a lashing that was part of the market's daily working, and the lashing had been the witness's side of it, and in fixing the hub Owen and Dante had moved the lashing and had not said the words. So the cart moved, and it moved fine, and the market could not *say* that it was tied.

They found that out at twelve-thirty, when a man came down the aisle with a slip of paper and asked the cart's owner to take it off the water line, because a cart that could move but could not be sworn to could not be counted in the day's chain, and a chain with a hole in it could not be sworn to either.

Nobody shouted at anybody. That was the part Adrian found genuinely frightening. The man with the slip was apologetic. The cart's owner was apologetic. Sesh was apologetic. The whole aisle developed a low, collective, embarrassed tone, the tone of a village apologising to a well, and the day's water went around the end of the aisle, and eleven stalls lost a row of trade, and nobody said the word *residue* out loud but Adrian heard it in the pauses.

"So the wheel works," Mina said, when he found her.

"The wheel is perfect. The cart's in a hole. It's like we fixed a man's leg and he can't be married now."

"That's exactly like that." She looked at him. "You people have very strange words and some of them are about thirty seconds of work. Is that the whole magic? Is that — a man says words and a rope means something?"

"No. I think the words are the *smallest* part of it. I think the words are the part you can copy. I think the actual thing is that everybody who could be affected agreed to be affected." He stopped. "Which is why my repair is sitting in an aisle being apologised to."

The thing that went wrong happened at half past two, and it was Jonah Pike's, and it was much worse.

It was a good idea. The dry-goods stall next to Sesh's had a shutter that had dropped and would not lift, because the lift was a counterweighted arm and the cord had perished, and the stallholder was sixty-eight and had been hauling at it since six in the morning. Jonah had watched this happen four times and had a folded tool in his pocket and a completely reasonable plan: get the arm up, get the weight clear, wedge the arm, replace the cord.

"I can take the weight off in about ninety seconds," he said.

"Nobody touches the front tie," said Sesh Brack.

"It's perished."

"I didn't say it was sound. I said nobody touches the *front tie*. My mother tied that. It's a morning tie. It's got a knot in it that my mother tied and it's got a word said over it and if you cut it, the shutter falls and it does not lift again until it is re-tied on a Sunday, and it is Wednesday."

Jonah had stopped with the tool out.

"I'm not going to cut it. I'm going to lift the arm off the counterweight and put a wedge in—"

"You are going to lift the arm off the counterweight, and the arm is tied into the front tie, because that is the arm that holds the front tie, and if you lift the arm the tie is undone whether you cut it or not." Sesh's voice did not rise. "And the tie being undone is not a rope. It is a Wednesday."

"Nobody told me there was a *tuesday rule*—"

"Nobody tells anybody. You ask. That is the whole of the asking." She turned to Adrian. "Is your friend going to do this?"

Adrian said no before he had decided to, and it was the fastest and cleanest thing he did all day.

Jonah looked at him. He did not look angry. He looked like a man who has just been told the price of something and does not yet believe it.

"She's an old woman in a stall," Jonah said, quietly. "I'm going to lift an arm and put a wedge in. In an hour there'll be a working shutter and no broken fingers. What exactly is the cost of a broken finger, Adrian, versus a Sunday?"

"That's not the trade you're making," Adrian said. "She said the cost was the tie. You don't get to swap in your own arithmetic."

"It is *my* arithmetic. It's my hands and my risk and the stall isn't mine."

And Sesh Brack reached out, fast, and put her hand flat on the front tie, and said, in the local language, four words.

And Adrian, who by now knew three of them, understood two of them, and understood the tone perfectly, said, quietly, "She just called the tie a witness. She's telling you it's a person in that sentence. She's telling you the knot has somebody's name in it."

Which turned out to be very nearly true: the front tie was a daily witnessed tie, and the woman who had tied it every morning for thirty years was standing in the aisle with her hand on it.

Jonah put the tool away.

That was the end of the incident, and the near-thing that ended it was not Jonah's conscience and was not Adrian's authority. It was the woman with the perished cord standing in front of her own stall, in a market full of people who had watched her do this every morning for three decades, being far more frightened of the four words than of anything Adrian could have said.

Sesh cut her hand on the hinge at six o'clock, trying to work the counterweight anyway, alone, with a broken cord and no one to hold the arm.

She got a two-inch laceration across the base of her thumb and a swelling that took eleven days to go down, and the arm came down on her from eight inches, and she did not lose the hand. She was still swearing about it at eight o'clock. Mina Okoye wrote the number of days in the margin of her own book, and wrote the word SURVIVABLE under it in capitals, and underlined it twice.

---

The proposal came at one o'clock, over Sesh Brack's good bread, and it came from Jonah Pike, and it was the most sensible thing anybody had said in Saltwake all day, which was the problem.

He had done his sums. He had them on the back of a market list, in a hand that was neater than Adrian's, and they were good sums. There were four other rooms above the chandlery on the upper road that a widow was letting by the week and that the widow would not let to six people who had no local standing and no way of paying. There was a man on the far side of the river who bought brass, tin, and good steel at a price that would make every Earthside tool in Adrian's pocket worth its weight four times over, and who wanted nothing except to be the only channel. And there was, in Jonah's words, "a woman in the Concord hall who is going to be writing down everything that man from the wall says, and nothing anybody else says, and that is a *distribution* problem, and distribution problems are my whole adult life."

"So here is the offer," he said. "I go and get the chandlery rooms. I pay the widow, I pay the brass man, I take the lease in my own name because that is the only way anybody here will paper it. And in exchange, all of it goes through me. Every word that comes out of that wall, every question anybody in this room wants to ask, every fact we turn up about the lock and the flood and the man in the room upstairs — it goes out through one mouth, and the mouth is mine, because I am the only one of us who can sit at a table with a man who owns a warehouse and not come away owing him."

"Who is it that gets the housing?" Priya said.

"Us."

"Who is it that gets to be asked?"

Jonah looked at her. "The same six people."

"And who is it that decides what the six people are asked?"

He did not answer immediately, and the pause was the whole argument, and Adrian watched it happen and felt something go cold and familiar down his spine, because he had seen that pause before, in a mirror, in a hundred playtests, in the exact two seconds when a room full of people looked at a man who had just said *trust me* and understood that they were already being asked to.

Pike," he said. "Pike, I have designed that screen."

"You have *what*?"

"Not you. The same idea. In a different room, for money, to make strangers care about a fictional empire." Adrian turned his notebook round and put it on the table face down, which was the only way he had of keeping his hands off it. "Exclusive access, one trusted voice, everything routed through the person with the key, everybody else told it's temporary. It always works. It works because it is *easier*, and because being the man who knows is the most valuable thing a frightened person can be sold. And then in about four months nobody in the room can tell the difference between a thing that was decided and a thing that was allowed, and by then it isn't a system any more, it's a man with a list, and the list starts with your six names and it ends up being the only list anybody's heard of."

"Say the last part again," said Jonah Pike, in a completely level voice.

"The list ends up being the only list anybody's heard of. You keep the gate. That is what I am describing. I have never once in nine years built one that did not end up being a gate, and I have watched four studios try to be careful about it and I have watched all four of them end up exactly where this ends up."

"Which is where?"

"Which is where you are standing right now, being extremely reasonable, and being extremely right, and about eleven weeks from now being the only person in the room with a key that nobody can check." Adrian pushed the notebook away. "I'm not saying you're doing it for yourself. I have known men doing it for the group and I have watched them never once notice the moment it stopped being for the group."

The room was quiet. Tamsin, who had come up the stair twenty minutes ago and had been listening from the doorway with her arms folded, said in English:

"Ask him the question he has not asked. Ask him who is not in the six."

"There are six of us—"

"There are seven of you in this room and there are eleven people in the yard who stood in a working for you yesterday for no money, and there are about four hundred people in this city whose names you cannot say yet." She uncrossed her arms. "You are describing a gate. The question is whether it is a gate into a room or a gate across a road. Because if it is into a room, it is the most sensible proposal anybody has made since Tuesday, and I will help you make it work, and I will do it as your bookkeeper. And if it is across a road, then in a year the people on this side of it will be asking you for permission to have a roof, and you will have to say no."

"I didn't say I wanted—"

"You did not say you did not," said Tamsin.

Jonah Pike folded his piece of bread in half, and then in half again, and set it down on the table, and Adrian watched a decent man take a very hard night and do it well.

"All right," he said. "No. Not exclusive. Tell me the alternative and I will tell you why it fails, and if the answer is good enough I will use my own money to buy it."

That was the moment Adrian actually knew the six of them were going to survive long enough to matter. It was not the moment he admired Jonah, and it was not the moment he was proud. Jonah had found out he was about to build a wall around six people, had refused to build it, and had gone straight to work on the other version. Adrian thought: *he did that in about four seconds, and I have watched men take four hours to do the same thing and get a committee out of it.* And then, in the voice he did not trust: *watch him.*

Then Priya said, to the window, in the flat voice she had been using for three hours: "For the record, the offer was made and the offer was refused in this room at one o'clock, and I have got it, and I am putting it in the log whether it happens or not."

"For the record," Jonah said. "Put in the log that I made it."

"I was going to."

"Good. Put in the log that I made it and that I withdrew it in the same hour and that the reason I withdrew it is that the woman from the lock made a better argument than I did." He looked around the table. "And put in the log that I am still right about the brass man, because in about three weeks somebody is going to sell that man a set of our tools at a price that makes us all rich and stupid."

---

Owen put his coat on at four o'clock, and nobody stopped him.

It had started at two, as a joke, and it had stopped being a joke somewhere in the third hour, and Adrian understood later that the trouble was not that Owen was wrong. It was that Owen was the only one of the six who had spent nineteen hours doing a job and had not found anything in the job worth doing.

"He had a shape," Owen said. He had the tape measure out again and was not measuring anything with it. "Coin, seal, a roundel, whatever. It stood on its edge behind that join, and it was the same on Tuesday as it was on Wednesday, and I am telling you that a thing which does not move for two days while the whole river moves around it is not a reflection and is not a trick of the light, because I have stood in a factory for twenty years and I know exactly what a reflection does."

"Owen," Priya said.

"I'm not finished. About the size of a dinner plate. And it is dimmer than it was on Tuesday." He put the tape measure away. "I don't know what that means. Maybe it is further off than it was. And if it is further off, then it is going, and then the only question in this room is whether one man and a rope can be at that wall before it is not there. That is not a philosophy. That is a shift pattern."

"Who would go?" Adrian said.

Owen looked around the room, and got as far as Priya, and stopped, and Adrian watched a man run out of floor in a room with four people in it.

"That is what I am asking," Owen said.

And that was the moment it turned, and Adrian could feel it turn, and it turned because Owen had said the words *one man and a rope* out loud in a room where six people had been pulled out of one place without asking and had spent nineteen hours learning precisely what that felt like.

"He is out," Dante said, to nobody in particular. "There he goes. He is out."

"He is a man with a cut on his head and a light none of us can see, and he has been saying so for three hours," Mina said.

"Then I am out."

He went down the stair. Priya said "don't," and Owen said, "Then don't," and went down the stair, and on the half-landing he said something to the Hearthguard woman sitting on the top step, and Tamsin, who was in the yard and who heard all of it, said afterwards that it had not been an insult. He had asked her where the second bay was, and it had come out sounding like an instruction, and that was the whole difficulty.

Adrian did not go down after him straight away, which was the correct thing to do and which cost him more than the shutter had.

He waited two minutes. Then he went down.

Owen was standing in the middle of the market yard with his coat on, in front of a market that had already been told to go home, and three feet away from him was Sesh Brack with a flat expression and a stub of chalk in her hand and no intention whatsoever of moving.

"Mister Park," she said, in her own language, and then again in English, and it was the first time she had used his name, which meant that somebody in the loft had said it out loud in a market. "There is a Hearthguard on the river road. He is nineteen and he has a stick, and if you walk out of this yard going east he will not ask you a question. He will write down that you went east, in a book, with the hour on it."

"Then I will go west."

"West is the lock." She did not raise her voice. "There are eleven men in the second bay this minute who have been holding a promise for two days for no money. If a man out of a wall walks onto the walk-round at four o'clock, they will not decide whether to let you past. They will decide whether you are one of us."

Owen stood in a market yard in a world he had not chosen and discovered that he had run out of directions, which Adrian suspected had not happened to him since he was nineteen.

"How long," Owen said.

"A what?"

"How long have you been able to see it. Since the wall."

"Since the wall," Sesh said. "I did not think it was real. I thought it was the water." She turned the chalk over in her fingers and did not look at him. "Now I have watched three people come out of that wall, and two of them are asleep upstairs, and the third has been down my aisle this afternoon selling a man's property on a Tuesday with a piece of brake cable. Now I think it is real. And I think it is not yours. And I am not the person who gets to say that, so you will ask the woman with the chain, when she comes, in a room with a clerk in it, and whatever she says goes in the book whether you like it or not."

They went back up. The stairs took a while.

Adrian stood in the middle of the room with the notebook out and shut, and he was aware as he put it away that five other people were listening to him, and that what he was about to do was the thing he had spent nine years getting paid to do, which was to take a thing people badly wanted and turn it into a shape they could be responsible for.

"You did not get me stopped," Owen said.

"No."

"Good. Do not do it again." Adrian put the notebook back in his coat. "And I am not going to tell you it is not there. I cannot see it, and I have no standing whatever to tell a man what is behind a wall in a city I arrived in on Tuesday. That is the true answer and it is the only one I have got."

"Then get them to go and look."

"I am not going to ask anybody in this room to go and look at a light behind a lock wall, in a flood, on the strength of a description, with nothing rated for it and no witness. That is not a retrieval. It is a burial that has not been arranged yet."

"Then what is it?"

"It is a question," Adrian said. "And it is not our question. It is a question the people who own that wall get asked, out loud, in a room, in writing, and they get to say no in front of witnesses and it goes in the book either way. And if they say yes, then it is a working, with a crew and a rota and a cost posted, and it happens at low water with a name against it, the same as everything else in this city does, and you will be on the rope, because you are the only person who can tell them which stone to stand on."

"And if they say no?"

"Then it goes in the book that they were asked and said no, and in a year you will be the only person alive who remembers that there was a light, and I will testify to that." He put his hands in his pockets, and one of them was the wrong one, and he left it there. "Which is not nothing. It is what we have instead."

Owen looked at the floorboards for a while.

"Right," he said. "Then it is a question. I can work with a question." He rubbed the top of his head, and then took his hand away quickly, the way a man does when he has just found out that a thing is still hurting. "Put my name on it. My name on the question and not on the crew, and put down that I offered."

"It goes in," Priya said.

"Good." Owen sat down on the end of the cot and was suddenly, extremely tired, in the manner of a man who has been arguing for three hours and has not had a drink of water. "Somebody tell me what time dinner is."

The four rules went up on the wall of the loft that evening, on the back of a flour merchant's invoice, in Adrian's terrible handwriting, and the list of names and fears went up next to them, because Noor Haddad made it go up next to them before he could suggest anything else.

The three at the top of the invoice were not Adrian's. He copied them out again, slowly, in his own bad hand, exactly as Tamsin had said them in the yard on Tuesday, under somebody else's very clear eyes, while she watched to see whether he got a word wrong:

*1. You will not touch a working. Not to help, not to hold, not to steady it, not even if it is falling apart and you are standing in it.*

*2. You will not use a name here. Not mine, not the keeper's, not the name on the board. If you have to make somebody understand you, you point.*

*3. You will ask before you take a thing. Every thing. Including water, including a rope, including a door out of a fire. Including a piece of somebody's day.*

And under the three, in the same hand, the fourth, which was his, and which he wrote last, and which he made sure was the smallest writing on the wall:

*4. Tell Mina about every injury. Every one. Including the ones that look survivable.*

Noor had been sick twice and had slept for an hour and a half and had done, in that time, the thing that turned her into the person who was going to run the ledger of this place for the next several years: she had taken Adrian's notebook, and the scrap of paper with the six names and six fears, and the market's common-box record, and she had built a page.

It was not a list. It was a page of ruled columns, and Adrian recognised it with a small cold shock of homesickness, because it was exactly the sheet he had used in a hundred kitchens in another life: what we have, what it is for, who it belongs to, who may use it, who is owed.

"Who owns this?" she had asked, of the spanner money. "Not who bought it. Who *owns* it, in the sense of who has a claim on it. Because Sesh put it in the box, so the box has a claim. Not us."

"You built that in two hours."

"I bake. Everything is a shift and everything is a rota and everything is short one person." She put the page down. "You are going to want to be the one who holds the book, Adrian. I can see it on you. Don't. The book is worse than the wall. The wall is at least honest about being a wall."

He gave her his remaining cash that night, in front of everybody, counted onto the sheet — a hundred and sixty-one dollars and change, and a small plastic card he told them he had never once used — and he wrote the phone over too, the one with no signal and no clock, and put it in the box of things on the sill. He made himself do it as a public act, in the middle of the room, with his own two hands, while four of them watched and one of them, Owen, watched with an expression Adrian could not read and thought about for a long time afterwards.

And then he did the stupid, wonderful, unforgivable thing, and he did it *on purpose*, and he told himself afterward that he had done it on purpose.

"Fourth rule," he said, to the room. "Everybody. Including me, including the people who came through the wall this morning, including the locals if they can read it. *Tell Mina about every injury. Every one. Including the ones that look survivable.*"

"Adrian," Tamsin said.

"Not a request."

"You have been in this world nineteen hours."

"I have watched a woman in this city put a cut hand on a *shutter* because she did not want to lose a Sunday, and I have a rule on a wall that says *be careful*, and it did not work. So I am putting a floor under it. You can add rules. You can argue with this one. You cannot work around it, because I will hold the book, and the book will have your name next to your injury, and there is no version of the week where that is optional." He looked around at five exhausted frightened people. "That's the first time I've ever made a rule for anybody instead of asking them what they want. I want you all to know that I noticed myself doing it."

Mina Okoye, who had been writing SURVIVABLE in capitals all afternoon, said, "Fine. And in exchange, I want one thing."

"Name it."

"Every time one of us says a number about how bad something is — a pain score, a probability, an *odds* — I write it in the book as what we said, and never as what it was." She did not smile. "Your friend Jonah Pike said *she'll be fine* in this room six hours ago and it was not fine, it was a hand with a swelling that took eleven days. I want a line between what we said and what was true, or in a year we will all remember this room the way we wanted it to go."

Jonah said, "That's fair," in a voice that cost him something.

And Tamsin Quill, who had been standing in the corner with her arms folded for the entire conversation, said: "You do not get to make that rule for the locals."

"I know."

"You do not know. You made it anyway."

"I made it anyway."

She looked at him for a while. Then she said, "All right. But understand what you have done. You have made a thing that people obey because you are the one who made it. That is a small door. In about six months, when somebody is frightened of you, they will do what you said because you said it, and they will not tell you they are frightened, and you will have built a working you are not a witness to." She put her hand on the door frame. "So do it again in front of people, and write down that you did it in front of people. And do not do it alone."

---

He had to go down to the yard at eleven, because Tamsin's crew were holding the maintenance oath in the second bay and he wanted to see whether he could still feel it.

He could. Barely. He got about forty feet down the stair and then the line came up through the stone under his boots, thin and cold and real, pointing at the third bay, and the headache started, and the taste of copper, and he stood on the step with his hand on the rail and let it go on for as long as it went on, which was not long, and then the men in the second bay finished their round and the line went out.

He stood there in the dark, on a step, in a world he had not chosen, with one eye streaming and the taste of iron at the back of his throat, holding absolutely nothing.

He could not open it. He could not close it. He could not see where it came from or where it went, and when the people holding the promise let go, he was a man standing on a stair with a headache, and that was all, and tomorrow they would be gone and he would be the same.

It took him eleven minutes to get up the stair, and he sat down on the top step and stayed there for a while, and then he got up and went to bed because there was a morning coming, and it came.

---

It was the crier who woke him, and the crier was the first official act of Adrian's life in Veyra.

She came up the stair at six in the morning in a grey coat with the Lantern Concord's stitched sigil on the shoulder, and she did not shout, and she read aloud from a sheet of pressed paper in the local language, and then she said it in English, in a flat clerk's voice, having been given to understand that this was required.

"To Adrian Vale, who came out of the wall, and to the others who came out of the wall with him, and to the keeper of this room, and to the Hearthguard of the outer ward, and to any other person in this city who maintains a gate: the Lantern Concord gives notice.

The gate of the Lock of Salt stands open and damaged. It is watched and it is not closed, and it will be watched until it is either mended or surrendered, and no person is to be blamed in the record for its being watched.

The next tide is before light tomorrow. If the gate's damage has not moved by that tide, it will move on its own, and the water will go where the water goes, and eleven villages will find out about it before we do.

The Concord has therefore set a question, and the question is not whether the man from the wall is clever.

The question is this: can the Lock of Salt be worked by more than one person, such that no one person can open it, hold it, close it, or refuse it alone?

A working that can be held by a single hand is a working that a single hand can sell. This has happened in this city before and everyone alive has heard of it.

The Concord will hear an answer at first light, the day after tomorrow's tide. Bring witnesses. Bring people who will sign their names under what they saw. Bring nothing else."

She folded the paper. She looked at him for a moment, and she said, in English, with a small, dry, entirely professional courtesy, "There is a stool and a bowl of something in the yard. I am told you will want it. The hearing is not a trial. You are not on trial. That is not a kindness. It is a hearing, and you will be asked questions, and the answers will be written down, and the writing down is the part that lasts."

And she went down the stair.

Nobody spoke for a while. Then Priya said, into her phone, "That's the best thing anybody's said to us since we got here, and it was a threat," and Dante Kuo said, "Can somebody translate the part about the eleven villages."

"She didn't say it kindly," Tamsin said, from the door. "She said it as a number. That is how this city says *people are about to be harmed and it will be somebody else's hurry*."

Adrian sat on the edge of the cot with his feet on a cold floor and his notebook open on his knee, and wrote, at the top of a clean page, the thing that he knew was going to be the most important sentence of his life and which he had not yet earned the right to write:

*The answer is yes, and the cost of saying yes is that I will never be the one holding it.*

He looked at it for a long time.

Then he crossed out the first four words and wrote underneath them, in the same flat handwriting:

*I do not know yet. Ask the people who maintain the gate.*
