# Chapter 0027 — The Missing Child

Owen Park asked for four in the morning and got it, and Adrian Vale stood on a stake at the end of the third ditch of the bottom field from ten past four until about twenty to six on the twenty-seventh day of the flood, and in two hours he saw nothing at all, and the reason he has never told this story as a funny one is that he spent a hundred and ten minutes in the dark in the rain on the worst of four mornings looking at a man in pain.

Owen had come to the second stair at ten past three with the strap of a sling not on, because Saye had said four days and it had been one and a half, and because Saye had also said, in a porch, to five people, that the arm would not be able to tell him afterwards and neither would he.

He did not mention it. Adrian did not notice it until they were nine hundred feet down the field, at about twenty past four, when Owen Park handed him a flask and got the arm up with the other hand in a way that took him four seconds and cost him a controlled breath, and Adrian's own hand was on a stake in the mud.

"Saye said four days," Adrian said.

"She said four days."

"It has been one and a half."

"Yes," said Owen Park, and put his arm down, and they walked.

The field was a hundred and forty acres of the best soil Adrian had ever stood on in any world, drained, the winter grain drilled in it in lines to the horizon in both directions, and the ditches were level, and there was no light in any of them.

That is the whole of what happened at the third stake.

Owen Park stood on the stake for about an hour and forty minutes, facing north, with his good hand flat on the post, and at some point he said, "There," and Adrian said, "Where," and Owen said, "Under the far bank. About where the third ditch meets the reed. It is standing on its edge and it has got a mark on it like the top of a thumb." And Adrian looked at the far bank for about two minutes, at a flat mud slope in the dark in the rain, at a place where a reed was bending, and there was nothing there. There was nothing there at four, nothing at five, and nothing at twenty to six, and at twenty to six Owen Park said, "It has gone," in the voice of a man putting a tool down, and they walked back up nine hundred feet in the rain, and neither of them said anything about it for two days.

On the twenty-ninth day, in a hall, a woman of about sixty from the upper road asked Adrian a question that had nothing to do with anything, and he answered it, and his answer is the reason for the last line of the notebook entry: *there is a space on the form where three names go, and I did not write them because he had not told me, and the form says he may not, and on the morning of the twenty-seventh I was the fourth name on a form I had written myself and the other three did not exist, and what I did for a hundred and ten minutes was look at a man's arm.*

---

They came up the field at twenty to six and Sesh Brack was at the top of the second stair in the dark with a lamp, and she had come and found them, which she has never done to anybody in this city, and she had a shawl, and she put it round Owen Park's shoulders without a word, and then she said, in the local language, to Adrian, in the voice she uses when she is about to say something that is going to cost somebody money:

"There is a child gone."

---

**Sefa Rood** was eight years old and lived at Four Elms, four miles down the Reach road and then a mile and a half along a reed path that goes nowhere on any map in this country and is on two, and she had been missing since the second hour of the morning of the twenty-seventh, which is the second night after the eleven came out of the ground, and which is the night that the water went out of four villages at once.

The water going out of four villages at once was not a mystery and it was not the eleven, and it had been coming for two days.

Adrian worked it out at about eleven that morning, standing in a yard in Saltwake with a notebook, and it took him forty minutes and it is the one piece of engineering in this volume that he was actually able to do, and the reason is that he had been measuring the same three chalked marks in the pale edge of the join in the gate every night since the second night of the flood, and he knew exactly how much water a lock's own work takes out of a river, and for how long it takes to come back.

"A big working yesterday morning at the top of the tide," he said, "took out of this river about as much water as four hundred acres of ditching takes out. That water comes from the bottom of the channels, and the bottom of the channels in the Reach is the bottom of the *ditches*, and the water in a ditch is the only water in this wetland that anybody is allowed to make a promise on, and if the water is not in the ditch then the ditch does not know what it is supposed to do, and a household that cannot say when its ditch will hold cannot say when its gate can be opened, and a household in this Reach that cannot say when its gate can be opened does not keep its share of the water."

"Say that again in fewer words," said Tamsin.

"Four villages have lost the thing their promises are made of," Adrian said, "and it is because this city did a good job yesterday morning, and the good job is in a book with a number in it and the number is nine inches toward the near gate, and I wrote the number."

Nobody argued with him about the mechanism, because Renna Ord said "Mm," in the way she says it, and then said: "And a child went out in the dark last night into a wetland where the water is not where it was, and the reason you are all standing in a room instead of on the paths is that the reed shift is doubled and every man who knows those paths is on a gate, and you are going to ask me for eight men and I have not got eight and I have not got four, and if you ask me for four I will have to take them off the second gate, and then the second gate does not get set on the day it is set, and then Long Water does not get its water, and Long Water is where the men who are going to look for your child sleep."

---

**Marda Rood** was sixty-one and she had walked eleven miles in the night with a bundle, and she arrived in Saltwake at about the ninth hour of the morning on the twenty-seventh day and she was in a room with about nine people in it for four hours, and Adrian Vale has never in his life been in a worse room and has written down in three books that it was not his worst room.

She did not cry. She was past it, or she was a woman of sixty-one in a country where you do not do that in front of strangers. She sat down when somebody pulled out a bench, which she refused for about ten minutes and then accepted, and she said that her daughter was at Long Water and had gone up the reach on the second day of the flood and had not come down, which was entirely normal and had been entirely normal for sixteen days, and that Sefa had slept at her mother's on the second night, as she did twice a week, and that at the second hour in the morning the girl had got up, and put on her boots — *her own boots, not the good ones* — and gone out of the door, and had not gone towards the path, which is four hundred yards away and lit, and had gone the other way, towards the water.

"She goes after dogs," said Marda Rood. "There is a dog. There has been a dog on those paths since the flood and it has been getting into the low field at night and I have put a thing out for it twice and taken it in because I did not want her to have it in the house. That is on me and I will say it to a room." She put her hands on her knees. "And she goes out after it, and last night the water was not where it was, and she would not have known that, because she is eight, and because the paths she walks have been the same for three years and she has never once needed to know where the water was."

---

The argument happened in the loft above the market at about the third hour of the afternoon on the twenty-seventh day and it took five hours and it was the worst argument Adrian has ever been in, and three of the people in it argued better than he did, and it was resolved by six people who are not him, and the resolution was correct, and he was not part of it, and that is the entire content of the chapter.

It started with Tamsin, and it started with a refusal, and it was a refusal to Adrian and not to the room.

"No," she said. "I am not translating a working for this. You can have the yard and the books and the ledger and I will stand there and read the terms out in a clerk's voice all night. But I am not putting a woman who has walked eleven miles in front of a shape in a wall and saying *there may be a door*, because there is no door, and she has been in this room for four hours and nobody has said that to her, and a door is not a search party."

"The water pressure—"

"I know what the water pressure is doing. Wenna Cray told you about the level ditches in the second week and it has got worse every day since, and it is in four villages, and it is in the ledger. But that is the *effect*, and the effect has a cause nine feet from the man who took it, and if you open that shape and send a person through it you do not go and look for a girl on a reed path. You go to *the other side*. Which nobody has ever been. And so the first opening ever made in this country, the first and only and unrepeatable one, at the cost of a season of somebody's field, with a village that has refused twice standing in the balance, would be spent on the off chance that a child of eight has walked into a *hole*."

"You don't know she hasn't."

"I know she has not, and I have known since the eleventh hour this morning and I have not wanted to say it because I wanted the room to have one thing to do." She sat down. "Adrian. A door is not a search party. That is the whole of it and it took me a month to learn it and you have got twenty-seven days and you are about to spend the only one there is."

Mina was harder, because Mina agreed with Tamsin and had a worse reason.

"And suppose you are wrong," she said. "Suppose there is a four per cent chance she is behind a wall, and suppose you open the shape, and suppose the person who goes comes back in eleven minutes and says *no, she is not here* — what has the first opening of a route in this country cost, and who is holding it?"

"The witnesses hold it," Adrian said.

"The witnesses hold the *terms*." Mina put her pencil down. "A working is a pattern and a hand and a willing witness and a cost, and the cost always lands in a place. The cost of that opening is a season of a field and a day of four men's time, and it lands in Corrow, four miles away, in ditches, tonight, in a winter that Elsa Skell is putting grain into this week. And on top of that cost there is another cost, and it is *you*. Because the first opening in this country will be remembered as the one where a child was lost and somebody spent it anyway. Not that a door was tested. That a door was tested on the day a child was missing, and every person in the Reed Reach will know the date and none of them will know the reason, because the reason is four per cent."

Jonah said the thing that is ninety per cent wrong, and he said it well, and he was argued out of it by a nineteen-year-old.

"There is a person in this room who has been here two days and has not asked this city for anything, and a person who has been here twenty-seven days and cannot stop asking it for things, and the difference between them is about eleven minutes of a walk through a shape. I am the only person here who has spent a lifetime building the thing that connects those two facts, and the correct answer is to send somebody with nothing here. I am not going to be ashamed of it. I would like somebody to correct me if I am wrong."

Dante Kuo corrected him, and did it in nine words.

"You'd be sending them because they're cheap," he said. "That's what cheap means."

And then Renna Ord, who had not spoken for four hours, ended the argument, and Adrian wrote down what she said.

"You have all been arguing about the wrong person and the wrong day," Renna Ord said. "Here is the work. An opening is not a thing you do in a morning. An opening is a **fresh inspection** by four people in the wrong weather, and then a cost, and then a working, and then the standing of the four after it. The four are the four who signed, and three of them are in this room, and one is nineteen and asleep on a bench in a chapel upstairs. So: an inspection on the twenty-seventh in whatever weather we get, in the wrong hour, with a fifth person on the walk-round who is not on the rota because he is not on the rota. And that is the work. Everything else in this room is a *reason*, and reasons are cheap, and I have been paid in reasons for twenty-nine years."

And then Hesper Saye, who had not come up and had come up anyway, said the sentence that ended it, and she said it to Owen Park and not to the room:

"That arm is not to be on a stair in a flood. If anybody sends him I will put my name against it in front of that woman, and I will put it in the book, and I have put two men of nine hundred right in thirty-four years and I am not putting a third one wrong in front of a family because the man in the book is frightened of a number going up."

---

At about the ninth hour of the evening, with the inspection being done in the wrong weather on the walk-round by four people who did not want to be there, the pale field came up on the wall of the loft, in the air, in the colour of a paper form, and stood there for about fourteen seconds while six people watched it happen.

> **A RETURN MARK MAY BE SET IN ONE HOUR BY A MAN WHO HAS READ A SEAM.**
>
> **The man who reads the seam does not travel and does not change.**
>
> **Only one mark may be set at a time and only one person may hold it.**

Nobody in that room could see it except the six who had seen it four times.

Adrian wrote it in the notebook afterwards, in the flat hand, and did not cross anything out, and it is the shortest and the most frightening page he wrote that month:

*It came in a loft, in a room, at the ninth hour, on the day a child was missing.*

*It is nearly all true. It did not say the hour would be the hour of the tide, which is why an hour matters. It did not say the person whose mark it is chooses, which is the only part of a mark that makes a mark a moral object rather than a mechanism.*

*And the fourth line — the man who reads the seam does not travel and does not change. That is a cost sentence in the place a cost goes, and it is true, and I have written four of those in this notebook in a month and every one of them was about a different person and not one of them was about me.*

*And it came in a room in which the argument had just been that the man who reads the seam is the man who cannot be refused, and it offered me the job.*

*Refused. And the reason I am writing the reason down is that I have now refused six things and I have said the reason out loud four times, and the interface has no memory of a refusal, and I do not know what it is doing when it offers a thing I have already refused, and that is a worse thing to write down than anything it has ever said.*

---

**Eli Mercer** was in the room.

He had been in the room for four hours and had not spoken, which was not like him, and Priya Shah noticed it and said nothing about it, and at about the ninth hour he got up and put his hand up, the way you do in a school, and Tamsin said, in English, "He has not asked anything in four hours and I would like the rest of you to know that I noticed and that I have been waiting."

"Me too," said Eli Mercer. "Right. Two things.

"I asked to go eleven times today. I want that in the book with the hours on it, because I know how this looks and I am not going to let it look like anything. Eleven times, starting about two o'clock, to five different people, and I asked to go and I said *I'll come back, I'll tell you what's there, tell me where to go.* That is what I said. And every single person turned me down, and not one of them enjoyed it, and one of them — " he looked at Tamsin " — one of them said *ask, and tell me what it costs to say no,* and she was right and it is the reason I am standing up now.

"And the second thing. I've been in this room for four hours listening to six people argue about whether to spend the one door this world has ever had, and every single one of them is talking about it as if the only question is *who*, and it isn't. There is no *who* problem. Every one of you has something here. You've got a debt and a post and a lease and a woman by the wharf and a village that won't speak to you. Adrian's got a black mark in his hand. So there is nobody in this room for whom the answer is obviously yes, and everybody in this room knows it, and that is why you have been arguing for four hours instead of deciding.

"So the question isn't who. The question is whether a mark goes on somebody who **has nothing here**, and there's exactly one such person in this building, and I have known that since the walk-round yesterday, and I have been sitting on it for four hours because I didn't want to be the man who volunteered in a room full of people who couldn't."

"And your reason," said Adrian.

"My reason is that I said *lore* out loud on a stair in front of three hundred people yesterday, and about forty people in this city believed me, and there is a number on a board in the market that says eleven more every time the thing wants something, and I put that on. Not on my own. But I'm in it. And if I go through and I come back, then the number in the market has got a face on it, and the face is a nineteen-year-old who went and looked and came back and told the truth about what was there. And if the face is one of yours — " he put his thumb at the six, at Owen, at Adrian, at the room " — then the number is about *you*, and in nine months it is about a man in a good coat's letter."

Nobody said anything for about eleven seconds.

"That is a very good reason," said Tamsin Quill, in English, "and it is the first reason anybody has given in this room that was about the *market* and not about the *child*, and I am obliged to say that the reason about the market is the real one and that all four of us have spent five hours being careful and he has done it in nine seconds."

---

Owen Park spoke for the first time in four hours, and it was about a third of a minute long, and it is in the record because Roke asked for it to be entered.

"He is not wrong," said Owen Park, "and I want to say the part that is mine, and I want to say it before anybody thinks I am being generous.

"I asked to go at about four o'clock. Not to a room. To him, on a stair, on Friday. And he said no, and he was right, and I have been carrying it for two days, and I want to say the reason out loud because I have watched him do it four times in a month and it is the thing I have learned most about.

"I asked because a woman in a raft told me that the man she can send is never the man who wants to be sent, and I have spent every one of twenty-seven days making myself the man who wants to be sent. So if a man is sending anybody, it is going to be me, and the two things I have wanted in a month are a road and a person to send, and I did not know until Friday that those were the same thing.

"So I am not sending him because I am a good man, and I am not sending him because I would go if I could, and I am not going to go, because a woman with tweezers has told a room that my arm is not to be on a stair in a flood, and she has been right about four hundred men out of nine hundred, and I am not going to be the five hundred and first she was wrong about in front of a family.

"Which means the fact that I cannot go is true of me for the rest of my life, and I have known that two days, and I have said nothing because it is the only thing I have ever wanted that I have actually lost. The route is not lost. The route is empty. There is a difference, and I have been making it in my own head for a month, and it turns out I was not making it. I was hoping.

"And I want it entered," he said, "in these words: that if he does not come back then I will say so in a book, with my name on it, on the day, and that I will not spend the rest of my life saying *I don't know what happened to him.* I did that once, in a market, on the fourth day, and it cost six people a week."

---

The inspection was done on the twenty-seventh at about the fourth hour of the afternoon, in rain, on the walk-round, by four people who did not want to be there, and took two hours and eleven minutes, and the seam was viewed, sounded along, and compared by three masons against the entry made on the first week and the entry of the sixteenth day, and it was not narrower and not wider, and the lip was as it was entered, and the standing of the instrument was satisfied for the period.

And then a fourth thing happened on that walk-round, in the rain, that is not in any book, and is in this one, because Ovin Sarr was nineteen and could not keep it.

"I was the man on the wall," he said, afterwards, to the loft, with his hat in his hands. "Not the man on the rota. I was the man on the wall because I was not on the rota. And I stood two hundred steps of it with a line and a horn in the rain for two hours and eleven minutes and I did not blow the horn, and nobody was behind me, and there was a man on a roof at the top of the stair who I could see and who I knew was there, and I want to say that I could see him and did not need him and did not want him, and I have had six watches in three weeks and this was the seventh and it is the first one I have done where somebody was watching me do it, and that is the whole of what a thing is for, and I do not know how to say it in a room and I have been trying for an hour."

---

The child was found at about four o'clock on the afternoon of the twenty-seventh day, on foot, by four people, on a reed path that is on two maps and is not the one they were looking on.

**Osten Fenn** of Long Water took three reed cutters out at two o'clock because he had decided to, and nobody sent him, and he told his father, who told Wenna Cray, who was at the second gate. They found her at four o'clock, in a lee of a bank, sitting in nine inches of standing water with a dog, alive, cold, uninjured, three miles from the point where anybody expected her to be, on a path that had been under a foot of water in the morning and had gone down by four o'clock because *the water was not where it was*.

The water had gone because the tide in Saltwake was four hours from the top on the following morning and the river had to go somewhere.

Osten Fenn's whole account of it is eleven words long and Vail entered it in full: "She was where the water was not. That is all it is. She was where the water was not."

---

The first opening of the Narrow Mark was at the sixth hour of the morning of the twenty-eighth day of the flood, at the top of the tide.

The terms were read at the fifth hour at the foot of the walk-round, in the open, in the rain, to about a hundred and forty people, by Tamsin Quill in the local language and by Adrian in English, and Vask read the mason's part herself. There are six items. Adrian can still recite four of them.

One: the standing terms of the instrument are broken in public, tonight, because the instrument is being used before the Concord has ruled on whether it may exist, and the breaking is entered as a breaking and not as a thing that was always permitted.

Two: one named living person goes, and the name is given by the person and not chosen for him. **Eli Mercer, of no address in this city, of the second list, aged nineteen years, who has not asked this city for anything.**

Three: the cost is the standing labour in the yard doubled for the day, a season of the bottom field entered against the instrument, and the day of four men's time that yesterday's inspection came out of, entered again because it has now been taken twice.

Four: **Adrian Vale reads the line and marks it. He is not in the opening. He has no place in it and no vote in it, and if the join does anything he did not expect he is the man who says the number and not the man who decides.**

Five: if the person does not come back within the hour, the instrument is closed by the four witnesses and no second person is sent, ever, and the standing of the instrument is suspended by the Reed Reach, which has the standing to suspend it, and the suspension is not appealable by anybody including the holder of the pull.

Six, and this one was Vask's, and it is the only item in the document that is hers: *the ground under this chapel is not a place anybody may go, in either direction, and the list on the chapel door stands, and if anybody in this city in the next hundred years uses a thing in this wall to fetch anybody out of the ground under this chapel then the list on the chapel door is read in the yard first, in the open, before the bar goes in.*

The return mark was set at about the eleventh hour of the night before, in the low room by the salt wharf, by a working performed by Hesper Saye and Tamsin Quill and Hettis Roke and a mason from Saltwake, in about forty minutes, with a cost that was entered and that Adrian is not going to describe here because it was not his.

He read the line on the walk-round at the fifth hour of the morning of the twenty-eighth and marked it with a stub of chalk at three places, in the rain, and the four witnesses signed, and the gate crew said the maintenance oath at its ordinary volume, and Eli Mercer, nineteen years old, of no address in this city, walked down two hundred steps of a walk-round in the rain, and stopped at the bottom, and looked up at about a hundred and forty people, and said, in English, in a voice that did not crack:

"For the record, I am doing this because I asked eleven times and I got eleven noes, and because if I do not come back then the man in the good coat writes a letter and the letter says *an unrecognised instrument is admitting people who describe themselves as players*, and I am not having that letter written about a number I put on a board."

And he went into the seam, and it took him out of the world, and the whole of it took eleven seconds and there was no water in it and no light in it and no residue anybody could name.

---

Marda Rood was in the crowd. She had slept about three hours in a chair by the low room by the wharf and Hesper Saye had made her sleep, and she was on the second step of the walk-round at six in the morning because nobody had been able to tell her to stay anywhere.

Nobody in that crowd knew what to do with their hands, and Adrian Vale stood at the top of the stair with a notebook and a stub of chalk and a headache, and at eleven seconds past six in the morning on the twenty-eighth day of the flood, a man of nineteen came back out of eighteen inches of dead-straight seam in the wall of the outer channel of the Lock of Salt, and put both feet on a wet stair, and looked up.

He was back in eleven seconds. He had a piece of paper in his hand.

And the first thing anybody did — not Adrian, not Vail, not the four witnesses, not Renna Ord, and this is entered because it matters — was that Marda Rood, a woman of sixty-one from Four Elms who had walked eleven miles in the night, stepped forward through about thirty people and said, in the local language, in a voice that carried up two hundred steps:

"Did you find my granddaughter."

And Eli Mercer, nineteen, who had been in another world for eleven seconds and who did not speak a word of the local language, looked at the woman who had said it, and then at Adrian, and then said, in English, at volume, for everybody:

"No. Ma'am. I wasn't looking for her. I'm sorry. I'm sorry — I have to be honest with you, I wasn't looking for her, I went to look at a *wall*, and I'm sorry, and I'll tell you everything I saw, and I'm telling you first that I wasn't looking for her because you asked me a question and you have had four people lie to you in two days and I'm not going to be the fifth."

And the child was found at four o'clock the previous afternoon and had been in a house in the city since about six, asleep, with a bowl of something, and Marda Rood knew that, and everybody on that stair knew that, and nobody said it, because he had not been asked to be sent for her and it had been agreed in a room the evening before that he would not be.

---

Afterwards, in the yard, at about the seventh hour, with a hundred and forty people standing in the rain who had all watched a man walk into a wall and come out of it, and with a woman of sixty-one standing at the bottom of a stair who had not believed any of it and had gone to bed and would not be told a story about it tonight, and with a child asleep two streets away —

Hesper Saye came up the steps and took Adrian's arm and turned him round, away from the crowd, and said, in English, in his ear, over the noise, so that only he could hear it:

"You did not perform it. Say that out loud to somebody in the next hour, because you are going to want to have done it, and everybody else is going to want to have watched you do it, and in nine days there is going to be a market with a story in it about the day a man from a wall opened a door, and the story is going to have you standing four feet from the seam with your hand on it."

"It was four feet back."

"It was four feet back," said Hesper Saye. "Now say the other thing. The other thing is that a woman from Four Elms asked a question at the bottom of a stair and a boy answered it honestly, and that is the only part of this morning that is going to survive the winter. Say it out loud, and say it to the *notary*, because she will write it down, and because she has been writing down the true things all month while everybody else has been writing down the interesting ones."

And then a docker of about sixty in a felt hat came up the wet stair and asked Adrian Vale a question, in a yard, in the rain, in front of a hundred and forty people, and it is the only question anybody asked him about it that day, and it is the last line of the twenty-seventh and the twenty-eighth days of the flood, and it is in the Concord's book in Vail's hand with the hour on it, and Adrian has never in his life been able to write about it except in these terms:

"Adrian," said Nell Bray. "What was *it* for."

---

He wrote it up on the morning of the twenty-eighth at about the ninth hour, in a room above a market, in the flat hand, and it is four pages and the first line is the one he read out at a hearing three days later.

*The twenty-eighth day, at the sixth hour, at the top of the tide, on the walk-round, in the rain.*

*Nothing gained. An opening was made and it carried one person and it was not a crowd and it was not a breach and it was not a door, and I did not perform it, and I read a line and marked it in three places with a stub of chalk and a woman by the wharf made me say that out loud afterwards so that it would be in a book before it was a story.*

*And a child was found on foot at four o'clock on the twenty-seventh day by four people from Long Water on a reed path that is on two maps, and she was where the water was not, and that is the whole of it, and it had nothing to do with a wall.*

*And a boy of nineteen went into a seam in a wall because he had nothing here and had said so out loud in a room, and he came back out of it in eleven seconds with a piece of paper, and he told a woman of sixty-one standing at the bottom of a stair that he had not been looking for her, and apologised, and that is the only act in this month that I would put in a book four hundred years from now.*

*And the route is not empty any more. That is what I have not written down yet because I have not sat with it. A mark exists in this world on a living person, and it belongs to a boy of nineteen who came through without asking and has refused to be attached to us, and the route carries one person per opening and needs a fresh inspection before it is used again, and a receiving community may suspend it, and Nine Elms has refused twice and stands in the balance every single time it is used.*

*I gave up the only other one in the first week in front of nine witnesses. I could have taken it back. Nobody could have stopped me and nobody could have proved it.*

*And on the afternoon of the twenty-seventh, in a room with nine people in it, with a woman of sixty-one from Four Elms sitting on a bench at the end, I said the second half of it out loud for the first time. I said: I do not know whether the mark I gave into that wall in the first week could have brought anybody home. It is in a stone in another channel and there is no procedure in nine hundred years of this city's record for taking a thing back out of a wall, and nobody will ever be able to tell me, and if the answer is yes then the only way anybody will ever find out is to have the thing back, and the only way to have the thing back is to take the thing I gave up, and I gave it up on purpose, in a room, in front of nine people, and I would do it again, and I have known since the fourth morning of this flood that I would.*

*Which means the answer to a question I have been asked every day since the third week went out of reach this afternoon, in a room, with a grandmother in it. And the woman who asked it was not a person who wanted a hopeful answer. She was a person who wanted to know whether a door she had been told about for a fortnight could have got to her granddaughter, and the answer was that nobody in this world knows and the man who might have known threw it away in the rain on the fourth morning and told nobody for three weeks.*

*Ask the people who maintain the gate. Ask the keeper. Ask the woman who keeps a count.*

*And Nell Bray asked me in a yard what it was for, and I did not have an answer at the time. I have one now, and it is the true one, and it is the worst answer I have ever had to give anybody: it was for finding out whether a person could go. Not for the child. Not for a road. For that. Once. And it cost a season of a field in a village where a woman has not spoken to me for a fortnight, and it is done, and it cannot be got back, and I would do it again, and I have not slept, and I am going to have to say all of that in a hall on Wednesday, in front of a notary, in about nineteen seconds, and I do not know how.*
