# Chapter 0039 — Three Truths

The longest entry in the history of the book of the Lantern Concord was drafted on the morning of the thirty-ninth day of the flood by a woman of twenty-four from another world who used a clause she had not written against a rule she had invented in a room above a market, and was entered by a notary of this city who is fifty and who is not allowed to be asked whether she agrees with it, and the reason it is the longest is that nobody in that hall could agree on a single sentence about how a man of thirty-eight days in this world died, and the two people in the room who could have settled it with one word each were a mason who was not there and a man who was in a room by a salt wharf the whole time with his hands in a bowl.

---

The hall was full at the first light with about three hundred and ninety in it, and about three hundred and fifty of them were still in it at the seventh hour, and about four hundred and forty were in the yard outside by then. There is a number entered for the yard and two numbers entered for the hall, and the two are nine minutes apart, and the reason is entered: about forty people had come for the tide and gone again.

Three accounts were in the city before anybody had opened a book on the morning of the thirty-ninth, and by the fifth hour all three of them were being said in the hall by people with a reason, and the reason is entered with each one, because a reason is the only thing about an account that can be checked.

**That it was the breach.** That there is a hole under the floor of a yard in this city, that it came open at four in the morning of the thirty-eighth and took a man into a space about four feet deep and gave him back, and that the hole is the fault and the man is a warning.

It was argued by **Aldis Kerrow**, assessor of strangers for the Lantern Concord, in a very good coat, in about nine sentences, and nobody in that hall thought he was a liar.

"I have sat beside the notary at that table since the fourth day of this flood and I have not spoken at length in this room once. I am going to say the useful thing and say it plainly.

"A document carried to Aurel in a wooden case four times a year is a document with sentences in it, and I have spent thirty-eight days watching this city find out what happens when a room is given a paragraph where a clerk wants a sentence. **A hole in a yard in Saltwake opened and a man of this city was carried into it and came back and died.** That is one sentence. It has a place, an hour, a witness and a result, and every part of it can be checked by somebody who was not there.

"And if instead the entry is four pages long and says *we do not know, and there were four people in the room, and here is what each of them thought* — " he put one hand flat on the table, " — then a clerk in Aurel reads four pages, finds that the city cannot say what happened to one of its own residents, and opens a file, and the file is what the second letter of the thirty-first day already is. **I am not defending the breach. I am defending a sentence. My whole trade is that strangers can be described in a way that a stranger's government can act on.**"

**That it was the door.** That there is a door, in a floor, that a man went through it, and that it is a door.

It was argued by **Perry Lask**, forty-one, of the second list, who came out of a chapel floor on the twenty-fifth day of this flood and said *this is a closed beta* in a yard and asked for it to be entered that she said it first, and who said four sentences on the morning of the thirty-ninth in front of about three hundred and fifty people, and asked for all four to be entered verbatim.

"I have been fourteen days in this world and I have been in a market four times and in a market this city is wrong about one thing and one thing only, and I have been letting it be wrong because I could not think of the sentence.

"There is a door. That is all. A man went through a door and did not come back and everybody in this building is being extremely careful about the word, and I have been careful about it for fourteen days, and I would like to stop.

"There is a door. There is a room on the other side of it. There is a light in the room. There is a person in the room, and on Thursday morning a man walked into it on purpose, and he came back, and he died. **A door is a thing with a far side.** That is not a game and it is not a trick and it is not a metaphor, and I have been a person who describes things for a living for twenty years and that is a true sentence about a thing I have seen with my own eyes, and about a man who went through it and came back and said *it is not a relic, it is the size of your hand.*

"And I am not going to be careful. Being careful about what I say about a door has cost that man his grave and it has cost this city nine days, and I would like somebody to come and tell me I am wrong and I would like to be told in a hall and not in a kitchen."

Nobody in that hall could tell her she was wrong, and about forty people in the room believed her, and neither Roke nor Vail corrected her, and the fact that neither corrected her is in the book, and it is the entry Adrian would take out if he could take out one.

**That it was the lock.** That the ordinary maintenance of the Lock of Salt is the mechanism, that forty men of this city's own crew say four words twice a day under a gate nine feet from the join in that gate, that it has been happening for thirty-eight days, and that a man died of it.

It was argued by **Marek Osley**, salt factor of the tally-house, forty-eight years old, with a waxed tablet he has not put down since dawn and which he turned over and put face down on the table at about the fourth hour, which is the first time a person in this city has seen him do that.

"I am the man who has to answer for it in March," he said, "and I have been the man who has to answer for it since the third week of this flood, and I am going to tell you the thing I have been carrying alone for four days and I am not going to be popular for it and I have never been popular.

"The man in the book has been saying since the nineteenth day that the lock is the mechanism. I have been arguing with him about it for four days because I do not have a number and he does. And on Thursday morning the water in this city went out of the ditches of four villages again — I have it, I had a man count it, I did not ask him and he has been told he was doing something wrong — and I had that in my tablet at six in the morning and I have had it since, and I put it face down on this table this morning because it is a number of the same kind as the number about a man, and a man does not go in a column.

"**And he is right.** The words under the near gate are the toll and the man in the join found the pattern and did not make it, and I have known that since the third week and I have said it in a market four times and I have entered every one of those four in a book and not one of them has been acted on, and on Thursday morning a man went into a floor in this city and died and the forty men who say the words are still standing under a gate and will be there tomorrow.

"So: it was the lock. That is the true mechanism and it is the only one of the three in this room that has a *pattern* in it, and a pattern is a thing a mason can point at, and I would like this city to be angry at forty men who are doing their ordinary work and are going to keep doing it, because **a city that is angry at forty men has a chance of stopping them, and a city that is angry at a hole has got to go on living next to a hole.**"

---

They argued it for about two hours and forty minutes and it is all in the book, and out of the two hours and forty minutes four things came out of that room that had not been in it at the first light.

The first was a woman of forty-one of Silt Row, who is on page one of that book against the name of the man in the seat, and who stood up at about the fourth hour of the morning and did not make a speech.

"There is a number in this room everybody is doing and nobody has said out loud," said Wenna Cray. "On the twenty-fifth day of this flood there were about three hundred and ten people in the yard under the lock chapel, and eleven people came out of a floor in it, and one of the eleven was a child of eleven. And on the morning of the twenty-eighth I put my daughter on a cart to Silt Row, because there is no room in that yard for a woman of forty-one and a child of nine in a flood month and I was not going to stand in it with her and I have thought about that every day since.

"So she was four miles away. And I have been reading a market all week that says a man went into a hole in that yard to fetch somebody. **If my daughter had been in that yard on the twenty-fifth, I would be standing in this hall telling you that I saw a man go into a hole in this city to fetch a child out of a floor, and every one of you would believe me, and about two hundred of you would be standing up, and it would not be true, and I would not be able to prove one word of it, and I would be believed, because a mother is believed.**

"That is what the number means. It is not a number about a man. It is a number about how quickly this city will believe a good reason. And I want it read into this entry, and I want it read before anybody makes a sentence, and I have been on page one against his name for thirty-eight days and this is the first thing I have ever asked for in this room and I am asking for it for him and not for me."

---

The second was the shortest thing anybody said in that hall, and it was said by a docker of fifty-one in a boat-coat who was standing at the side wall with a barrow behind her because she had come in from a night shift, and it is four words and it is not in the book until about the sixth hour because nobody could find a notary to take it.

Renna Ord said: "He was sent."

And about nine people in that hall said, out loud, the same time, four different things, and one of them was a woman of forty-one who has been in three markets about it.

"He was not sent," said the man in the book.

"He was sent," said Renna Ord, from the wall. "You are the holder of the pull. There is a post in a yard. The ground does not begin without him. He asked in front of two hundred and forty people and you did not say one word, and I have entered that at three in the morning on the Thursday and I entered it as a fact and I was in the room, and I am not going to be asked to soften it, and the difference between *he was sent* and *a hole took a man* is about four hours and about one word, and the one word was yours, and I have been saying since the fourth day of this flood that a man who has asked for nothing in a room is a man you can send, and I said it about him and I was right about him and it turns out I was also right about you and neither of you was the worse of it."

---

The third was a boy of nineteen, and it is the reason the entry is the longest one in the book, and it took about nine minutes and it is on the third column of the roll of the thirty-ninth and it is signed by six of the eleven and the sixth of the six was a woman of twenty-four who had not been asked to be there and came anyway.

**Eli Mercer**, nineteen, of no address, of the second list, who was hurt on the morning of the thirty-eighth day and who is entered on the thirty-ninth:

> **A. I was hurt. My right hand. It was on a stone at about twenty to four in the morning on the thirty-eighth, and I was not in the terms, and I knew I was not in the terms when I put my hand on it, and I put it on the other side of the scar from the man who was in the terms because a thing is held at two ends. My hand is not a scar. About a third of the mark went. Saye has looked at it twice. I can make a fist. That is what I have.**
>
> **B. I am not joining the six. I said in a hall on the twenty-sixth of this flood that I was not joining them and I said why, and I have not withdrawn it and nothing that happened on Thursday changes it and I would like it read that nothing changes it.**
>
> **C. I am not speaking for anybody. Not for the eleven, and not for the six, and I have now been asked in three markets this week to say whether it was a door and I have declined all three and I am declining in this room, and the reason I am declining in this room is that I have been in one, for eleven seconds, on the twenty-eighth, and there were eleven of us and there was nobody else in the room, and I have said that in a book twice and nobody has ever asked me whether I am sure, and I am sure, and I would like it in a book a third time in my own hand.**
>
> **D. And the refusal is entered and is not held against me, and I would like that in a book, and I would like it in a book in a form that a person can use, because there are going to be a great many more of us and there are going to be people in this city who will want to use the fact that I was on that floor, and I am asking for a standing now, at twenty-four hours' notice, from a room that has never given one to anybody who was not in a bad trouble, and I am not in a bad trouble. I have a hand. It is a third of a mark. That is my whole trouble and I can make a fist.**
>
> **— and I would like a notary to ask me whether I understand what I am refusing to speak for, because I do not, and I have never known, and I would like it entered that a man of nineteen does not know what he is refusing to speak for and refused it anyway.**

Sera Vail read it out. It took a minute and a half. She asked him the question, in the room, in front of about three hundred and fifty people, and he answered it, and it is in the book, and it is nine words long: *I do not know, and I would rather be nobody's mouth.*

---

And the fourth thing that came out of that room was the entry, and it is the reason the entry is the longest in the history of the book, and it took about an hour and ten minutes, and it was fought over at every sentence by a person who wanted the sentence shorter, and the person who won was a woman of twenty-four who had stopped a word in public in a hall on the twenty-sixth of this flood and who had said in a room on the twenty-ninth that a document which is internally consistent and can only be checked by one person is not a record but a story with a seal on it.

"I am not going to let anybody write *he died because*," said Priya Shah, at about the sixth hour of the morning of the thirty-ninth day of the flood, to about three hundred and fifty people, in English, and then in the local language, badly, with Tamsin Quill correcting her four times.

"Here is why, and it is not moral and it is not a feeling and I am not being brave.

"**A record is not a finding. It obliges somebody to answer it and it obliges nobody to believe it, and it is not a finding and it cannot be used as one.** That is the standing clause of a form for a statement of what a person said they saw, and I did not write that clause. A woman with a bowl wrote half of it and I wrote the rest and Adrian wrote the rest of that and neither of us wrote it as a rule about a death. It is a rule about a claim that cannot be looked at again by another person, and the whole of the form is that it stands there and nobody has to believe it.

"Write *he died because a door was opened* and that is a finding. Write *he died because the words under the near gate are a toll* and that is a finding. Write *he died because a man in the book did not say a word* and that is a finding, and that one is the one I want to write more than anybody in this room wants it written, and I want it not written.

"So we write the facts. And the facts are: the hour, and the list that was read, and the two men on the floor and the one who was not in the terms, and the number of inches the scalp had gone into the bone since the second week, and the shoulder that went on the twenty-fifth and was never set, and the form on a wall with an empty chain of witnesses on it for thirteen days, and the post in a yard, and the one word, and the man, and the hand.

"And it will be four pages. And it will be the longest entry in this book, and in four hundred years somebody will point at the page and say *there* and they will be able to say it because there is nothing in it that anybody would have to take on trust. **And every single person in this room who wants a shorter entry wants a shorter entry because they want to be able to point at a reason.** And a reason is a finding. And findings are what the twenty-fifth day of this flood was, and eleven people came out of a floor because a good working's residue arrived at the oldest damaged thing in this river at a time nobody chose, and nobody chose that, and everybody has been living with a reason for it ever since, and there is no reason."

---

The entry was read out at about the seventh hour and it took twenty-two minutes and it is in the book and the three things that are in it that are not in any other entry in the history of this book are these, and Adrian Vale has asked for all three of them to be read back to him in a year by a person who does not like him, which makes four things he has asked for, and the first three are in the book and the fourth is in a notebook and nowhere else:

> **And: a hold is a working without a witness, and the residue of one goes into the person who did it.**
>
> **That sentence was spoken in a doorway in this city's yard on the twenty-fifth day of this flood, by a woman from another world to a man from another world, about a child of eleven on a standing flagstone, and it has been used in this city since by everybody who has wanted a true sentence to stand on, and it is entered here on the thirty-ninth day of this flood because it is the truest sentence anybody in this city produced in a flood month and it did not stop one thing, and a true sentence that does not stop a thing is a description and not a reason, and this book has just refused to make it a reason, and that refusal is the entry.**
>
> **And: the man in the book is the reason and it cannot be proved to be.**
>
> **That is the ninth clause of the same form, and the ninth clause was written by him on the twenty-fourth day of this flood about his own hand, and it is entered here unchanged and in his own words, and the entry is made by a notary and not by him, and the reason is that a statement of what a person said they saw may be entered by any person and is obliged to be answered, and the holder of the pull is barred from entering one against anything on his own seat, and so this entry is being made by Sera Vail, of this city, in a room, and he did not write it and he is not allowed to.**
>
> **And: he asked me to write the true one.**
>
> **On the thirty-eighth day of this flood, at about the quarter past two hour of the afternoon, in a room by the salt wharf, a man on a table asked the holder of the pull to write down the true reason for a refusal given in a room on the twelfth day of this flood, and not the fair one, and to write it where a former one had been written, on a keeper's board in a yard, in a keeper's hand, with a mason named under it. He said: *I read it every morning for six days and I did not know it was mine.* This is entered in his words, at his request, and the request is entered with it, and the true reason is entered with it, in the local language, in the hand of a mason of Saltwake who read it out, and it is in a book and it is not on the board, and the difference between those two places is entered and is not resolved.**

There is no name in that entry that is not a name of a person of this city or of a person who came out of a floor, and there is no word in it out of another world except the word *scar* and the words *hold* and *unclosed* and the four words of a maintenance oath that Owen Park learned in the rain on the second morning of this flood and said every morning for the better part of a month, and they are in the entry because he asked for them to be, and the asking is entered.

The second group was named and housed separately on the afternoon of the thirty-ninth day, and it took about an hour and a half, and the hour and a half was almost entirely an argument about a room.

They had been in the disused Salt Weavers' chapel two floors above the market for fourteen days. It has four benches, a floor, a window behind plaster that nobody has opened in six years, and a key held by a market woman, and no arrangement of any kind beyond that, and there was a condition attached to the key — *take the plaster off the north wall at the end of it* — and the plaster was still on, because eleven people had been in a city for fourteen days where eleven more had come out of a floor under them and nobody had a spare day.

"They are to be housed separately from the first," said Vail, in the hall, "and separately means a room, and it means a page, and it means witnesses of their own choosing, and it means that the two lists in this book do not share a line, and it means that a person on one of them may not speak for a person on the other, and every one of those is a thing this city has had to invent in a month and the first of them was invented by a boy of nineteen on the twenty-sixth and it is the only instrument anybody has got."

"And where," said Adrian.

"That is the whole of the afternoon and I would like you to let the room do it and not you, because you are the person in this city who cannot be allowed to decide where eleven frightened people sleep, and every person in this hall knows that and one of them has said it out loud and it was not me."

It was done in about an hour and a half and the outcome is this: the **upper floor of the rope store above the chandlery on the upper road**, three rooms and a drying loft, dry, above the high-water line, held for a season on the word of the woman who keeps the chandlery, with a list of what is owed for it on the wall and a key and nothing else.

The list on the wall is: *three rooms. One stove. A key to the front and a key to the back. Water is carried up in the morning by a boy of fourteen of this city and is not asked for. Nothing else is promised. Nothing else is owed. Nobody is to be put in these rooms who is not on the second list.*

And the condition attached to the Salt Weavers' chapel key — *take the plaster off the north wall at the end of it* — **lapsed**, and it is entered as lapsed, and it is entered as lapsed with the reason, and the reason is in Vail's hand: *because a city that conditions a dry room for eleven people on fourteen days of work is a city that counts, and this city has been counting wrong for a month, and I am not going to be the notary who entered a charge on eleven people.*

The surname on the register is entered as it was given. There is a column for it and the column is narrow, because a column in a book is narrow, and about a hundred and sixty people in this city keep registers.

> **Odile Ferrand-Buchanan, thirty-eight, of the second list. Entered in full, in the form given, with the hyphen and with the second part of it, and not shortened, and not entered as *Ferrand*, and the width of the column is entered as a fact and the entry is made across two lines of the page in a hand that is smaller than the rest of it, which is the correct instrument for the problem and which I would like somebody in four hundred years to find.**
>
> **And it is entered that the person gave the name in that form and was asked twice whether the second part of it was wanted and said yes both times, and that the clerk who took it down was seventeen and has said in this room that she did not know she was shortening anything, and I have entered that, and she has asked that it be entered, and it is entered.**

And the last thing that happened on the thirty-ninth day of the flood was at about the eleventh hour at night, in a room above a market, and there was no notary in it and it is in no book, and it is in a notebook, and it is the reason a man with a seat in a salt store in this city did not sleep on the night of the thirty-ninth, and it is the reason about four people in that room are the only people on this side of the world who know it.

There is a wall in the room above the market with thirty-one names on it in four columns. It is not a roll. It is not in anybody's book. It is a group rota written by Priya Shah in the second week of this flood and it has been added to nine times since, and the fourth column is the one that matters and the fourth column is **what the work is for, not what the person is for**, which is Sera Vail's ruling from the eleventh day and which has been on that wall since the eleventh day and which is the single most important sentence in a room that also has Adrian's form on it.

On the thirty-ninth day of the flood, at about the eleventh hour at night, the first column of that wall was changed.

It was not crossed out. Nobody in that room crossed it out and there has never once been a moment in this city when anybody has struck a man's name off a wall, and the whole of what the Concord learned about strikes in five weeks is that you do not do it, you put a line under it and you write the date.

There is a line under the name in the first column, and the date is the thirty-ninth, and the date is in Priya Shah's hand and the line is in Priya Shah's hand, and the fourth column under that name is empty.

And it is going to be empty for a long time.

"We are not going to put anything in it," said Priya Shah, to a room. "That is the decision and I have made it and I am not going to be argued out of it, and if anybody in this room thinks I am being sentimental they should say so now and I will listen to it and I will still do it.

"He is dead. There is no work for that. And in about four days one of us is going to be tired, and in about nine days somebody is going to want to be able to read that wall, and if the fourth column has a word in that row then the wall becomes a wall that says what thirty people in this city are *for*, and it is the only wall in this city that anybody from another world has ever been on, and Vail's ruling is the only reason it is a wall and not a list, and I am not going to let it turn into a list because a woman of twenty-nine has died and I am tired.

"And I want the fourth column to sit there empty for a while," she said, "because that is what the empty space is *for*. The whole of what Adrian's form does is leave a space for three names and then leave it empty. The whole of what the statement of error does is leave a page in a book that somebody has to come back to. The whole of what the keeper's binding does is have no name at the top. **And this is the first time in five weeks that anything in this city has a hole in it on purpose, and I want to see whether the city can stand a hole in a wall for eleven days without putting a word in it.**"

Nobody in that room had an answer and the argument went on until about the second hour of the morning, and the answer turned out to be that nobody could, and the four people who stayed in that room agreed to say nothing about it to anybody for eleven days, and eleven days is a long time and the reason it was eleven is that a woman of twenty-nine said *if you are going to do it then do it long enough that it is a decision and not a feeling, and eleven is the number I would choose and there is no reason for it and I have looked.*

---

He was in that room and he did not say much and the reason he did not say much is that he had been asked a question about a wall and had answered a question about a wall eleven days earlier and had got a different answer, and the person who had asked the second question was sitting eight feet away with a slate.

At about the twelfth hour he got up and went to the shelf where the boxes are and took out the notebook, and did not open it at the table.

He did it at the second hour of the morning, on the second step outside the back door, in the cold, with a cup of something hot that Sesh Brack put in his hands without saying anything, which is the fourth time she has done that in thirty-eight days and which she has never once mentioned.

There are two pages of that notebook that are not in it any more, or rather there is one page that is not in it, and he has not opened the notebook in front of anybody since the twenty-ninth.

Page forty-one is in a leather folder in a room in this city with a woman who keeps a book, and Sera Vail has not mentioned it since the twenty-ninth, and Adrian has not asked.

And there is the last page, and it has his sister's name on it, and he wrote it on the eleventh day of this flood, in a room above a market, at about the second hour of the morning, with three missed calls and one unanswered text on a telephone that has had no bars since the first morning.

> **Nora Vale. Twenty-five. Camden, in the flat above a locksmith's.**
>
> **She runs the rota. Tuesdays and Thursdays and Saturdays, six to nine, the Camberwell Larder, and there are about eleven people on it and four of them are the only reason it works. If I am not there on a Tuesday by six she sends somebody to the workroom at half past seven with a flask. She has done that three times since I stopped answering and the third time she did not leave a note because she said a note is a thing you read when you have decided you are not coming.**
>
> **If I am not there by the Saturday, somebody is to be sent, and I have written down who, and it is not her.**
>
> **And if I am not there by the Saturday after that, she rings the police, and I know that because she told me in November and I have known it since November and I have not thought about it once since the first morning of this flood.**

He had not turned to that page since the eleventh. It is twenty-eight days. He sat on a step in the cold and read it and read the sentence about the police, and the sentence about the flask and the third time, and about a Saturday rota in Camden at six o'clock, and he thought about how he had had a notebook in his hand every day for thirty-nine days and had not turned to one page out of ninety-one, and how the page had four lines of somebody's ordinary life on it and a man's handwriting that was a printer's handwriting and that he could not now remember the shape of, and how he had written it down on the eleventh day of a flood because he was frightened, and how a person gets frightened about a page and then forgets to be frightened.

It is a list of a different kind. That is the whole of it. Every other page in that notebook is a list of things he has to do, and a debt, and a cost column, and a list of forty-one names reduced to nineteen lines a city can act on. The last page is a list of one woman's Tuesday, and a number of people she would send, and a flask in a cloth.

He read it twice, and then he wrote on the back of it, in the flat hand, the date, and the true hour, which is the second hour of the morning of the fortieth day of the flood, and then the four words he has written at the bottom of every page in this notebook for five weeks, and then a fifth thing, which is new, and which is in his own hand and which he is not going to put in a book:

> *A list of one person's Tuesday, and I did not turn to it for twenty-eight days, and I have been in a city for thirty-nine days where a book that goes to Aurel in a wooden case four times a year has my name in it in my own words with two dates on it, and I did not turn to the page that says what happens to a woman in Camden if I do not turn up, and I am not going to pretend that I did not choose the page, because I chose it, every day, thirty-nine times, and I chose the weather in the Reed Reach and the state of a gate and a form on a wall.*
>
> *Ask the people who maintain the gate. Ask the keeper. Ask the woman who keeps a count. And on Saturday morning ask the woman who keeps a count, and then ask somebody else, because a woman with a barrow is not the only person in this city who keeps a count, and there is one other, and her name is on a rota on the first column of a wall and I struck a line under it about four hours ago.*
