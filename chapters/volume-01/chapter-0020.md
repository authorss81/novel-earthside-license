# Chapter 0020 — The Road Down

The dig began at seven in the morning at the bottom of the tide, and it began with a man saying four words badly in a yard, and the second thing that happened was that it did not work, and Adrian decided afterwards that this was the correct order.

Owen Park had said them every morning for eight days, in the rain, with Vask correcting him, and on this morning Vask did not correct him, because Vask was down the ladder with a lantern and two masons and a bar, and Owen was standing in the middle of a wet yard in front of an arch with a board on the door behind him that had a list on one side and five words on the other, and he said them.

Nothing happened.

There was no cold, no wire, no line under anybody's boots, and the arch stood there being four hundred years old and being a doorway, and Owen Park said the fifth word, and waited, and nothing at all happened, and the rain came down on the back of his neck.

"Again," said Nyla Ferrow.

"I said them."

"Say them again."

Owen said them again, at about a third more slowly, and this time he put his hand flat on the arch the way he had watched her do it four hundred times, and at the end of it he said the fifth word, and Adrian — who was standing at the trestle with a notebook and had a headache already — felt nothing.

And then, about two seconds after the end of it, he felt something, and it was not a join and it was not a line. It was the specific, unmistakable sensation of a room agreeing to be a room, which is not a thing you can describe to anybody and is exactly the thing Adrian had spent nine years designing a screen to make people feel about a menu.

"That was the second one," Vask said, from the bottom of the ladder, without any drama at all. "The first one is always the one you throw away. Do it again at noon and write the hour on the board, because in three weeks somebody will come down here and tell me it has always worked at six, and I want the board to disagree with him."

---

The terms were read out at the north door before anybody put a tool down, in the local language first, by Vask, and in English after, by Adrian, badly, with Tamsin standing behind him to correct him every time he got a preposition wrong, which was six times in eleven minutes and which she counted.

The cut is in the floor at the hairline in the flagstones, four feet beyond the threshold slab at the foot of the arch, and not in the threshold slab, which every person in this city steps over and which nobody touches. That line opened in this room on the first morning of the flood and carried water uphill, and it has been dry and still ever since.

The dig stops the moment the void is found. The bar goes in no further than Vask's own judgement, and Vask's judgement is not appealable by anybody including the holder of the pull.

It is not a rescue. Roke wrote that on his slate on the first day and Ovin Sarr had copied it out, and Roke read it out himself in the yard, in the local language, and then in English, and then said, "That one is mine. I want it said in the hall that that one is mine, because I have been shouting in a yard for eighteen days and everybody has been very polite about it."

It is not a door.

And the list, which was on the chapel door in Vask's square mason's hand, four inches high, where anybody who came up into that yard would have to read it before they could get at anything else: *what may not come out of this hole. 1. The stone in its place. 2. Water, in any vessel, for any reason. 3. Any writing. 4. Anything that answers.*

Dante Kuo, who had come down on the dig crew because a dig is carrying, read item two aloud to a small crowd of about thirty people who had come to watch, and then read item four, and said, "Is that a joke?"

"It is not a joke," said Vask. "It means do not take a voice. It does not mean there is one, and I have read this chapel's register from the year it was built and it is not in it once. It is on the list because I put it on the list, and I put it on the list because I have been in a hole once in this city with three good men and a bar, and we came up with four hours of something in our heads that belonged to somebody else, and it was not a voice, and it was worse than a voice." She shouldered a barrow. "Do not ask me about it. I have four feet to do."

---

It went wrong in small ways about a hundred times before it went wrong in one large way, which is what everybody who has ever actually done work has told Adrian and which he had not believed.

The first cut was in the wrong place. It had to be moved eighteen inches north, and the eighteen inches had to be done twice, because the first eighteen inches was done off a string line that one of the masons had pinched with his thumb.

The lantern flooded twice, and the third time it flooded because somebody had put a stone on it to stop it swinging, and the stone was one of the flags that were stacked in a row like teeth in a skull and the mason who had put it there was Renna Ord, and she got the story of it out of him over about nine minutes while the two of them carried water.

A boy of about eleven came down the stair behind his father at nine in the morning without being asked and put his boot through a flag that everyone including the boy had understood to be a flag, and the flag broke into four pieces, and the boy went white, and Vask took the four pieces out of his hands one at a time and looked at him and said, in the local language, "You will pay for that. Not with money. Go and tell your mother and she will tell me and I will tell her what it is worth, and it is not a whipping and it is not a shilling, and it is not going to be a thing you carry for a year. Go on." And the boy went, and came back at half past four with his mother, and the two of them cleared stone for four hours, and Adrian watched it and understood that he had been taught something about cost that no book had ever managed.

The water table came up higher than the bar had said it would, and it came up at a quarter to eleven, and Vask's whole crew stopped within about forty seconds of each other without a word being said, the way a thing stops when everybody in a room has been in one before.

And at about eleven, Adrian offered to go down, and Vask said no, and he asked why, and she gave him the answer in a yard in front of thirty people because she had decided in advance that she would give it in public.

"You cannot read it," she said. "I have watched you not read it twice this week. When the masons were holding the arch in the chapel on the second night you could follow a line; when I was under a flagstone last Saturday you found the edge of the void in about eleven seconds and could not see one inch past the top of it. There is nothing under this floor to read, Englishman. There is a *room* under this floor, and a room is not a join, and a room does not run anywhere, and the thing you are good at is following things that go somewhere."

"I can tell you where the edge of the cut is."

"You can tell me where the edge of the cut is, and I do not need you, because I have got a string and two masons and a thing called a plumb-line, and it has been in this trade since before your grandmother." She shouldered the barrow again. "Stand where the keeper puts you. That is the whole of your job today and it is not a small one."

And Nyla Ferrow, from the chapel doorway, in English, which she had learned badly from Owen Park and which she now used roughly once a week and always at a moment of consequence:

"You will stand where I put you and I will not tell you why. If I tell you why you will work it out, and then it will not be a working, it will be a man with an idea. And I have had a man with an idea in my yard before and it was my mother's mother, and she was the best keeper this room ever had, and she was not the keeper, she was an idea, and the room is still here and she is not."

So Adrian stood where Nyla Ferrow put him, which was four feet back from the lip of the cut with a sight-line down the ladder and nothing else, and he held the notebook, and he wrote down the times, and he was, for five and a half hours, the least useful person in a yard in Saltwake.

He had a rule about that by then, which was: *do not be useful in a place where the usefulness would displace somebody. There are about forty people in this yard whose living is being a body at the bottom of a hole, and the moment I am useful at the bottom of a hole, one of them is not.* He wrote that down too. He did not cross it out.

---

They found it at four feet, at ten past one, and it took eleven minutes and Nell Bray found it with her fingers, which nobody had thought to do.

She was on her back in the void with a lantern hooked to her wrist, feeling the wall the way a docker feels a lashing, and she said, "There is a face."

"Stone," said Vask.

"Cut stone," said Bray. "Down here. At four feet. That is a floor that has been *made*."

They cleared it for forty minutes with three buckets and a great deal of swearing from Renna Ord, and at eleven minutes past two the bottom of the cut was clear over about a nine-foot square, and Ottoline Vask got down on her knees in it and looked at what was under them, and did not say anything for a while.

Adrian was four feet back. He could see the faces of two men and the top of a lantern and a strip of worked stone the colour of wet slate, and he could not see one inch of it, and the man who had told him not to come down was right and had been right in the yard with thirty people watching, and the whole of his usefulness in this world was standing four feet back with a sight-line down a ladder.

"It is dry," said Vask.

"It is *in* a wet place," said Renna Ord. "Everything down here is wet. It is under a chapel on a bank. Everything under here has been wet since before the flood wall."

"It is dry," said Vask.

The yard went quiet in a way that thirty people know how to do.

"I have dug in this city for thirty-four years," she said, to nobody, from the bottom of a hole, in the flat voice of a woman stating a fact. "Thirty-four years. I have lifted stones out of banks on both sides of that lock. And I have never once, in thirty-four years, found a stone under a building that had not been wet since the year it was laid. And I am not going to guess in front of a hole, and I am not going to guess in front of thirty people, and I am not going to guess in front of *you*." She put her hand flat on it. "It is dry. And it has been dry a very long time."

"It was dry when I was here," Adrian said.

Vask got up as far as her knees and turned her head and looked up at him.

"Say that again."

"You said it in your first week. You said: a thing that has been somewhere it did not belong for a very long time has been holding a shape instead of holding water, and a thing that holds a shape does not take a drop. I said it. In the chapel, with nine people in the room, about the stone in the yard." He was aware that his voice had gone into the flat register and that there were about thirty people listening to it from the rim of a hole. "I have been thinking about that sentence every day since. I did not know what to do with it. You do now."

"Yes," said Vask. "I do now. And I am going to be careful with it, and so are you, because I have got four feet of it and a bank, and a man with a quarter of a thing who says the whole of it is a man selling." She wiped her hands on her apron. "Look at the face of it. Look at it properly, all of you, because every person in this yard is going to hear about this room for the rest of their lives and I want them to see it and not have it described to them."

---

It was worked.

That was the second thing and it was the thing that stopped the dig, and it took a mason with thirty-four years in Saltwake to see it, and it took an hour and forty minutes of clearing and a lamp held sideways before Vask was prepared to say it out loud.

The stone was a face, not a floor. It was dressed, close-grained, grey, and it had been cut with tools in a pattern that four Veyrans in that yard recognised immediately and that Adrian did not recognise at all until Renna Ord explained it to him, patiently, twice, with a stick, on a piece of paper, while the tide turned.

"It is a wall," Vask said. "Not a floor. A wall, laid on edge, and the face is the face of a wall, and it is *dressed* — see the tool lines, see the way the chisel went down and stopped and went down again, that is a mason laying a face and not a river laying a crack. And it is not a *crack* in it. It is a *joint*. A putlog hole, three of them, in a line, the height a man would want to put his feet on, and there is mortar in all three and the mortar is older than anything in this chapel."

"And the joint," said Renna Ord. "Come and look at the joint."

The joint ran the length of the exposed face, about nine feet, and it was dead straight, and it sat directly under the chapel's own hairline seam in the flagstones four feet above — the hand's-width line that had opened on the first morning of the flood and carried water uphill, and had been dry and still and unmoved ever since. They were the same line. The crack in the floor was the top of a joint nine feet long, and somebody had filled the crack over it with lime and clay and a new flagstone, and had done it well enough that nobody in this room had known for three weeks that there was anything under the floor at all.

The same join. Seen from underneath, from the north end, four feet down, in a wall that ran on under the bank.

"Somebody opened it," said Nell Bray.

"Somebody opened it," Vask agreed, "and looked, and closed it. Two hundred years ago, or near it. And they closed it *well*, England, that is a better joint than anything either of us has made and it is holding a chapel on top of it." She sat back on her heels. "That is not damage. There is no damage down here. There is a thing that was built, and a thing that was shut, and a wall that goes on under the bank."

"How far under the bank?"

"To find out I would have to dig under the bank," said Vask.

"That is the answer to that question," said Renna Ord, from the rim, in a tone Adrian had learned to hear: she was about to say something that everybody was going to be annoyed by and she did not care.

"Go on."

"That is not four feet," she said. "That is the flood season. That is a cofferdam, and a pump that has to be manned day and night, and three more men than I have, and it is the *bank*, and the bank is the river's own, and you do not put a hole in a river's bank in the root month." She looked round. "And I will tell you the other thing. Everybody in this yard wants the next cut to be one foot. I have watched thirty people look at that joint and go quiet, and I know what thirty people want. One foot under this cut, south-west, on the angle of the wall. Because that is where it goes under the bank, and everybody in Saltwake has been thinking about a bank since the morning the six came through."

"Then nobody will have it," said Vask. "The bar goes no further today. Item two."

---

The dig stopped at twenty past three and the ground was boarded by half past four, and Adrian accepted a duty at ten to five that he has never been able to describe as anything other than the most expensive free thing anybody has ever handed him.

It came from Nyla Ferrow, standing in the archway with the board, in front of a yard of about forty people, in English, at a volume that made a mason put down a barrow.

"The ground under this room is open. It is boarded now and it will be boarded until the flood season, and when the flood season ends it will be opened again, and the flagstones come up, and that is not the end of it, and I am not going to promise anybody it is.

"And here is the price I set last Saturday, and it is in the Concord's book in Vail's hand, and I would like it read out, and Quill will read it out." Tamsin read it out. "The binding is done in the yard, twice a day, in the open, in any weather, by whoever is nearest, for as long as the ground is open."

"That is done," said Vask. "The boy does it at six. It has been eight days."

"Eight days," said Nyla Ferrow. "It will be a season. It will be longer than a season if that wall goes on where it is going. And here is the other half of the price and I am naming it now, in a yard, and I am not naming a man, I am naming a *position*.

"The man with a mark in the wall is the reason this yard has a hole in it. That is not an accusation; it is a *fact about the ground*. A mark goes in a stone, and a mark in a stone is a thing you can stand near, and the archwright who would not tell him where to put his feet in this room on Saturday night is the reason a man is standing four feet back from a hole with a notebook instead of being useful at the bottom of it. And a man whose mark is in the stone of this building is not a visitor to it. And a visitor can leave." She looked at Adrian. "So: for as long as the ground is open, the ground does not open without him. Every beginning. He is here or it does not begin. Not because I want him here and not because he is useful. Because if he goes out of this yard for one day and something happens under this floor, then the next thing anybody says about this floor is that a stranger came out of a wall, put his hand in the stone, and left."

"That is a hostage," said Adrian.

"It is a *post," said Nyla Ferrow. "You would know the difference if you had ever held one. A hostage is taken. A post is stood, and there is a difference in it that you cannot see from inside it, and I have been telling you since the week you came out of that wall that the only thing standing between this room and a room that can be done to is *attendance*, and attendance is a thing you do in a room, in the open, twice a day, and I have been doing it since I was nineteen and my mother did it before me and you may go and look at the register, because it is in there, every morning, for four hundred years, and it is the only line in that book that has never once been in doubt."

He said yes in about a second and a half, and he did it in the local language, which he cannot do properly, and Vask corrected him on the verb and let the rest of it stand.

"And I want the whole of it in the book," Adrian said. "The cost, and the field, and the yard, and this. Not *he is in attendance*. Everything. In a form that a man in Aurel could read, if a man in Aurel ever asks, and I want it in the form where it looks like what it is, which is a season of one man's attention paid to a room he is not able to leave, given by him, in a yard, in front of people, and the reason it is not a fine is that there is no end of it in sight and no sum of money that finishes it."

"It does not finish it," said Nyla Ferrow. "It stops when the ground is made. That is all the ending there is."

---

He had not looked for the pale field once that day, and he noticed that he had not, and he wrote down the date on which he had stopped, because he was the sort of man who would want it later.

He had stopped on the Friday night, in a room above a market, in the middle of a sentence Jonah Pike was using the word *quest* for a thing in a wall. He had understood in the middle of it that he was the only one in the room who knew what the word was supposed to cost, and that the word had got out of his own mouth in a market on the Wednesday without his hearing it, because the market was forty feet from where he was standing. He had written it out that night, in the flat hand, at the bottom of a page, and had crossed out the two words above it and left this:

*I am not going to use that word again. I have been handed a job with a deadline and no reward and a cost that falls on somebody else, and the word in the language I brought with me for that is the word I am refusing to say, because I have watched, in this yard, this month, what it does to rooms. A man in a good coat has been saying it at me since the night in the chapel, with a reward in it. I would rather be given the job and never be asked.*

---

At eight o'clock on the Saturday evening Sera Vail came up into the yard with a slate under her arm, and told them the date, and told them what the date was.

"The Concord has set a hearing on the question it opened on the morning after the second tide. Whether a first license may exist in Saltwake. It is to be heard."

"When," said Adrian, though he already knew, because a woman does not come up into a yard with a slate for anything else.

"The next tide is in nine days. The hearing is three days after it." Vail put the slate on the trestle, squared it, and did not sit down. "So you have until then to arrange, and the last day of the salt month is before that, and your field review is in it, and I am telling you both dates in one yard because I have been a notary for twenty-six years and I have watched what happens to a man who is given one date at a time. He plans the first one. He is always planning the first one. Nobody in this room is planning the second one, because nobody has been told the second one, and when they are told the second one in a fortnight with a day to go, it is too late to arrange anything at all, and that is how it has always worked in this city, and it is always a mistake, and it is always made by a man who is pleasant about it."

She looked at the boarded ground, and at the chapel door with its two lists, and at Owen Park, who had a cold and had said the four words in the rain at six that morning, and at a man standing four feet back from a hole with a notebook he had not been allowed to use.

"Nine days," she said, "and then three. Twelve. And in those twelve days this ground is not going to open again, and he is not going to be able to leave the yard, and the four witnesses have a shift, and there is a woman in Corrow who has to decide on the last day of the salt month how much of her life to put into the ground, and the man from Aurel is still in this city, and the Crown's letter is in a case on a river boat, and the reach has refused, and the gate is three inches wider than it was a fortnight ago, and there is still no mark on any living person in this world that would let anybody go home."

She picked the slate up.

"That is the true count," said Sera Vail. "I have kept a count for twenty-six years and I am a person who enters things, not a person who makes them, and I want it understood that I have never once in my life entered a true count that anybody could use, because there has never been anything true to count. There is one now. I would like somebody to be frightened by it at the same time I am."

---

She had gone, and the yard had emptied, and the tide had turned hours ago, and the rain had stopped, and Adrian sat down on the trestle in the archway, four feet back from a boarded hole, with a notebook, and opened it to a clean page, and wrote at the top of it, in the flat hand:

*The nineteenth morning. Nothing gained.*

*I can still see a line. I cannot read anything under that floor and I was told so in a yard in front of thirty people and the man who told me was right, and I have got a *post* now, which is a season of one man's attention in a place he cannot leave, given by me, in the open, for no money, with no end of it in sight, and I could not buy it and I could not refuse it and I would not have wanted to.*

*I am the holder of the pull of an instrument that has no recognition and cannot be used and has never been used, and I am owed a season of a hundred and forty acres that has been growing about forty times a day since the third day, and I am going to be asked about it in public in six days by a woman who has kept a count for twenty-six years and who came up into a yard tonight and told us two dates at once because she is a better administrator than I will be in a year.*

*The gate is three inches wider than it was when I arrived. The route is empty. I have no road, no return, and no way home, and there is no mark in this world on any living person that would take one home, and that is still the single most important fact in this yard, and there are forty people in it who would trade the whole of what we have argued about for it.*

And then, lower down, in smaller writing, the honest version, which he did not cross out and never did:

*And it may be me. I wrote that on a door in chalk on Friday night and I am writing it here again because the difference between writing it for other people and writing it for myself is the whole of what I have left to learn in this world, and I have not learned it, and I have twelve days.*

*Ask the people who maintain the gate. Ask the keeper. Ask the woman who keeps a count.*

*And do not, under any circumstances, fill in the field.*
