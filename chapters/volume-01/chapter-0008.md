# Chapter 0008 — The Price of Translation

Two hours after the lantern went out, the lock was lower than it had been all day, and the first thing Owen Park said about that was: "Somebody's opened the floodgates."

Nobody answered him, because the answer was that the lock's problem had never once in two days been somebody opening a gate.

They had the chapel. They had one lamp on a pole, two, actually, because Owen had insisted on two, on the grounds — and he was not entirely wrong, and that was the maddening part — that a single light in a room with a scar in the floor was an invitation with a shape to it.

There were seven of them. Adrian, Tamsin, Vask, Roke, Mina, Owen, and Priya, who had come because she said she would record it and then had spent forty minutes discovering that a phone with no bars, no clock and a failing battery could not be a witness to anything, and had said so out loud, twice, in the voice of somebody arriving at a professional conclusion.

Tamsin had not wanted the chapel opened. She had said so, in the yard, for twenty minutes, in a low argument with Roke and Vask and then with Adrian, and she had lost it on a point of law rather than a point of fear.

"You cannot seal it," she had said. "You understand? A sealed room is a room that is not on the public roll. In this city, a thing that stops being on the public roll is not a thing that is forgotten. It is a thing that can be *done to*. If you put a door on this chapel tonight, in six months there will be a door on this chapel and no record of why, and in nine months somebody will come with a paper and it will be lawful."

"So we sit up all night in it," Roke said.

"We sit up all night in it," she said, "and it stays on the roll, and anybody at any hour of the night can come and see the line and ask what it is, and that is the *whole* of what keeps a thing from being done to. Attendance."

So they had the chapel, with the door propped, and the rain coming under it, and a lamp, and a hand's length of black line in the pale threshold slab at the foot of the arch.

---

Owen wanted to put his hand in it.

He wanted to put his hand in it so badly that Adrian could see him not doing it, which was worse. He stood with his boots on the flagstones six inches from the line and he looked down at it with an expression Adrian had seen before on the faces of very good engineers.

"It is not a door," Adrian said.

"It is *aligned*. Look at it. Look at the shape of it. That is not a crack, cracks are not that—" Owen crouched, and his hand came out, fast, palm down, and Adrian got a hand on his shoulder and held it, and Owen let himself be held, which was the thing that told Adrian how frightened he was.

"I know you can see it," Adrian said. "I have watched you not see it. There is a bright edge on the outside of the wall that anybody standing on that walk-round in daylight can point at, and it belongs to the stone, Owen, it is new stone against old stone and the sun gets into the gap, and I know that is not what you are looking at, and I know you are not mad, and I know you can see the other thing because I watched you cry about it in a loft full of strangers."

"Do you," Owen said. "You don't believe I see it."

"I believe you see something. I have got a mark on my hand that you cannot see and that I cannot put in a report, and if you told me you had a light behind a wall I would not sleep for a week." Adrian crouched down beside him, keeping his weight off the line. "And I know exactly what I want it to be, and I am telling you what I want it to be. I want it to be a route. I have wanted a route since I got here, and every single time I have wanted something this badly I have found out later that wanting it was the reason I got it wrong."

The room was quiet.

"Then what do you want it to be," Owen said.

"I want it to be the *bright edge*. Because the bright edge is a thing I can put in a record, and a record is the only kind of magic this place has that I have seen work."

---

They tried it the way Tamsin said you had to try it, which was slowly.

The chapel floor had three useful features and no others: the arch, with the old promise in it; the patch of lime and clay, which Nyla had renewed that afternoon and which was a *daily* tie and not the arch; and the line.

Adrian had a page of paper in his coat and on it he had copied out the offer, all five lines, in his own handwriting, from memory, which is a thing he was good at and which turned out to be the only useful thing he had brought with him.

Tamsin read it out twice in the local language, slowly, for Vask and Roke, and once more for the two dockers who had come with Roke and were standing in the doorway, and Adrian watched the two dockers' faces and knew from the moment the third line was read that they had never in their lives been told about the stone.

"A stone is out of its place," Vask said. "Under the chapel. That is not news. That is a Tuesday."

"It's news that it says *before the next tide*," Adrian said. "That's the part that's interesting and that's the part that isn't in your Tuesday. Somebody has put a deadline on it, and a deadline means somebody has calculated how long the thing can be out of place before the water makes the decision instead of us."

"You have calculated it," said Vask. "How?"

"I've been standing in a lock for two days comparing where the water goes with where it ought to go." He turned the paper round. "So here's the second half. It tells me where the stone is. It does not tell me where the stone *ought* to be, and it does not tell me that there is a second thing underneath it, and it does not tell me whose river it is. My friend here says a promise nobody takes has nowhere to land. I tested that this evening and got a mark in your floor for free. So my question is: what happens if somebody *does* take it?"

Tamsin said, "Ask Vask."

"I am asking Vask."

"I heard you ask Vask." She turned round and spoke in the local language, at some length, with the air of a woman who has been asked to be a doorway again. Then she said, "She says: it is the oldest form. The old oath binds the gate and the man who is standing in the gate. The gate cannot be the only witness, because a gate does not answer, so the man answers for it, and the man is bound, and the water goes where the man said it goes. That is the form she was taught at nine. It was stopped because it is a form that requires a person to stand in a lock in the dark, in flood, on a rotation, forever, and this city is not poor enough to keep doing that."

"And the man in the gate — where is he?"

"He is in the gate. That is the whole of it. There is a man in the gate tonight and he is saying the words and nobody knows which man it is."

"Then that is the missing witness," Adrian said. "That is what the offer left out. It didn't leave out a river. It left out a *person*. Somebody has to be standing in that join saying the old words for this offer to be landable, and there is no such person, and the offer did not tell me that, and I would have gone down there."

Then Vask sent for her two masons, because Adrian asked her to do something, which she agreed to at the price of being told why.

He wanted the line in the slab measured against the pressure in the lock.

"You can feel the pressure," she said, in the local language, and then again, in English, flatly, as though she did not believe it and had decided to stop arguing about it. "Can you feel it *now*, or only when my people are holding something?"

"Only when they're holding something," Adrian said. "That's the honest answer and I would rather say it than be impressive."

"Then hold something," Vask said, "and tell me which line it is."

Her two masons went and stood at the foot of the arch on the old lime and clay and said the daily words over the patch, and the wire came up through the flagstones, thin and cold, and Adrian put his hand flat on the chapel stone and followed it as far as it would go.

It ran the length of the room, out through the wall, and stopped.

"Right there," he said. "Where the yard wall turns. It is thinner on that side than the arch, and it is not going anywhere after that, it just — ends. And the whole weight of the room is sitting on the arch, so when I tell you the arch has to be held tonight, I mean the arch has to be held while somebody stands at that corner, and if the corner goes first there is nothing behind it."

Vask looked at the corner for a while. Then she said, in English, without any ceremony at all: "That is a fact about my city. I have lived here thirty-four years and I did not know that, and I have just found it out from a man who cannot write my alphabet." She looked at him. "The offer is on that wall. Do you understand now what the offer is? It is a permission that would let somebody move through the corner without the arch. It is a door round the side of a load-bearing wall. And it is offered in a voice that says *no road back* as though that were the risk."

Then she sent her masons back and the wire went out from under his hand, and he stood there with his hand on an empty slab and nothing under it, and it had been the only reason he was any use at all for ninety seconds.

"I have got four hours of this and no more," he said. "So write it down that I can do four hours of this, and write down that I have no idea whether four hours of it is any good."

---

Vask was looking at the line in the slab. She had her arms folded and she had not said anything for a while.

"It did not tell you," she said, "because it cannot. A thing that has lost its site does not know what a site needs. It knows what a *name* needs." She looked at him. "That is the distinction, Englishman, and you may write it on the wall next to your four rules, because you are the first man from the other side who has come close to it. Everything that wants to happen through this wall has to be said by somebody. You can kill the message. You cannot kill the person. If there is a man in that gate, then there is a man in that gate who chose to be there, or who was put there, and neither of those is a thing a line in a stone can tell you."

---

Then Mina did the thing she had come to do, and she did it badly at first, and then well.

She wanted to look at the people who had held the lock.

"There are six men who stood under that gate yesterday and five more in the bays and on the wall," she said, "and two of the ones who held the near join have been coughing since this morning. And I need to know whether that is dust or whether it is something that happened to them, and I cannot tell, because I have been a nurse for six years and I have never in my life seen a body that has been *used as a tool*."

Roke said something short. Tamsin did not translate it. "He says they are not sick."

"They are coughing."

"They were coughing before the door. It is a stone room under a river in flood. Half this city is coughing." Tamsin said it fairly, and then, more quietly: "He is also frightened, and he does not want a healer counting his crew, because a crew that is counted is a crew that is stood down, and then the lock is not maintained, and then the flood is his fault and not the wall's."

And Adrian did not have an answer, and wrote in the notebook that he did not have an answer, and underlined it, because the next time this came up he was going to want to pretend he had.

"Nobody is stood down," Mina said. "But somebody is going to sit down with each of them for ten minutes and ask them what happened yesterday, in order, with times, and if there is a gap I am going to find out about the gap. That is the job."

"Who is going to do that?"

Mina looked at the doorway. "You," she said. "You said you were a systems designer. That's what a system is — it's what happened, in order, with times, and where it stops making sense." She turned to Tamsin. "And you. Because I do not speak it and he does not speak it and if I ask him he will tell me what he needs to hear."

So it took two hours, and it was the best work Adrian did in Veyra.

He sat on the floor of the maintenance yard with Roke's slate on his knees and he did it interview by interview, and Tamsin sat with her back against the wall translating everything both ways, and after all eleven of them he had it laid out: a timeline, a *route map of a shift*, with every man and his position and the hour at which each of them had gone under the gate, and two gaps, both forty minutes long, both real, both belonging to men who could not remember the middle of the afternoon.

Roke's was the worse one, because Roke had the slate.

"Here," he said, in his square hand, tapping the second watch. "Two of the hour. I wrote the watch change and I wrote the tally and there is nothing in between, and I know what I was doing because I wrote the tally, and I have been doing this for nineteen years and I do not *forget* a shift."

"You didn't forget it," Adrian said. "You wrote down the numbers so you could hold them, and then your head put the names in a drawer, because it had nine other things to hold. That is the ordinary explanation and it is the one I want, because the other one is that something in the stone put your afternoon in a drawer on purpose, and I have no way on this earth to tell the two apart yet."

Roke turned the slate a hand's width to the left, as if the light in the yard had changed. "You are telling me it is ordinary."

"I'm telling you I don't know whose fault it is. Maybe it's you, at nineteen years, running on slate and no lunch. Maybe it's the lock. Maybe it's the pressure. Maybe it's the sound." Adrian turned the slate round and showed him the layout, the eleven names, the hours, the two gaps side by side, so that Roke could see that somebody had put his hole next to the other one and made them a shape. "This is a record now. And a record can be argued with. A hole in a head can't."

Roke took the slate and turned it over, and then turned it back, and read the two gaps side by side again, and something happened in his face that Adrian did not have a name for.

"My grandfather held this gate," he said, in English, in a voice with nothing in it. "I found that out in the winter, when I was clearing his things out of a house on the upper road."

"Yes," Adrian said.

"He is buried in the ordinary ground behind the ordinary wall. There is no stone with his name on it. There are eleven of us in that ground and I have walked past the gap where eight of them ought to be since I was a boy, and I have never once, in nineteen years, asked anybody why." He put two fingers on the left-hand gap. "Do you know what I have just worked out, Englishman? I have been on nineteen years of shift and I do not know the names of eight men I have been standing shoulder to shoulder with. And you have been here two days and you have got the whole of it out of me in a morning with a piece of chalk."

He got up and went out into the rain.

Nobody followed him. That turned out to be a rule, and it was not a complicated one: in this city a man was allowed to go and stand somewhere wet by himself, and the courtesy was to let him.

---

The voice came back at about the fourth hour, when the rain stopped.

It came out of the line in the chapel floor at the same volume as before, which was the volume of somebody talking in a room with a stone ceiling, and it said the four words again in the local language, slowly, and it made a mistake on the third one.

"Mina," Tamsin said. "Mina, get up. Get up, get up—"

"It's not a word," Mina said, from the floor, where she had fallen asleep on Roke's coat. "It's not the same four words. There's a fifth one. He keeps putting a fifth one in and it's a different word, and it's — Tamsin, it's *earlier*. It's older than the old form. That's not a man remembering a form. That's a man being taught it."

Tamsin sat down on the step of the altar. She put her face in her hands for two seconds, which Adrian had not seen her do, and then she took her hands away and said, "Yes."

"Which word?"

She said it for him. It was one syllable in the middle of the four, and when she said it in English she said *answer*.

"So somebody's down there learning the old oath in the old order," Adrian said, "and it isn't an echo, and it isn't a message somebody left."

"No."

"Then it's a person. And you can hear them. And they can hear—"

He stopped.

The voice said the four words a fifth time, and then it said, in the local language, slowly, the way you would say a name you had been given by a person who had told you it was theirs and you did not believe him:

*Ad-ri-an.*

---

He had about four seconds in which he could have answered.

He was standing six inches from a black line in a stone slab in a world two days old, and something on the other side of it had just said his name, and it had said it in the local language, which meant that whatever was on the other side had learned a language by talking to people, and that it had a *person's* idea of who he was, and that the fourth rule on the wall upstairs — the one he had invented on Tuesday night, for people — applied to this too.

And there was a system in him, an old one, an extremely competent one, that said: *a named thing with no site is a rule with no owner. A thing that repeats is a loop. A thing that says your name is a lookup. Do not answer it. An answer is a promise, and a promise given to something that has nobody standing in it does not land anywhere.*

So he said nothing, and he stood there with his mouth shut and the mark on his palm burning like a coin held too long, and he felt the specific and physical grief of a man who has just been offered the only thing he has wanted for two days and has watched himself not take it.

Tamsin was watching him. She had understood it too, and she was watching him *not do it*, and when he turned round she was looking at him with an expression that was not gratitude.

"You let it go," she said.

"It used my name. It used a name it had no right to. Tamsin, if I answer that, I am making a witness, and there is nobody here to be one."

"You are making a *promise*," she said. "Which is not the same and is worse, and you know that it is worse, and you did it anyway." She stood up off the altar step. "For the record: the man from the wall did not answer the voice. I want that written in the record, tonight, in your hand, because in about six weeks there will be somebody who says he did, and there will be a song about it."

Adrian wrote it in the notebook, in the flat hand, and signed it, and then he found that his right hand was wet and there was a thin line of blood along the heel of his palm, following the branching shape of the mark exactly, like a crack in a thing that used to be whole.

He held it up under the lamp, and Vask said, from the altar step, "Do not wash it."

"I wasn't going to wash it."

"You were thinking about it." She came over and looked at it without touching it. "That is not the cut. The cut is the cut. That is underneath it, and it is black, and it is the length of two of your fingers." She said it in English, flatly, and then again in the local language for Roke, who had come back in out of the rain with the two dockers and was standing in the doorway. "A black line. On a person this time. It has not opened, and it is not going to open tonight, and I have seen it twice in thirty-four years and both times to a man who had told somebody else that he could manage a thing he was being asked to hold."

"That is me," Adrian said. "I told Tamsin on Wednesday that I could follow a join for as long as her crew was holding the lock's working. I have not been able to follow anything all night except a line of black in a slab, and I did it anyway for four hours, and something took that as an answer."

"Yes." Vask wiped her hand on her apron, though she had not touched him. "It is not a punishment and it is not a reward. It is a stone telling you what you are standing on. When somebody finally mends the line in that slab, that will be a day of work and a day of money, and you should not confuse the two. This one is cheaper. This one is worse, because nobody comes and tells you about it. Nobody steps over your hand."

He put the hand in his pocket. He did not show it to anybody. He noticed, distantly, that this was the first time since Tuesday he had made a decision about information on the grounds of what it would do to somebody, and he put the observation away where he kept the other ones he was not ready to look at.

His left ear had been ringing since the voice stopped. It would go on ringing, on that side only, longer than a night, and by the time it finally stopped he would find that he could no longer pick out a specific note that a woman in the second bay made when she counted the men under the gate.

---

They went out at dawn to look at the wall, and the light was flat and grey and ordinary, and Vask got there first and stood on the walk-round in the rain and looked at the bright edge on the join with her hands on her hips.

"There," she said, to Owen, without any particular unkindness. "You want to see the real one? That is the real one. It is not magic. It is new stone and old stone and a hundred and thirty years of somebody not caring to repoint it. You can put your fingers in it. Everybody can put their fingers in it."

Owen went and put his fingers in it, and stood there, and took his hand out, and wiped it, and said, "Yeah," in a completely deflated voice. "Yeah, that's what that is."

Adrian let him have the thirty seconds. It was the kindest thing he did that week, and it cost Owen something, and Adrian understood at the time that a kindness which costs the person it is aimed at is not automatically the kind thing, and went on doing it anyway, because the alternative was better.

Then they went down to the second bay to look at the water, because that was where the answer was, and Adrian had spent a whole night of no sleep working it out.

It came down to arithmetic. There were nine places in this lock where the pressure dropped, and Adrian had listed all of them in the notebook in the order he had found them, with times, and if you drew a line through them in time order it did not wander. It went one way. Every hour, the pressure was being taken out of the same place: the lower channel, the outer field, the eleven villages, the Reed margin, the whole wet country below the lock, being drained a foot an hour by something that was standing in a join and saying an oath four words long.

Tamsin listened to him lay it out and then said, "Say the last part again."

"Every time that voice says those words, the bottom fields lose pressure. Not all the time. Not when we are working. When *it* is the one working." He put the notebook flat on the baulk so she could see the line through the nine marks. "It is a working. It has a pattern, which is the gate. It has a hand on it, which is the man in the gate. And it has a witness, and the witness is—somebody, somewhere, who is willing. That is why it is costing the bottom fields instead of the top ones. Somebody down there has an audience."

The lock made a noise under them, a long grinding shift, and the water in the channel dropped another two inches.

"Right," Adrian said. "Then it is not a ghost and it is not an echo and it is not a broken wall. It is a man in a join, doing a working, spending the river to keep a road open, and he did not ask the eleven villages, and I have met him, and he is *very* good, and that is the worst part, because I have been listening to him for four hours and I like him."

Tamsin looked down at the channel, and at the four words and the one extra syllable written in her notebook, and at the line of black in the slab four feet behind her, which had not moved and had not grown by the width of a hair.

"That is a person down there," she said.

"Yes."

"Alone."

"Or with people. Or with something he is calling people. I don't know, Tamsin. I know the offer on that wall has a deadline and a road and a person-shaped hole in it, and I know that hole has been standing in a join for long enough to know his name, and I know that neither of those things is going to stop just because we have told it to."

The rain started again. Down in the channel the water came up two inches, and then went down one, and then went down another, and far away down in the wet country under the lock, out of sight of anybody, the first thin green shoots in a hundred and forty acres of bottom field began to go back under.

The field did not know. The field had no idea that anything was happening at all.
