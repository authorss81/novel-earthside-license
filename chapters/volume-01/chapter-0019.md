# Chapter 0019 — The Second Cohort Does Not Arrive

It had been the best three weeks of Adrian's life in Veyra, and he wrote at the top of a page that night that he noticed at the time that this was a bad sign.

The rota was on the wall of the loft in Priya's hand in ruled columns, and it had thirty-one names on it, of which six were not from the wall. The second column was the shift and the third was the place and the fourth was what the *work* was for, and Adrian had argued for twenty minutes about the fourth column on the Tuesday and had lost, because Priya had gone and asked Vail and Vail had said, in a clerk's flat voice, that a column which says what a person is for is a column that licenses a person, and that the whole of the practice of a rota in this city is that the job is there and the man is not.

Mina had been in the low room by the wharf four days a week and had stopped calling it the low room.

Dante was carrying loads with a strapped arm and had been careful with it for six days out of the eleven that Hesper Saye had prescribed, and had stopped being careful on about the ninth, and everybody knew it and nobody said it.

Jonah had refused the Brass Measure on the Monday in one sentence in a yard, in front of eleven people, exactly as Priya had instructed him to, and had put the sentence in her log with his own name on it, and had not mentioned it once since.

Noor's ledger had a new column with a small square round it, and four names in it, one of which was Sesh Brack and one of which was the market's common box.

Owen had a lease on two rooms above the chandlery, taken in his own hand because none of them had any standing here, with a landlord's two conditions on it — one of which was to go up the street and ask a market woman whether the roof over her head was the roof being sold, which he had done, and the answer to which he had not yet told anybody about — and a nine-year-old who had decided inside four days that he was the most interesting man in Saltwake and followed him at a distance of about six feet.

And there was a board on the chapel door with Vask's list of what may not come out of the hole on one side and the keeper's four words and the keeper's fifth word in chalk on the other, in letters four inches high, and since the bar had gone in under the north flagstones on the Saturday before last, the binding had been said in the open, in the yard, twice a day, in whatever weather, by whoever was nearest.

And Owen Park had been saying it.

He had learned the four words over four days from Vask, who corrected him every eight seconds, and he had learned the fifth word — the keeper's word, the one that is not in the oath, the one Nyla Ferrow's mother said every morning for thirty years and which had not been *said* once in four hundred years, only kept — on the sixth day, and Vask had told him to leave it out of it for four more days, and he had obeyed.

Adrian had been woken by it every morning for nine days. Six o'clock, in the rain, a foreign accent reciting four words of a maintenance oath in a dead-flat voice with a mason correcting him, and then Owen, out of breath, saying the fifth one wrong.

---

The walk-round was thin on the Friday night and it was thin because of him.

That was the sentence he used for a week afterwards, at four in the morning, in a room above a market, and it was true, and it turned out to be about nine times more complicated than true.

The cost column of the Narrow Mark said, in Sera Vail's hand, on page one: *one shared labour shift, one day a month, taken by the witnesses and by the holder alike, out of the gate crew's rotation.* Which meant that on the fourth Friday of the month the day crew of the Lock of Salt — eighteen men, one foreman, and the two dockers who had held the quorum in the outer channel — were not on the gate. They were in the yard, on the dig terms, on a day's labour. And the flood shift had been doubled to cover, and the walk-round, which is the two hundred steps of outside wall that nobody in this city has watched properly since a Hearthguard called Brann stood in the yard on the morning the six came through and shouted for a change of watch, had one man on it.

One man, and it was Ovin Sarr, who was nineteen, and who had a lantern, a line, and a horn, and who had been told at the top of the stair by Roke, in the local language and then in English, that if the join on the outer wall opened a hand's width he was to blow the horn and go up, and not to go down, and not to look.

Adrian had not sent Roke up the stair. Roke had gone because the day crew had gone, and the day crew had gone because there was a line in a book with a name in it, and the name was his.

He came anyway, and nobody had asked him to, and he wrote that down.

---

He took them at one o'clock in the morning, alone, in the lower gallery, with the folding steel rule and a stub of chalk and the lamp turned down as far as it would go.

Not on the walk-round. He had started out on the walk-round on the first night, because that is where a man goes, and he had given it up after two nights when it became obvious that the outer wall of the outer channel is not where any of this is happening and that a rule held against a parapet in the rain gives you a number about a parapet. The three places were in the gate itself: three places in the pale edge of the main breach where the new stone meets the old, one low, one in the middle, one high, marked with three small crosses of chalk that nobody had ever looked at and that he had rubbed out and put back every night for three weeks so that he would know he was measuring the same stone.

The lower gallery is thirty feet under a yard and it is the loudest room in this city. At one in the morning there is nobody in it. The water moves over the sills in the three bays and makes the noise it makes, and a man can stand in the second bay in a suit with a lamp on the sill and put a steel rule into a crack in a gate and write a number down, and if anybody had come down the stair and looked at him they would have seen a man doing something absurd.

He had twenty of them by the Friday, one a night. He had been writing them in a column since the night he woke up on the stones with a fever, and he had not once plotted them, because plotting was the kind of thing you did when you were not doing process, and he had spent three weeks doing process.

He plotted them on the step under the near gate at about ten past one, in the cold, on the back page, with a pencil stub.

They went: four and seven-eighths, four and seven-eighths, four and seven-eighths, four and seven-eighths, five and five-eighths, five and five-eighths, five and five-eighths, five and five-eighths, five and five-eighths, five and five-eighths, six and three-eighths, six and three-eighths, six and three-eighths, six and three-eighths, six and three-eighths, seven and one-eighth, seven and one-eighth, seven and one-eighth, seven and seven-eighths, seven and seven-eighths.

Four steps in twenty nights. Three inches in twenty nights, and every inch of it arrived in a quarter-hour, and it sat there for days at a time like a man waiting for something to be over. And a crack does not step.

That was the thing Vask had given him on the first morning, in the tunnels, in the rain, in about forty seconds, and he had written it in the notebook and had not understood it for three weeks: a crack under load grows smoothly, and it grows faster the more load there is, and a mason can tell the difference between a wall that is being *pressed* and a wall that is being *worked* by the shape of the movement on a gauge. A press is a curve. A work is a decision.

He put a mark in the margin beside the four jumps and wrote the four dates beside those.

The first: the Friday the book was read and the mark went into the seam in the outer channel, and two hundred and eleven people stood in a salt store until one in the afternoon, and the gate crew put a second man under the near gate all day because there was a crowd coming down to the lock in the rain.

The second: the day the water at Silt Row went brackish and the reed shift was doubled for four days running and a gate had to be set in the dark on the Wednesday of it.

The third: the day after that, when the whole flood crew went out to the Reach to re-set nine gates in one morning because if they had gone one at a time it would have been the ninth day instead of the eighth.

The fourth: the day of the inspection. Ninety people on a wet walk-round, a salt factor reading out a column he had not been asked to read, a woman of Nine Elms committing a refusal she had already given once, and a woman of sixty from the upper road who had walked down two hundred steps to say that she was not here for the seam at all.

Not the tide. Not the flood. The tide had come and gone on every one of those twenty nights and had not moved that wall by a hair, and neither had the rain, and neither had the first cold of the season, which had come in the second week and had been the only other new thing in the month.

Adrian sat on the step under the near gate with the lamp on the sill and followed it one step further, because that is the only thing he can do, and it is the reason anybody thinks he is worth anything.

*Four steps. Four days of unusual work at this lock, out of twenty days of ordinary work that moved it not at all.*

*Here is the part I cannot get past. The near gate is a join the dockers hold with the oath, and it stands nine feet from the join in the gate, in the same course of stone and not in the same piece of it. The main breach is the resonant site: three jurisdictions say the same words there twice a day. And the near gate and the breach are nine feet apart and they are not separate facts, they are the same thirty-foot wall of the same lock with a gate in it, and a wall does not know that a join is nine feet from another join. When the crew is doubled under the near gate and says the words forty extra times, the stone they are standing in is the stone the breach is cut in.*

*Which means the ordinary maintenance of the Lock of Salt is not the background to this. It is the mechanism. Which means every shift of that gate crew is a small payment out of a hundred and forty acres, and the payment is a foot an hour, and nobody has noticed because a foot an hour is nothing to a man standing next to it.*

*And which means the one thing any person in this city could do to the main breach is stop working the lock.*

*And stopping the lock means the tide comes in through a wall that is three inches wider than it was when I woke up on the stones, in the next flood, and eleven villages drown in the ember month instead of a field going under in the flood month.*

He put the pencil down on the step.

There was no version of it with a correct response attached. Not the record, not the seat, not a receiving community that may suspend, not a refused route, not a piece of paper. A book can hold a cost. A book cannot hold a choice between a field and a flood, because a field is a number and a flood is a Tuesday, and Vail's ledger had been built by a city that had known that for nine hundred years and had solved it the only way it could, which was by making the maintenance oath a thing that a person stands in, twice a day, in the rain, so that at least there would be somebody in it.

The measurement was good. That was the horror. It was honest, it was twenty nights of his own arithmetic with a folding steel rule and it was the straightest line he had ever drawn, and it said that this city's ordinary work was costing a field an hour at a time, and the only lever in the world was to stop doing the ordinary work, and the only consequence of pulling the lever was a flood.

He wrote one more line, in the flat hand, because he had learned in Chapter 5 of his own life — Wednesday, in the tunnels, with blood coming out of his nose — that a conclusion you have not argued against is a conclusion you have not earned:

*This is unactionable. That is not a reason to keep it to myself. It is a reason to be extremely careful about the first two sentences, because the first two sentences anybody says about this in a market are the ones the man with the lever is frightened of, and they will be: he says the lock is eating the field. He says the lock has to stop.*

---

He was on the second turn of the stair at about ten past one when he decided, and it took four seconds, and he wrote it down afterwards and underlined it twice, which he had not done for anything else in the notebook in three weeks.

*Not the room. Not all six. Mina, first.*

*Not because she is the doctor. Because in her loft she overruled me in front of five people and she was right, and because I have spent three weeks in rooms full of people and I have never once had to persuade a single one of them of anything, and because I would like to find out what happens when I hand a thing to a person I have no reason to think I can manage.*

*And write down the date. Tonight. Because I know what I am, and the reason I am not telling everybody at once is not that I am protecting them. It is that I have been the only one who can see for three weeks and it is the best feeling I have ever had in my life, and I am not going to hand a room a weapon I have not even fired.*

---

The binding in the yard was at six and at noon, and Adrian came up onto the yard at twenty past five because he could not sleep.

Ovin Sarr came up behind him at about the same time from the other stair, wet to the thigh, nineteen years old, with a horn he had not had to blow, and he said, in the local language, and then, unexpectedly, in English:

"It is quiet."

"Quiet."

"Three weeks." The boy was elated about it and Adrian did not have the heart to tell him what he was actually doing on that wall. "I have had a watch where I did not blow the horn. Roke said if I had four in a row then I am to be let out on my own in daylight. That is the rule. Four in a row."

"You have had four in a row?"

"Six," said Ovin Sarr, with enormous pride, and went to dry his line.

Adrian sat down on the trestle with his notebook for eleven minutes and did not say anything.

He watched a nineteen-year-old's name get a key back on a board on his second morning, and he had spent four hours of his life trying to make sure a public record would survive him. He watched a nine-year-old on the upper road follow a man at a distance of six feet. He watched a woman of sixty carry a folded sheet of paper down two hundred wet steps to a wall.

He watched Owen Park say the four words, badly, in a dead-flat accent, in the rain, with Vask on his left correcting him every eight seconds and Nyla Ferrow on his right with her arms folded saying nothing at all, which was the highest thing anybody had said to him in this world.

Owen got to the fourth one and stopped, and looked at Nyla, and she did not move, and he said the fifth one, and he said it correctly, and Adrian's hand went cold on the notebook.

The yard did not change. The rain went on being rain. Nyla Ferrow gave a short nod and went inside, and Vask said something short in the local language that made Owen stand there for a second with his eyes shut.

And then, at the fourth hour, from four feet away, out of a raised pale slab of stone that every person in this city steps over every time they come into a chapel to keep a gate, a voice said a sentence in English.

It was not a whisper and it was not loud. It was the volume of somebody talking in a room with a stone ceiling, at a distance of about four feet, and it was the voice Adrian had been hearing at four in the morning for three weeks.

It said one sentence.

"You are in the seat, and they can ask you now, and a chair is a system that has stopped asking whether it is needed."

---

Nobody in the yard moved for about six seconds.

Then Nyla Ferrow, from the chapel doorway, in the local language, said four words in a voice Adrian had heard her use exactly twice, and then she said the fifth, and the yard was very quiet.

"It is here," she said, in English, which she spoke badly and did not care about. "It is here. Not the floor. Not the arch. From the stone everybody steps over. It has been there since the seventh day and I have said to it every morning I have stood on it, and I could not hear it, and I did not tell you because I did not think a man from a wall would hear it when a keeper of the room could not."

"You knew."

"I knew there was a line in it since the second morning," said Nyla Ferrow, "and I have not been able to hear a thing speak from that stone since I was nineteen years old and my mother was alive, and I did not know that I could not until the second morning, and I have been afraid of it for nine days, and I have not put it in a book because I did not know who to put it in a book for. You are the man in the book. So now it is in a book. That is all that has happened."

---

He told Mina at half past six, in the corridor outside the low room by the wharf, where she was already standing because she had come up early to look at Owen's head, and he gave it to her the way he had given it to himself — the numbers first, then the steps, then the pattern, then the only conclusion it would bear.

He told Tamsin at seven, and he told her separately, and that was not a choice about trust: the voice had come out of the chapel's own threshold stone, and the keeper of that room had a right to be told by the hour, the way anybody is told about a thing that happened in their floor.

He told the other five at nine, together, in the Salt Weavers' chapel with the plaster still on the north wall, and he gave it to them the same way and then, last, the three sentences.

Jonah Pike stood up out of his chair.

"You have just told this room that the Lock of Salt is the mechanism," he said. "Do you understand what you have handed me? There is a Crown office, and a man in a good coat who has not been in this room tonight, and I could put this in a letter in a form that would arrive in Aurel in nine days, and the letter would say *the lock is charged, the lock is leaking, the lock is doing this*, and not one clerk in that building would fail to act on it, and I would be ending it. Permanently. The road goes, the flood goes, the field is safe, you go home."

"Then do it," Adrian said.

"Do not do that to me," Jonah said. "I have not slept. I have been sitting on that for two hours deciding whether I can live with the version where I do not, and I want it in Priya's log that it took me two hours and not eleven minutes, because it is going to matter in a year and I am going to remember whether I was fast or slow."

"No one will put that in a log," Priya said, "because if it is in a log then it is a thing we considered, and in a year somebody with a warrant is going to want to know whether we considered it."

Owen Park had not stood up. He had been sitting with his hands flat on the table.

"It is not the lock," he said. "You are all looking at a lock. There is a man in a join. It has been that since the seventh day. Adrian's numbers say the lock is where he does it, and that he is stronger when we work, and that the way to stop him is for all of us to stop. It is not a thing about locks. I have been sitting in this room for two hours and I am the only one who has got the thing none of you are saying."

"Say it."

"It is not the *stone*," Owen said. "It is the words. It is what we say under that gate. The numbers step when the crew is doubled, and the crew gets doubled when there is extra work, and what the crew does under that gate is say the maintenance oath. So the thing that spends the bottom field is not the lock. It is four words that forty men say twice a day to keep a gate shut, and whoever is down there is standing in the middle of them. I do not think he built that. I think he found it, the way we found a hole in a wall." He turned round. "Which means the thing in that join is a man on a *route*, and I have been asking the wrong question since the Tuesday I arrived. I asked it out loud in a market on that Tuesday and Sesh Brack told me the cost of going east, and I went upstairs instead, and I have been upstairs ever since. So: I am not being difficult and I am not being brave. I am saying that if he is on a route then the field is a toll, and if the field is a toll then somebody is being *charged*, and the only question left in this world is by whom, and whether they have agreed to it — and they have not, because I have been in this room for three weeks and I have not seen one single person in it who has agreed to anything."

---

Nobody in the room said the last sentence of the voice for about nine seconds.

Then Owen said, "Read it again," and Adrian read it again, and Priya wrote it out in her own hand, which she had not done for anything since the night of the wall, and then the three of them sat in a cold room and looked at twelve words on a slate.

"That is your sentence," Tamsin said. "That is *your* sentence, Adrian. Not the oath. Not the old form. That is not a phrase out of a city that has been here nine hundred years. That is a man in a room designing a thing he was going to ship."

"Yes."

"Say where you wrote it."

Adrian put his hand flat on the notebook, the way you do.

"Nearly three months ago, in my time. In a room four floors above a car park in another hemisphere, at about four in the morning, in a document called `ACOA_PERM_LEDGER_LOCAL_ONLY`." He was aware that his voice had gone into the flat register, and he let it. "It is the *only* sentence in that document that is not in the document. It is the sentence underneath the joke underneath the rule. And one Tuesday I argued in a meeting for about forty minutes that a confirmation screen should be permanent in a product that has been cancelled, and I lost the argument, and at four in the morning I wrote that sentence at the bottom of the page in the wrong file, and eight days later I deleted the whole section, and the file has not been opened since before I came here, and it is on a laptop in a locked office in a building that has a door code that changes on Mondays."

"Which means it is not on the laptop," said Noor Haddad, in a very quiet voice, from the end of the table. "Which means it is not anywhere. Which means it did not come from a file, because there is no file."

"Yes," said Adrian.

"Then where did it come from?"

Adrian looked at his own right hand, at the heel of the palm, at the thin pale ghost of a branching line and, underneath it, two inches of hair-thin black that had not moved in three weeks and that nobody in this room could see and that he was going to feel for the rest of his life.

"From me," he said. "Or from the thing that has been listening to me since Tuesday at twenty to three in the morning, and I do not know which, and I have been constructing a fairly excellent explanation in my head since one o'clock this morning in which the honest answer is that a man in a good coat has been reading my files, and that explanation requires me to be a spy, and I have been gripping it like a rail because I would very much like it to be true."

Nobody said anything.

"I want it on the wall," Adrian said. "Not the numbers. The last part. *I do not know where the sentence came from, and one of the two answers is that it has been inside me since Tuesday, and I have decided to write that down and put it where I cannot find it alone, because I have been the only one who can see for three weeks and it is the best I have felt in this world and I would like somebody else to have hold of the other end of it.*"

"Say that again, the last part, and let me write it in the local language," Tamsin said. "Because that is the part that is not about a wall, and the wall is the part every man in this room is standing behind right now."

Adrian said it again. Tamsin wrote it in the local language, in her own hand, on the back of a board, and Nyla Ferrow's son carried it down to the chapel on the Saturday morning and Nyla Ferrow chalked it on the door under Vask's list, on the other side from the four words and the fifth, where anybody who came up into that yard would have to read it before they could get at the rest of it.

And then Adrian went out into the rain and walked up and down the yard for a while with Owen Park, who had come out with him because Owen Park had spent nine days learning four words out loud in the rain and had decided, somewhere around the third of them, that he was not going to be the sort of man who stayed inside.

At about half past ten Adrian said, "I told her first."

"Mina."

"She overruled me eight days ago in front of five people and she was right, and I have spent eight days making a list of everybody I could convince and she was not on the list, and I have known for about a month that I do not actually have a way of telling anything to anybody in this world except people I can already make a plan with, and it turns out that is not a professional skill. It is a *kindness* for me."

Owen was quiet for a moment.

"It is not," he said. "It is the only thing you have done in this room that is not a system. And I want you to notice that you have spent three weeks making a rota of six people and you have had one conversation that was not about a rota, and it was the one where you gave somebody the thing you were frightened of before you gave it to the rest of us, and she earned it inside a week, and you have been treating it like a technique ever since."

The rain came down on the yard of a lock chapel at half past ten on a Friday night, and the ground under the north flagstones was a void that had been under there longer than the chapel, and the arch was being promised twice a day by a man from another world, and the main breach in the gate was open and watched and three inches wider than it had been the night I woke up on the stones, and in a cold room two floors above a market a woman was writing twelve words onto a board, and in a yard a woman of sixty was chalking a confession onto a door, and Adrian Vale stood in the rain with a man who could see a light nobody else could see and said the thing he had been not saying for three weeks:

"It might be me. That is the one I cannot get out from under. There is a man in a join who has been spending a river an hour at a time for three weeks, and I can see a line in a wall that nobody else can see, and I have a black mark in my hand that has not moved in three weeks and that four people have told me is a stone telling me what I am standing on, and it is possible — it is *possible* — that whatever is down there has been getting a running commentary since Tuesday, and that I have been running it myself, and that the voice is not on the other side of anything. It is in the room. It is the room. I have been calling it a voice out of a wall since Tuesday night because that is the sentence that does not have me in it."

"That is a different thing from everything else you have said tonight," Owen said.

"Yes."

"Are you going to be all right?"

"No," Adrian said. "But I have got a notebook, and I have got a keeper who is going to put a sentence on a door, and I have got four people who can stop me, and I have got a village that can say no, and I have got about eleven people in this city who are walking out onto a flat field in the dark putting flour on the banks of a ditch that nobody ordered them to." He turned his hand over and looked at the heel of it in the light from the chapel door, and there was nothing to see, because there was never anything to see. "That is the whole of what I have got. It is not a power. It is a queue with people in it. I have spent a decade building systems so that nobody would ever need me, and it worked beautifully, and now the only thing I can do in this world is stand in a room and be the one it comes to, and it turns out that is the job, and nobody advertised it."
