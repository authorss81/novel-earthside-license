# Chapter 0030 — The Hearing

The second letter from Aurel arrived on the morning of the thirty-first day of the flood, in a leather case, by the hand of Peth Lammay, eleven years a carrier of the Crown's second-table mail, who put it on the table of the Lantern Concord at about the second hour and then went and sat down at the back, because she has learned where to sit in a room in this city.

Adrian read it twice before the hearing and then a third time in the yard at ten to five, and what he took from it, and has written down, is that it is the first document in a month that was not written by anybody who lives here.

> **From the Office of Crossings and Tides at Aurel. To the Lantern Concord of Saltwake, and to the holder of the pull of the instrument called the Narrow Mark, and to the nine households of Nine Elms by name.**
>
> **1. The refusal entered at page twenty-three of your book on the ninth day of this flood is received. A file has been opened. It is not a proceeding. Nothing in this letter begins a proceeding.**
>
> **2. It has come to the notice of this office that on the twenty-eighth day of this month the instrument called the Narrow Mark was used, before this office had ruled on whether the instrument may exist, and that one person was carried through it, and that the standing terms of the instrument were broken in public to permit the use. The office notes the use. The office does not accept the use. A thing entered and then used is not thereby recognised, and a route that has been travelled is not thereby a road.**
>
> **3. The Registry requires, before the next quarterly sitting, one of two things, in writing, under the seal of the Concord.**
>
> **    (a) A registered holder. That is: a named person, holding a workway recognised by this office, answerable to the Registrar of Crossings and Tides, receiving the protection and the stipend of this office, with a Crown advocate appointed to him.**
>
> **    (b) A statement of permanent closure. That is: a statement, sealed by the Concord, that no crossing will be made from the Lock of Salt in any form, in any direction, by any person, on any day, and that any person who makes one is answerable to this office.**
>
> **4. The Registry notes that the holder has described himself in the roll of strangers as *Unbound*, and that a seat with no workway is not a form this office is able to accept, and that a holder who is unable to hold the thing he holds is not a holder but a position, and a position may be filled by a person who can hold it.**
>
> **5. The Registry notes that the refusal at page twenty-three declines eleven villages money which has been offered to them, and the Registry does not say that this was right. The Registry says that a refusal which costs other people money is a decision about other people, and that a decision about other people entered in a book in one city is read in a court in another, and that a man who has refused compensation may at a later date be asked by a person he refused it to, whether he would accept it now.**
>
> **6. The Registry will not be represented at the sitting. The Registry has read the terms and the cost and the two refusals and has nothing to add to them at this time.**
>
> **— Anwen Kroon, Registrar of Crossings and Tides, Aurel.**

Item four is the one Adrian read eleven times.

Item six is the one that made four people stand up in a room.

---

The hearing was at the first light of the thirty-first day of the flood, in a converted salt store, white to the shoulder with brine and black to the beams with four hundred years of smoke, and there were about four hundred and ten people in it, which Aldis Kerrow entered in the roll as *the largest attendance in the history of the Lantern Concord*, and which Sera Vail entered as *a number*, and which is how Sera Vail has entered every number for twenty-six years.

They were not all there for the question.

Ninety of them were there because a letter had come from Aurel and everybody in a city of eleven hundred people knew about it by the second hour of the previous day. A hundred and forty of them were there because a piece of paper had been taken down the Reach road on Thursday by a woman who carries the Crown's mail, in her own hand, ahead of the Crown's case, and a hundred and twenty of them were on the two benches at the back who had come out of a ground in a yard on the twenty-fifth day of the month. And about sixty people were there for no reason anybody could identify, and Vail entered that too, because in nine days somebody would say that those sixty were organised, and she wanted a page that said they were not.

On the north wall, under oiled cloth, the roll of public business had eleven sheets on it. The first was the four terms of the instrument. The second was the continuation of the review. The third was the count of the twenty-fifth day. The fourth was the crown's letter from Aurel, entered as a fact and read out in full by **Renner Sallow**, who is the second clerk of the Registry and who had sat beside Vail at that table since the first sitting without anybody noticing him, and who stood up on the thirtieth and read two hundred and forty words of a letter in a flat voice and was the only person in that room whom nobody had ever heard speak at length, and every head in the building turned round to look at him, and he was aware of it in a way that Adrian recognised in about four seconds because he had watched himself do the same thing for a month.

The fifth sheet was the description of the dressed joint beneath the north flagstones, entered on the twenty-fourth day, and not named.

The sixth sheet was the entry of the twenty-fifth, and Adrian read the top line of it and had to stop reading for a second, and then went on, because he was reading, and it was his job that morning, and the terms of the Chapter 27 opening put him in the third bay of the lower gallery at the sixth hour on the twenty-eighth with a stub of chalk and no vote.

> **The sixth sheet. Entered the twenty-sixth day of the flood, at the eleventh hour of the twenty-fifth, read twice, witnessed.**
>
> **The dressed joint described on the twenty-fourth day, beneath the north flagstones of the yard of the lock chapel, four feet beyond the threshold slab, described and not named, is entered as SCARRED.**
>
> **It is the first place in the history of this city entered as damaged and not closable. It was not made by a working of anybody's. It was opened by the residue of a working performed at a distance of three hundred yards, and the working was consented to, costed, witnessed, and performed by persons of this city, and it did the work it was set to do, and its cost arrived where it was not sent.**
>
> **The word used in this city for what has happened is *a black mark in a place*. There is one other in this book, entered on the seventh night of the flood, of a single pale slab at the foot of the arch which every person in this city steps over. They are not the same thing. A line in a place and a mark in a person do not behave the same way, and a man in this city who has told me so is not wrong, and I have written it as he said it, in his words, and the words were the words of a mason with thirty-four years in this city, and they are in the book on the twenty-fourth day, under a description, and not summarised.**

Vask wrote the phrase **a black mark in a place** on the chapel door on the twenty-seventh, in her square mason's hand, under her own list, and nobody in that yard has read the two things together and understood them, and Adrian has, and it is the entry he is going to have to say out loud in about an hour.

---

The question was put at about the fourth hour, and Kerrow put it, and he put it in English and then in the local language off his slate, as he had put it on a walk-round in the rain a fortnight ago, so that nobody could be helped by the speed of it.

"Sera Vail will read back. The instrument entered in this city's book on the fourth day of the flood, at the outer wall of the outer channel of this lock, is entered, costed, inspected, signed, and formally unrecognised. It was used on the twenty-eighth, in breach of its own terms, by one person, and the person was brought back, and the terms were broken in public to permit it. It has not been recognised, and being travelled has not made it a road, and a notice from Aurel has been read.

"The question this Concord opened on the morning after the second tide, and has not answered in twenty-seven days, and is required to answer at this sitting, is this, and it is the only question, and it is a fair question and it has been fair since the day you came out of the wall: **may a first license exist in Saltwake?**"

Four hundred and ten people in a salt store, and a boy of nineteen at the back who had been in two of them, and a woman of sixty-one from Four Elms who had walked eleven miles, and a mason who had signed a page in a wet yard in her own hand with the word *mason* spelled out, and a docker of about sixty in a felt hat in the third row, and Hettis Roke on the side wall with a waxed slate, and Sera Vail at the table with the chain through the ring, and Adrian Vale standing in the gap in the floor where a man stands when a room is going to say something to him.

Which was not the question.

"The question in front of this room," said a woman from the fourth bench, in the local language, and Tamsin translated it, "is not whether there is a door. Everybody in this room knows there is a shape in a wall. The question in front of this room is who may be angry at whom, and nobody in this room wants that answered, and I am the first person in this hall who has said so out loud in a month and I have said it in front of four hundred people, and my name is **Wenna Cray**, of Silt Row, and I am on page one of this book against his name, and I would like that read out before I say anything else so that the room knows who is talking."

Vail read it out. It took eleven seconds and the room was silent for all of them.

"So here is what is in front of the room," said Wenna Cray. "There is a village of nine households that has said no twice, in a room, in words, and it has said it as a *commitment*, and if this instrument is used again the whole of this coast finds out in a week that those nine households are the reason, and that is a thing nine households can survive and a thing nine households never recover from, because a village that is the reason a door did not open is a village that gets asked for everything after that. I know that. I have been one of the four hundred people who looked at them and thought *well, somebody has to*."

"And on the other side of the room there is a man in a good coat who has been nothing but useful for a month, and a letter out of Aurel that says a *position* may be filled by a person who can hold it, and there is a boy of nineteen at the back of this hall who went through a wall on Monday and came back with a paper, and there is a woman of twenty-nine on the reach road who has put a piece of paper into a village before a clerk can, and every single one of those is a person this city should be angry at, and about half of them are people we should be angry at in nine months instead of this morning."

"And him." She did not point. "He is the only person in this room who is answerable, and everybody in this room has had a month to be angry at him and has used it, and he has been in a yard every morning and a hall every morning and a gate every morning, and in a fortnight he is going to be a man in a city with a scar in his ground and a number in a book and eleven strangers and a route, and the question is not whether he may keep the route. **The question is who is allowed to be angry at him, and how long for, and what happens on the day he stops being angry-able.**"

---

They argued about that for two hours and forty minutes and it is in the book, and the argument was the argument this volume exists to have.

Ottoline Vask wanted the instrument finished. Marek Osley wanted a number and had wanted a number for a month. Jonah Pike wanted a form of words that could be carried to Aurel in a case in April, and produced one, and it was good, and Adrian said the two best sentences of it out loud in the room and then said the third sentence was a lie, and Jonah took it out. Nyla Ferrow said that the ground under her mother's floor was not going to be shut by a piece of business in a salt store and that she would read the binding in the yard at six that evening whatever this room decided, and about a hundred and forty people stood up and went out with her at that point, and Kerrow entered the number that left, because a clerk enters everything.

Adrian said, at about the seventh hour, the thing that is the entire content of the chapter, and it took four minutes and it was the third time in a month that anybody had asked him what the mark in the wall was for.

"Three weeks ago on a walk-round in the rain I said *I do not know*, and it took two minutes and eleven seconds, and about forty people were disappointed in me and stayed, and a docker stood up and said it was the first true thing anybody had said on that wall in a fortnight, and asked for it to be asked again in a month and again in a year. So here it is, a month, in a hall, and I have had a smaller answer and it is uglier.

"If this were an *onboarding*—" and he stopped, in the middle of the room, in front of four hundred and ten people, because that was a word from another world and about forty of them had never heard him say a syllable of it, and because the whole point of the next thirty seconds was not to slip.

"Three weeks ago I had a large answer and it was *I do not know*. This is the small one and I have had five days to get it. **The mark in that wall is a place where one person at a time can be named, out loud, by other people, before they go somewhere.** That is the whole of what it has ever done. Everything I wanted it to be was in my own head, and I have said that out loud in this room in the last fortnight four times, and the fourth time somebody wrote it on a keeper's wall in a keeper's own words, and it is still the only true thing I know about it.

"And the cost of it is not eleven measures of salt. The cost of it is **the crew.**"

He turned and looked at the door, where a hundred and forty people had just gone out into the rain.

"Every opening needs a fresh inspection. A fresh inspection is four people turning up in the wrong weather, and those four people are the four who signed, and every one of them is out of the gate crew, and the gate crew is nineteen men and one foreman, and the flood crew is eleven, and one of them is nineteen years old. And the work at the lock is the thing that is opening the join in the gate. I have said that in a notebook and in a yard and in a room above a market and nobody has acted on it, and here is why nobody has acted on it, and it is the answer to your question, and it is this: **the price of a door in this city is the crew, and the crew is the wall, and a wall cannot be a price for very long.**

"And the people who pay it are not in this room. They are at Long Water and Four Elms and Black Sluice and Silt Row and Corrow. They are paying it in the thing a promise is made of, and not one of them is in this hall, and I have not once in thirty-one days asked one of them what they think a door is for, and I am not going to today, because I do not have the right, and because there is a form on a wall upstairs that says what happens when a person does not want to tell, and the answer to that is that you build the thing that does not need them to tell you, and I have not built it, and I told this room in the fourth week of this flood that I did not know how, and I have not taken it back, and I am not going to learn it in a hall."

He put his hand flat on the notebook.

"So: I do not know if a first license may exist in Saltwake. That is not a heroic answer and it is the same one as three weeks ago. What I can tell this room is what it would cost if it did, and who would pay it, and the answer to who would pay it is *not in this room*, and that is the reason I am standing here saying I do not know for the second time, and I would like the first person who asks me to do it again to be somebody who is not in this hall, and I do not know how to arrange that, and I have tried for a month."

---

The answer came at about the ninth hour and it was four minutes long and it closed nothing, and this is the whole of it, and it is entered in the ledger and read back:

**The instrument called the Narrow Mark is not licensed. It is continued.**

**The instrument called the Narrow Mark is continued in this city's book, with its cost column standing, with its four witnesses standing, with the standing of the Reed Reach to suspend it standing, and with both refusals standing — that of the nine households of Nine Elms, given twice, and that of the instrument, which is that it is formally unrecognised. Those are entered together, in the same entry, on the same page, and neither of them is above the other, and a person reading either of them is required to read the other.**

**The instrument may not be used.** It was used once, on the twenty-eighth day of the flood, in breach of its own terms, in public, by one named person, and the person was brought back, and the use is entered as a breach and is not a precedent and does not make the instrument a road. It may not be used again until: a fresh inspection has been performed; the receiving community has not suspended it; and this question is answered.

**And the hearing's product is not a permission. It is this:**

> **A STATEMENT OF ERROR.**
>
> **Any person in this city may enter, in this book, a statement of error against any instrument, entry, count, or record entered in it, in their own words, with their name on it, and without giving a reason and without a cost.**
>
> **The person named by the statement must answer it in public, in a room, within a term stated at the time of the entering, or must withdraw it or say in public that they stand by it and can say no more.**
>
> **No entry of this kind may be removed from this book except by the person who entered it.**
>
> **An instrument, entry, count, or record against which a statement of error stands unanswered may not be used for any purpose, be relied on by any person, or be carried to any court, until the statement is answered or withdrawn.**
>
> **And: the holder of the pull may not enter one against anything on his own seat. Nor may any person of his household, nor any person whose standing in this city is derived from his. That exclusion is entered because he asked for it and a woman with a room by the wharf told him in the second week of the flood that a rule about people made by a man who had not asked was the worst rule on a wall.**

"And it has a name," said Vail, "which I have not got, because I have been a notary for twenty-six years and I have never had to name one of these, and if I name it wrong it becomes a form and forms are what killed the Crown about ninety years ago. Quill. You have the local tongue for this and I have been listening to you say *it* in four different ways all morning."

Tamsin Quill took about nine seconds.

"We do not have a word for it," she said, in the local language. "We have a phrase. The phrase is what a keeper says when two witnesses disagree about a thing that happened at an arch, and one of them is right and one of them is wrong and both of them are standing there. The keeper does not decide. The keeper says: **say it out loud again.** And then everybody in the room hears both of them, and the book has both of them, and in four hundred years there is a page that somebody can point at."

"Then that is what it is called," said Vail. "Say it out loud again. And I want the phrase on the notice, in the local language, and the rule under it, and I want the two of them on one sheet so that anybody who takes the sheet away takes both."

---

The first statement of error in the history of the Lantern Concord was entered at about the tenth hour of the morning of the thirty-first day of the flood, and it was entered by a man of twenty-two from Long Water, and it was about a gate, and it is four sentences long, and Adrian has it in a notebook and has read it more than anything else in the month.

"My name is Osten Fenn. I hold the second gate at Long Water. On the sixteenth day of this flood the Concord entered that a first inspection of the instrument was performed at the walk-round by four named persons, and I do not say that four persons did not stand there. I say that the gate crew of Long Water was on the bank at Long Water all that day, in the rain, because we were setting a gate that had gone, and there were nine of us and a boat, and we were not on a wall in Saltwake. So the four persons who were entered as being at the walk-round were not off the day crew. And if the four who signed were not off the day crew, then the man in this city who is in a book was not the only one who was somewhere he should not have been that day, and I would like the Concord to say whose name is in the entry and why."

Vail read it back. It took a minute and a half.

"The second gate at Long Water is entered as set on the fifteenth," said Vail, "by a man of twenty-two years, on his own time, out of the roof of his own house, and not reported to anybody, and the entry of the sixteenth says the day crew was on the wall. One of those two entries is wrong and I do not know which and I am not going to find out in a room at the tenth hour of a hearing."

"Then say it is wrong," said Adrian.

"I am going to say *doubtful*," said Sera Vail, "which is a word I have never entered in this book, and I am entering it now, and I am telling the room that a word has entered this book today that was not in it yesterday, and that the reason it is in it is a twenty-two-year-old with a boathouse, and that in four hundred years somebody will be extremely grateful to him and will not remember his name, and I am putting his name on the page so that they can."

---

And at about the fourth hour of the afternoon of the thirty-first day of the flood, Dante Kuo, who is thirty-five and who had a right forearm reduced on the tenth day of this flood and who had stopped being careful with it on the twenty-first, put his right hand up to steady a door frame in a yard in the rain, and it went.

It was not the forearm. It had been the forearm for twenty-one days and the forearm was fine and would be fine, and Saye had told him in the second week that the shoulder had been doing the arm's work for three days without being asked and that in three weeks he would lift a sack and something would go that was neither the arm nor the sack.

He did not lift a sack. He had been carrying nothing at all since the twenty-ninth, because a man in a good coat had put him on the yard work and on the ledger and had never once let him lift anything, which is the single most useful thing Ivenn Marrow has done in this city and is entered in no book.

He steadied a door frame.

Hesper Saye said, from the step, without any particular expression, to about forty people: "There it is. Day after the heavy thing. It is *never* the heavy thing." And then, to Dante, in the local language: "You will be able to use it. Do not be told otherwise. But you will not be able to carry above your head, and you will not be able to put both hands above your head, and in about eleven years you will not be able to put a hand on a roof, and you will not notice any of it for about a year, and then you will notice it every day. And I have said this to four hundred men and I have been wrong about ninety of them, and I would like it entered that I said it in a yard in front of forty people and not in a room with him in it."

---

And at about the sixth hour of the evening, in the lower gallery, at the top of the second stair, with about nine people, Adrian went to look at the tide table on the wall.

It is a board about four feet long with the tide in columns and the work of the lock in the last column, and the last column has one word in it for every day of the salt year, and the word is *works* or it is blank, and Adrian has read it every night for a month and has never once been able to make it interesting.

The top of the flood was the twenty-eighth. The next tide is the thirty-third, two days off, and it is a small one, and there is a number against the thirty-third in the ordinary column and the word in the last column is the ordinary word.

Which means that on the thirty-third day of this flood forty men of the crew of the Lock of Salt will stand under the near gate of this lock, twice, in whatever weather there is, and say four words at their ordinary volume, in their ordinary order, out of the line and not together, because a docker of about sixty in a felt hat made them promise it in a ledger.

And at about one in the morning on the thirty-third, at one o'clock in the morning, a man with a folding steel rule and a stub of chalk will come down the stair into the second bay of this gallery and put a rule into a crack in this gate and write a number down, and that number will be about three-quarters of an inch wider than it was on the night he woke up on the stones, and the four chalked marks will go with it, and a crack does not step, and the step will fall on a day the lock did **unusual** work, and it will be entered as a fact and not as a fault, and there will be nobody to be angry at except the wall.

Adrian Vale stood in front of a tide table in the lower gallery and read the last column for about a minute, and then Renna Ord came down the stair behind him with a barrow because she was going to do something with the sill, and she read the board over his shoulder, and said:

"There it is, then. The last day of the season. Somebody has to write the word in that box and it is not going to be *not worked*."

"Who writes it," Adrian said.

" whoever holds the chalk that day. It is not a job. It is a Tuesday." She shouldered the barrow. "And when the next one comes, and the one after that, and the one after that, and they are all going to come, and every one of them is going to be in that column, and about four times a year one of them is going to be a day the lock does something it does not normally do — and there will be a number in your book the next morning — then the man who writes the word in that box is the last man in this city who can decide anything, and he decides it with a stick of chalk, and he decides it on a wet morning, and nobody will ever ask him what he decided, and he will not be able to tell you himself by the Thursday."

She went up the stair with the barrow.

And Adrian Vale stood alone in the lower gallery of the Lock of Salt at about a quarter past six on the evening of the thirty-first day of the flood, with four hundred people behind him in a salt store, and a scar in the ground under a chapel that cannot be closed, and a route in a wall that is continued and may not be used, and a form on a wall for a claim that cannot be tested, and a page in a ledger for eleven people who came through without asking, and a debt that is bigger than it was on Tuesday and has no final figure and a review date, and a debt of a second kind that he has been carrying since the twenty-ninth and has not put down: that a man in a good coat wrote a sentence in chalk on a keeper's door on the thirtieth day of the month that said *he will do it*, and that the sentence is true, and that nobody in the world knows what he is going to do it about, and that he has four days.

*Ask the people who maintain the gate. Ask the keeper. Ask the woman who keeps a count.*

*And on Tuesday, on the thirty-third day, on the thirty-third day of a flood month, in the fourth week, a man will stand at the top of a stair in this city with a stick of chalk and the last column of a tide table in front of him, and he will write one word, and it will be the ordinary word, and the ordinary word is that a lock keeps working, and forty men will say four words twice that day in the rain for a hundred and forty acres four miles away that have no guess in them any more, and nobody in this city will be angry at anybody, and that is not because anybody has forgiven anybody.*

*It is because the whole of what I have built in a month has been an argument about who may be angry at whom, and I have got the argument into a book, and into a wall, and into a form with a refusal clause in it, and the argument has worked, and the argument has cost a season of a field and a boy's shoulder and eleven people out of a floor and a woman's reach road and a man in a good coat's chalk, and it has produced a door in a wall that nobody may use and a scar in a place that cannot be closed and a debt with no name on the end of it.*

*And the volume's last act is not going to be mine. It is going to be a man with a stick of chalk, on a wet morning, in this city, writing the ordinary word in a box, because a lock is a thing that has to be worked, and the field is a thing that has to be paid for, and this is the border between those two facts, and it has a gate, and there is nobody standing at it but a docker with a barrow.*
