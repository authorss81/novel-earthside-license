# Chapter 0033 — Nine Elms, a Second Time

There are nine households in Nine Elms and they have been asked twice and have said no twice, and the third time was not a question, it was a piece of paper, and the paper had been signed by Adrian Vale in a good mood on a raft in the rain on the twenty-third day of this flood, and it took a woman of seventy to make him read it back.

---

He could not sleep on the Monday night and did not try, and at about the eleventh hour he was in the loft above the market with Tamsin Quill and a sixteen-year-old girl who had answered a question in a hall of three hundred and ten people five days before and had not stopped answering questions since, and he was putting four things on a table.

"Three days' walk, or one," said Tamsin.

"One. Five miles down the Reach road and a mile and a half on the water side. It is one day there and back if we start at four and do not stop and I do not get sick, and I will get sick."

"You will get sick."

"I will get sick at the second stile and I will be fine by the ditch and I have done that in front of nine people and they did not notice, because the second stile is a long way from anybody." He put his hand on the first of the four things, which was a sheet of flour-merchant's paper folded in four with a signature on the outside of it in his own hand. "I am going to Nine Elms to tell nine households that the thing Hessa Ruun gave me on the twenty-third is a document that can be used against them, and that I have known that for nine days, and that I have not come."

"You have known it for nine days," said Tamsin.

"I have known it for nine days, and I signed the other side of it, and that is the part I would like somebody to look at before I get on the road, because I have been not telling anybody things for about a week and I would like to stop at one and I do not think I am going to be able to."

Kirsty Lund, who was sixteen and had been in the world eight days and who had been asked in a market on the Sunday morning by a chandler whether she was a *player* and had said yes, and who had spent two days explaining to about sixty people in three markets that she did not know, said:

"Do you want to go and knock on their doors, or do you want to go and have a meeting?"

"That is not a small distinction and I do not know how to say that it is a big one."

"It's the difference between the two," said Kirsty Lund. "You can knock on nine doors and every person in nine houses can say no to their own face. In a meeting they have to wait for somebody else to finish. I said that in a hall on the thirtieth and it got written down and I have thought about it every day since and I still think it's true, and if you turn up and have a meeting then it's a meeting, and you can't un-meeting it."

Adrian Vale looked at a sixteen-year-old for about four seconds.

"Then we knock on nine doors," he said, "and if the doors are shut we go to the yard at the well-head, because a yard is where things get said in front of witnesses, and a yard is not a meeting, and the difference between a yard and a meeting is about nine feet and forty years and I have not worked out which way round it goes."

---

They left at four in the morning on the thirty-sixth day of the flood, which is a Tuesday, and they were three people and a dog that belonged to nobody and followed them for a mile, and Tamsin carried a basket and Adrian carried the paper and Kirsty Lund carried a slate and a pencil because she had learned in eight days that a thing written down in a person's own hand is the only thing in this world that anybody has believed, and that she had written four of them down in eight days, and that nobody had asked her to.

The Reach road is four miles of causeway above the flood line and then a mile and a half on the water side, and the water side is where the ditches are, and on the Tuesday morning the ditches were doing the thing they had been doing for three weeks, which is standing level, with no fall in them at all from end to end, and a man from Silt Row was standing in the second ditch at about the sixth hour with a barrow of flour on the bank, and it was the fifth morning he had done it, and he was doing it before anybody was up, and it is entered in the book as a fact because the miller refused to have it entered as a cost.

The four villages' gates could not be promised that week. That is the sentence Wenna Cray said in a market on the Monday and it is in the book on the Tuesday, and it means what it means in the Reach, which is that a household that cannot say when its gate will open does not keep its share of the water, and there are four villages in this Reach that did not say anything at all in the second week of this flood and have not said anything since, and it is not a fine and it is not poverty and it is the only damage anybody in this story has ever been unable to put a number on.

Tamsin did not go past the second ditch without stopping. She stood at the bank for about a minute and a half and looked down a flat brown line of water that should have had a fall in it.

"Line seven," she said.

"Line seven is a boundary."

"Line seven is a boundary and it is in a table and it is mine." She did not look at him. "I am going to see my mother on the way and I have not been in this village for about three years and I am not going to see her today, and if anybody in that yard asks me about it I am going to say what I am going to say, and I would like to know now whether you are going to try to help me with it."

"No."

"Good." She picked the basket up. "Then keep up. I have been walking this road since I was six and you have been walking it since Tuesday, and I am not carrying anything for you."

---

Nine Elms is nine houses and a well-head and a drying frame and about four hundred yards of hedgerow, and it is on the water side of the road and it is eleven households' worth of work with nine houses in it, and it is the only village in this Reach that has said no twice, and both of those refusals are in a book in a salt store in Saltwake with a date on each and are on the same page as the instrument's own refusal, neither of them above the other, in a form a notary invented on the thirty-first day of this flood.

Adrian had not been to Nine Elms. He had been to the meeting-ground at Verrow twice, and to Four Elms once at four in the morning through a child's bedroom, and he had said in a hall on the sixteenth day of this flood that he had a right of no standing whatever in the house of a village that had refused twice, and that had been correct, and that had not helped anybody.

There were about thirty people in the yard at the well-head, and about four times that in the village, on a morning in a flood month, because a woman from another world was on the road with a sixteen-year-old girl and a woman who could speak, and because nine houses had heard a rumour on the Sunday.

Ilda Marsh was sitting on the well-head with a sheet of paper in a waxed case and a stick of sealing wax and about forty sheets more of paper, because Ilda Marsh is seventy and has refused twice and does not refuse things, she *processes* them, and the difference has taken the Reed Reach nine years to learn and has been the making of a fourth line.

"Sit down," she said, in the local language, which Tamsin did not have to translate, because Ilda Marsh addresses Adrian in the local language and has done since the twenty-third day of this flood, which is a thing she has never done to anybody from another world and which she explained once, to herself, out loud, in front of a woman of twenty-four who was taking notes.

"I am not going to sit down, I am going to—"

"You are going to sit down, because you have come five miles to tell nine houses a thing they already know, and you are going to do it standing up in about twenty minutes and it is going to be shorter than you have planned and I have been doing this for fifty years and you have been doing it for thirty-five days." She put the waxed case on her knee. "Say the thing you came to say and then I am going to say a thing, and then there is going to be an argument in this yard, and it will be about the twenty-third day, and there are three of us in this yard who were in that room and I want the other two to hear it before it starts."

---

He said it standing up, in about nine minutes, badly in the local language with Tamsin correcting him four times, and it was this: that a concession is a document; that a Crown scholar reads concessions; that on the twenty-third day the Reed Reach had handed him four lines and he had carried them to a hall in Saltwake and put them there; that the fourth line was a gift and the fourth line was a knife, because it let any village refuse the charge and send the whole of it back to the one man; and that he had signed the paper that made it work, in a good mood, on a raft, and had not read it, and that he had known for nine days what it was and had not come.

And when he had finished, about four people in that yard said that they had heard, and about nine people said nothing at all, and a woman at the back said, in the local language, in a flat and slightly hopeless voice, the two words she had heard a chandler on the upper road say in a market in Saltwake on a Sunday, and Tamsin translated them, because Tamsin translates everything, and the yard went quiet for about two seconds, and Adrian Vale stood absolutely still and could not have moved if somebody had asked him to.

"That is a word she has heard in a market," said Tamsin Quill, in the local language, without turning round. "It is not a word she has a use for and it is not a thing she believes. Do not give it to her and do not take it from her, and do not look at her. Adrian. Sit down. You are doing the face you did on the twenty-fifth and there are thirty people in this yard and about nine of them have never seen it and I have watched nine people in this city get that face wrong about the wrong things in the last fortnight and I would like you to stop making it in front of villages."

He sat down on the well-head, which is Ilda Marsh's well-head, which she allowed.

Ilda Marsh waited until the yard was quiet again, which took about a minute, and then she opened the waxed case.

"Hessa Ruun told you on the twenty-third that a concession is a document and that a village that has refused twice can be brought to a meeting-ground about a bill." She took out the first of about forty sheets. "I have been a water-count's clerk for nineteen years and I have signed a bill before. This is the bill. It went to a meeting-ground nine days ago and it has a date on it and I have read it and I have had it copied forty times, because there are nine houses in this village and I do not trust paper, I trust copies, and I have wanted somebody in Saltwake to have a copy for about a week and nobody has come."

She put four sheets on the well-head and squared them.

"Here is what I have done about it and it took nine days and it is not what you came for and I would like you to understand that I did not do it for you."

---

> **To the meeting-ground of the Reed Reach, from the nine households of Nine Elms, the eleventh day of the new month.**
>
> **We have read the term sheet of the twenty-third. We have read the fourth line and we know whose it is and we know what it is for, and we are not going to pretend this week that we have not understood it.**
>
> **We are exercising it.**
>
> **The nine households of Nine Elms refuse the standing charge. Not the receiving. The charge. Our water is not received and has not been received and will be received on any day the household chooses, and that stands, and it is not for sale and it is not part of a bill.**
>
> **And under the fourth line, the whole of the charge returns to the private debtor.**
>
> **We are aware of what that does. We worked it out in a day and a half and we have told every household in this village what it does before we sent it, which is the only reason we are allowed to send it, and which is why it will hold for about a season and no longer.**

Nobody in that yard said anything for a while. A hen moved. Somewhere down the hedgerow a gate was being lifted and put down again by somebody who did not know there were thirty people forty feet away.

Adrian said, in the local language, and got it wrong, and said it again: "No. Don't. You don't understand what that does."

"Read it back to me," said Ilda Marsh.

"You hand the whole of it to me. The season. Four villages. Not just yours — yours was never on it, you refused the receiving, so the whole of the charge that the Reach would have shared comes back to one man who has no money and a post in a yard and a debt with no figure on it. Which means the Reach is not charged for this. Which means the Reach gets the door for nothing as long as nobody makes me pay for it, and I cannot pay for it, and so the door stays shut, which is what you wanted, and it is the correct thing to want, and I am telling you that you are about to hand the whole of this Reach's season to a stranger to be polite to yourselves."

"Yes," said Ilda Marsh. "That is what it does. I have written it out. Would you like to see my copy of that."

"Stop it—"

"And I would like to say the next part in front of these thirty people," she said, "because you are going to go away on Wednesday and forget this yard by Friday, and I am seventy and I will remember it, and there are nine houses here who will remember it, and there is one of you who will not."

---

Here is what she said, in the local language, slowly, and Tamsin Quill translated it into English afterwards in the yard because Adrian could not follow all of it and did not ask her to stop.

"You have been in this country thirty-five days. For thirty of them you have been the only person in a room who says no, and about two hundred people in this city have got used to it, and I have read about it in four markets and I think it is a very good thing and I am not going to be one of the people who tell you to stop."

"Ma'am—"

"Be quiet, I have not finished." She turned the sheet over. "On the twenty-third day you came into a room on a raft in the rain and Hessa Ruun handed you four lines and I forced a fifth idea into them and you signed it. Did you read it."

"I signed a paper in a good mood on a raft in the rain because I had spent two days in a room where nine people did not want me and somebody had finally handed me something to do, and I did not read it, and I have known for nine days what it was, and I have not come, and I came today."

"That is the truest thing anybody has said in this yard this month and it does not help us at all." She put the sheet down. "Now. Here is the part I have said to myself for nine days and I will say to you once.

"You have been protected from your own arithmetic since the twenty-third. Every village in this Reach that has looked at that paper and thought about it has thought about it in one direction, and the direction is: *what does this cost us*. The right question. I have asked it every day for nine days. And not one of us, in nine days, including two women who can read and a water-count of fifty, has asked the other question, which is *what does this cost the man who signed it*, and I asked it yesterday, on my own, sitting on this well-head, and I have been ashamed for about a day and a half, and the reason I am ashamed is that it took a stranger signing a piece of paper in the rain to make one of us think of the person who signed it.

"So no. We are not going to be protected from your arithmetic. We are going to be the first village in this Reach to put the whole of it back on you, and we are going to say so in a yard, and we are going to send forty copies, and about four of them are going to end up in a book in a building in Aurel with a clerk in it, and by March you will not be able to walk into a room in this Reach and be believed about anything, and that is the correct price of the twenty-third and it is nine days late and it should have been nine days ago and it is not your fault that it is nine days late and it is not ours either."

---

Kirsty Lund had written it all down in her own hand, in a school exercise book, and she had got about two thirds of it right and she was going through it with Tamsin at the well-head while the two older ones stood and did not look at each other.

And then Nesta Gower, who is about fifty and who has kept a count of something in this village for twenty-two years and who had said nothing at all so far, said the thing that Tamsin Quill had been walking toward for five miles.

"You read the table out in a yard in Saltwake," Nesta Gower said, in the local language. "Line seven. I was told about it by a man from Silt Row on the Friday and I have had it in my head for four days, and I have been waiting for somebody from this village to say it, because it is our line and it is in your hand, and I am going to say it now and she is standing four feet away and she is not going to say anything clever.

"You read the table out in a yard in front of three hundred people on the fifteenth day and everybody in four villages heard about it by the Saturday. And then you read it out again on the twenty-third, in a room, when it made you look like the only person in the room who had noticed anything. My name is Nesta Gower. I have lived in this house forty-one years. Line seven is a boundary put in the ground in the year my grandmother was thirty and it is the reason this village's water is where it is, and it is in the table, and it is in your hand, and you are the youngest clerk's daughter that table has ever had and you were twenty-one.

"So that is mine to say and I have said it."

There were about thirty people in that yard and Tamsin Quill did not defend it.

She did not explain it. She did not say that the line had been carried a hundred yards downstream at a cost of four hundred people's houses, or that the unliftable half of the same line was under a chapel floor three hundred yards from a gate, or that she had read the table out twice in a yard in a month and that the second time she had read it out in front of three hundred people and had got the answer, and that she had not told her own village either time.

She said: "It is mine. It has been mine since I was twenty-one and I have never said so in a yard and I am not going to explain it to you in one, and you are right, and you should not let me."

Nesta Gower waited for about four seconds for the rest of it, and when it did not come she said, "Then say it anyway," and Tamsin said, "No," and that was the end of it, and it is entered, because Priya Shah was not here and Tamsin Quill entered it herself in a notebook that is not a public document, and read it back to Adrian later on the road, once, in English, and said, "Do not put that in a book."

---

They were at the well-head for about two hours and forty minutes and Adrian spoke for about a quarter of an hour of it, and at about the ninth hour, while Ilda Marsh was reading out the fourth line for the third time in her own words to nine householders who had heard it twice before, a pale field came up in the air in front of him, in the colour of a paper form, and stood there for about eleven seconds, and two people saw it happen.

Kirsty Lund had been on her feet in under a second, which was faster than anybody from the first six had ever managed.

"Adrian."

"Yes."

"It is up."

> **A PRIVATE DEBTOR MAY BE REPLACED BY A PERSON WHO WILL HOLD THE DEBT.**
>
> **The replacement is entered in the same entry and the debtor's name is not struck out.**

The fourth line was in the grammatical position where a cost goes, and Adrian had eleven of those in a notebook and had learned, over five weeks, to read the shape of a sentence instead of the content of it, and the shape of this one was the shape of *a holder who does not come may be replaced at the next sitting* from the second week, which was stronger than what the ledger said, and of *a debt may be closed* from four days ago, which had been the best one yet.

The thing it was not saying — not out loud, not in the yard, not anywhere — is that a debtor's name is not struck out.

"It says no," he said, out loud, in the local language, and then in English, twice, to a yard of thirty people in which about nine understood the first one and about four understood the second one and one of those four was a sixteen-year-old girl with a school exercise book.

"Adrian," said Kirsty Lund, in English, quite sharply. "You have to say why. You have to say why out loud, because that is the rule you wrote on the wall and you have not been keeping it in this village."

"Fine." He turned round. He had four answers and three of them were bad. He said this one:

"Because it does not take me out of the book, it puts a second man in it. The name is not struck out. So in about four months there will be two people answerable for one season of one field, and one of them will be me, and one of them will be whoever the Reach finds, and they will be able to be angry at each other, and about half this village has spent a month working out that a man in a book is only safe while he is the only one in it. That is the only reason I have a number in a book that I cannot get rid of, and I have spent thirty-five days finding out that it is the only thing holding it together, and a thing in my own hand has just offered to double it, and I am not going to let it in front of nine houses that I put the fifth idea into nine days ago."

Nobody in that yard had any idea what he was talking about except two people, one of whom was sixteen and wrote it down exactly, and Ilda Marsh watched him the entire way through and afterwards said one word to him, in the local language, which was *good*, and which was the first time in a month that anybody had graded him in public.

---

They walked back. It took until about the sixth hour of the evening and Tamsin did carry nothing for him and he got sick at the second stile and was fine by the ditch, and about a mile from Saltwake a woman came out of a gateway and said his name, and it was Petra Oake, thirty-one, of Nine Elms, who had come up the road on the Tuesday because there was a woman from Nine Elms in a yard on the Monday and she did not live in the village and had wanted to hear what was said in it.

She walked the last mile with them. She did not ask about the paper or the fourth line or the forty copies. She waited about two hundred yards, which was the exact distance a person waits when they want to ask something they have asked before and been answered wrongly, and then she said:

"You did not answer me at Verrow."

"No."

"I want to say the thing I have come to say and then I want you to answer it or say plainly that you cannot, and I do not care which, and I have told your notary that I do not care which." She stopped walking. "There is no man in your book who is on my side of this question, and that is not an accusation, because you are not on my side of it either. You are on the side of the thing that is true about it, which is a different thing and is more useful and is not the same.

"So. Petra Oake, of Nine Elms, at the sixth hour of the evening of the eleventh day of the new month, on a road, with two witnesses. **Is there a person on the other side of it.**"

The river came past below them. Nine Elms' ditches were level and had been for three weeks.

"Not is the room open, is there a room," Petra Oake said. "Not is the hole deep. Is there a man in it. Because I have been in three meetings in this Reach since you were in one of them, and every single person in all three has assumed that if that thing is anywhere at all then it is somewhere a man could be fetched out of, and not one of them has asked what happens if there is not."

Adrian Vale stood on a road four miles from a city of eleven hundred people in a flood month, with a woman of twenty-nine beside him who had a basket and a dead night's sleep behind her, and a woman of thirty-one in front of him who had written her question down and folded it into her coat in a room over a boat-yard, and he had had nine days to build a sentence and had built eleven, and he said:

"I cannot answer it, and I am not going to give you a fourth one that sounds like an answer."

"That is the first true thing anybody from the wall has said about it."

"It is not the only one. There is one more and it is worse and it is this, and I am giving it to you and not to a book, because you are the only person in this country who has asked it the same way twice. There is no mark in this world on any person except two, and they are both on people in Saltwake, and neither of them can go through a shape in a wall in a way anybody has any standing in, and the route that could use one of them is continued in a book and may not be used, and the ground that a man could stand on to go out is a scar in a place under a chapel floor that came open once in front of three hundred and ten people and took eleven of them out of it without asking." He stopped. "And I have not answered your question because I do not know whether a room with a man in it is a room somebody has been in twice, and if it is, then the only honest thing anybody in this country can do about it is what I have done for thirty-five days, which is to stand four feet back and say *I do not know* in a yard, and I have just watched nine houses do it better than I do it, and they did it by refusing a bill."

Kirsty Lund wrote all of it down in the school exercise book in the failing light, in her own hand, word for word, including the part about two marks, and asked for both pages, and on the walk back nobody said anything for about a mile, and then Owen Park's name came up — because the six of them had been walking in front and he was not one of the six and could not hear, and he was not there — and Tamsin said, to nobody, in English, "He will be the one who tries."

And she was right, and it was nine days later, and it is in the book.
