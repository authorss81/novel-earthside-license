# Chapter 0050 — The Trial Licence

On the twenty-fifth day of the new month, which was the fiftieth day of the flood and a Tuesday, four people who signed an inspection on the fourth day of this flood walked two hundred steps down a river wall in the wrong weather, looked at a closed socket with a mason's hands on it, and cut a route down from four witnesses to two, and the argument for it is the argument of this whole book: **a working can be made cheaper without being made private.** The fourth column of a wall in a room above a market was filled in for the first time in fifty days, in two hands, and what went into it is the oldest question in this city, and it was not answered and was not asked to be. Four pages about a man under a board behind an ordinary wall were confirmed in a converted salt store, and a woman of twenty-four stopped a Crown assessor from shortening them with a form. Seventeen people from another world were named, six on one page and eleven on another, and the longer of the two lists is what they do not have. A man from another world was offered a small seat with a name on it and a review date for the third time in five weeks, and this time the instrument said **nobody** and he said the sentence himself. And at about the eighth hour of the evening, at the Lock of Salt, with the tide at the top and the join dressed and the number on the wall of the second bay not one sixteenth of an inch different from what it has been for a month, a boy of nineteen who had never once been asked for anything in fifty days wrote a word in an empty box; a lock opened for one named living person and closed; a woman of sixty-one came down two hundred steps and said a number to a boy of nine; and a boy of nine asked whether the next person would be a player, and the man who was the reason did not have an answer yet that does not make the child smaller.

**The four went down at about the seventh hour of the morning of the twenty-fifth day of the new month, and they are the four who signed the inspection of the fourth day of this flood, and the fifth person with them is a woman whose hands are on the stone and whose name is not a signature.**

Tamsin Quill. Nell Bray, in a felt hat. Ovin Sarr, nineteen, out of the day crew. Hettis Roke of the Hearthguard of the outer ward, nineteen years in, who read nineteen shapes aloud in a yard in front of about two hundred and ten people. And Ottoline Vask, who is not one of the four and has not signed an inspection in this city in thirty-four years, who put both hands flat on the dressed face of the seam and kept them there for about nine minutes.

The seam is eighteen inches long and three fingers wide, in the outer wall of the outer channel, under the cap of an old landing. It is closed. It is not a door. It was closed by five witnesses at half past ten on the third day of this flood, and it has carried exactly one person in its life, on the twenty-eighth day of the flood at the sixth hour at the top of the flood, and that use is entered as a breach and is not a precedent.

Vask took her hands off it and said one thing about it, and it is in the book in her own words, and it is thirty-four years of a trade in one line:

"**It has not moved. Not one sixteenth. I have been checking it since the third day of this flood because I did not believe it and I have been wrong, and being wrong about that is the best thing anybody has said to me in a month.**"

And then the four did what four people do in the wrong weather, which is that they turned round and came back up two hundred steps and sat down on the wall at the top, and Ovin Sarr wrote it in a school exercise book that was not his, and the argument took about forty minutes and it is in the book whole.

**What was proposed, and by whom, is this.**

The route has stood since the fourth day of this flood with four named witnesses who sign for themselves and may each refuse, and the refusal of any one of them stops the working. That is the quorum. **The quorum does not change and this instrument does not change it.** The four are the same four, and they are the only people in this city who may alter who opens the route, and they may alter it only in a room, in daylight, with a date on it.

The count required **per opening** goes from four to two.

Nell Bray said the sentence that the rest of it is made of, and it is in the book in her own words, and she said it in about nineteen seconds on a river wall in the wrong weather:

"**Four was a quorum. Two is an opening. A quorum decides whether there is a door and an opening carries one person through it, and if you make an opening cost a quorum then nobody will ever open it, because four people in the wrong weather before every crossing is a price nobody can pay four times, and if you make the quorum cheap then you have a man who opens it on his own, and that is the thing we have been refusing for five weeks in about nine rooms.**
**So: two to open and four to authorise. The four are the only people in this city who may change who the two are. And the two may not both be of the crew, because the crew is the hand and the hand may not hold the door.**"

And a docker of about fifty who cannot read, who is one of the nine named persons who may stop it, stood at the top of those two hundred steps while that was being said and entered that she had been asked about it twice in nine days and had given the same eleven words both times: **two is a shift and four is a quorum and a shift is a thing you turn up for and a quorum is a thing you turn up to decide.**

And then the standing order was read and entered, and it is the whole of the licence:

> **A trial licence of the Lock of Salt, in a seam called the Narrow Mark, in the words of this city.**
>
> **One named living person per opening. Two witnesses, named on the day, at the opening, who are not of the day crew or the flood crew, and who sign for themselves and may each refuse, and the refusal of one of them stops the opening and the person goes back where they were standing.**
>
> **A fresh inspection of the seam, signed by four people, before every opening, in whatever weather there is. Not the tide's weather. The weather that is.**
>
> **A return mark, separately established, separately costed, separately witnessed, before any person goes, and belonging to the person who goes and to nobody else. The mark is not evidence of the route and the route is not evidence of the mark, and this licence does not make a return mark for anybody, and does not make one for a person who asks.**
>
> **Reviewed at the end of the flood season, in daylight, in a room, with a date on this page before the day it is taken.**
>
> **Suspended by any one of nine named persons, or by the receiving community by its own count, or by the four who authorised it. A licence that nobody can stop is not a licence and is not this one.**
>
> **The water of the nine households of Nine Elms is not received, is not received today, and may be withdrawn on any day the households choose. The licence does not survive their refusal and does not require their agreement to survive it.**
>
> **And the ninth page of the instrument of the twenty-second day of the new month stands and is not amended by this: the city is the debtor, no man's name is at the top of the roll, and the holder of the pull is not on the roll of persons who may open it. There is no holder of the pull today, because the term ended at noon yesterday, and a vacant seat is not a fault.**

Ovin Sarr said the thing that is in the book as his, and he is nineteen, and it is nineteen words long:

"**That is the first thing anybody in this city has made cheaper without asking me whether I was cheaper.**"

And Renna Ord said, "You are not cheaper. You are the same price you were," and he said, "I know," and that exchange is in the book, and a boy of nineteen has just discovered the difference between a price and a wage.

**And the fourth column of the roll on the wall of the room at the top of that stair was filled in at about the ninth hour of the morning of the twenty-fifth day of the new month, and the eleven days were up at midnight, and four people had agreed to say nothing about it for eleven days and one of the four was a man from another world who said the eleven was up in nine words and then asked the only question nobody had asked.**

And then somebody said the nineteen stones, because a woman of sixty-one had said on the nineteenth day of the new month, in a yard of about two hundred and forty people, that she writes the shapes and has never once written what she thinks they mean, and that somebody has to be the one who writes that down, and that she is sixty-one and would like to stop.

"So write it down," said Nyla Ferrow. "**Not for him. Write what it is. And the cell beside the name with the line under it, and the date, was filled in at about the half hour past the ninth hour of the morning, in two hands — the first a woman's from another world, the second a Hearthguard's of nineteen years — and the width of the column is entered as a fact for the third time in the same year.

And under the English, in his own hand, in the local language, the same thing again, and the two are in one cell, and the second is a translation and not a second finding.

And Hettis Roke said, at about the fourth hour of the afternoon of the twenty-fifth day of the new month, in the yard under the chapel, in front of about two hundred and forty people, when he was asked why there was a number at the bottom of the cell, in about nine seconds, and it is in the book as his and it is the ninth time in nine years that he has said a sentence about a number in that yard and the first eight times were other people's numbers:

And a woman of sixty-one who cannot read came down the two hundred steps to the lock yard for the third time in nine years at about the fifth hour of the afternoon, and stood in about two hundred and forty people, and Hettis Roke did not look at her, and about nine people noticed, and nobody has asked him why and nobody is going to.

**The converted salt store on the river market held about two hundred and forty people from about the eleventh hour of the morning until about the sixth hour of the evening, and four things happened in it, and the first one was about a man who is under a board.**

The four pages are four pages. They were entered on the thirty-ninth day of this flood by a notary and not by the holder, read in full at a graveside in the ordinary ground behind the ordinary wall on the sixteenth day of the new month by a woman of fifty-eight who did not cry and did not summarise them, and they carry three terms Adrian Vale asked for and got: that the name is Owen Park and is said out loud in both languages with his own reason; that the four pages are read whole; and that the record notes that the injury which killed him is the injury which was not set.

The three terms were entered formally on the twenty-fifth day of the new month, and the entry is four pages long, and it settles the burial term in the only way this city's book knows how: by saying that it is closed, and by saying what it may not be used for.

> **The four pages stand. They are not shortened. They are not summarised in any instrument, in any return, in any quarterly description, or in any letter.**
>
> **Every sentence in them is entered as what a person said. Where the sentence is another person's it is attributed to that person in that person's own words, and the attribution is in the sentence.**

>

And a man in a very good coat tried.

He is the Concord's assessor of strangers and he reads Crown Law and he has been in this city ten weeks, and he has a quarterly description to send to a building on a river in Aurel in a wooden case, and the description has a column four lines high in it. And she read the clause aloud in English in front of about two hundred and forty people, and then in the local language, and then she said the thing that is in the book in her own words:

"**You have got a column four lines high. Write *died here on the thirty-eighth day of the flood; the account is four pages and is in the fourth volume; the four pages may be read by any person who asks for them and a copy is made at a person's cost.* That is the whole of what a column four lines high is for, and it is true, and if a clerk in Aurel cannot read that then a clerk in Aurel does not get a reason.**"

And Aldis Kerrow put the pen down, and wrote the four lines, and read them back, and said one sentence that Vask entered: **that he had known the rule, and that a column four lines high is the only thing that keeps a man alive in a clerk's mind, and that he had been doing it for nineteen years, and that he was going to have to think about the rest of it in a room with nine people in it and not here.**

And a man in a good coat was in that room the whole time and said nothing about it.

**And then the seventeen were named, and the page with what they do not have is longer than the page with what they have, and about ninety people in that yard wanted a stipend out of it, and there is not one, and the reason was given in the language of the room and it is the best argument anybody has made in a yard in five weeks.**

**And then the licence had to say who answers for the next test, and the room reached for the only man in it everybody can be angry at, and he was offered a seat for the third time in five weeks and this time it was not refused in a yard and it was not accepted in a yard, and the instrument says nobody, and he said the sentence himself, and then a pale field came up in the air in front of about nine people and stood there for about nineteen seconds, and it is the seventeenth thing the wall has ever said to him and it is the best of them, and he refused it in under a second and gave a reason that was small and true.**

A seat with a name on it and a review date and a standing in the city, in this city's own book in this city's own words, offered by about nine people in about four minutes, the third time in five weeks, and the room wanted it for the same reason it wanted a Crown advocate on the seventeenth day of the new month, which is that a man with a name on a page can be sent for.

Then Renna Ord was asked, by Nell Bray, in the yard, in front of about two hundred and forty people, in about four words — "**What did you decide**" — fifty days after she wrote a word in a box with a barrow, and she said, in about nine seconds, and it is in the book in her own words:

"**I decided to write it because I did not want to be the only person in the room who had not. That is the whole of it and it took me fifty days to be asked and I have had a bad fifty days.**"

And about nine people in that yard said afterwards that they wanted a better answer, and Vask entered that she was one of them, that a better answer would have been a working and this was not one, and that a woman of fifty-eight who cannot read has just answered a question nobody asked for fifty days, and is owed nothing, and is going to be asked again.

---

**And the lock opened at about the ninth hour of that evening for one named living person, and the two witnesses had signed a fresh inspection at about the seventh hour of the same day, and the four who authorised the route were in the yard and were not at the opening, and that is the reduction working.**

The gate opened because it is a tide gate and the tide was at the top, and it was dressed and it worked, and it made the sound it has made for four hundred years.

The opening that is licensed happened in a seam in a wall of the outer channel, under the cap of an old landing, and it carried **one named person**.

The two witnesses were **Peth Lomas**, a mason, fifty, who is not a crew, and **Hessa Ruun**, water-count of the Reed Reach, about fifty, who came up two hundred steps in the wrong weather and said, "I have never been in a room with a stone in it and I am not going to pretend I understand what I am signing, and I am signing it because the fourth line is mine and I wrote it, and I will not have a door anywhere in this city that I have not read."

The return mark was separately established, costed and witnessed on the twenty-seventh day of this flood, it belongs to the person who went, and it is the only return mark in this world.

The person was **Eli Mercer**, nineteen, of no address, of the second list, who said the word *lore* on a stair and refused on the record to join the six and to speak for them, and who was asked to go eleven times and refused eleven times, and who has not been asked for anything since the forty-third day of the new month except a day's work he turned down in nine seconds.

He was not asked to go on the twenty-fifth day of the new month. He was told, at about the fifth hour of the afternoon in a room above a market, that there was a licence, that it carried one named living person per opening, that anybody in that room could put a name forward, and that his was on the list of names put forward, and that a person may strike their own name at any time.

He struck it, and then unstruck it at about the sixth hour, and gave one reason, and it is in the book in his own words, and it is nineteen words long:

"**I have said no eleven times to people who wanted something. I am going to say yes once to a page.**"

He went at about the ninth hour and he came back at about the ninth hour and eleven minutes through a seam in a wall of the outer channel, and the whole of it took about eleven minutes, and nobody has the least idea what is on the other side of it except that it is a room with no window and no door anybody has found and a light that has no shadow, and about eleven people have been in it, and Eli Mercer is the twelfth person anybody in this city knows of. There is no page in the history of this book that says what is in that room. There was not one before this and there is not one now.

He came back and said one thing, and it is four words and it is in the book:

"**Same room. Taller.**"

Nobody asked him a second question and he did not volunteer one, and it is entered that this was deliberate, and the reason is Renna Ord's and it is eleven words long: **a man who has been asked eleven times is owed a place where he is not a question.**

And the lock closed at about the tenth hour, and the join stayed dressed, and the number on the wall of the second bay is eight and seven-eighths with one ruled line left in the column, and the main breach said nothing at any hour of that day.

---

**And Tamsin Quill wrote the next inspection date in the public ledger at about the eighth hour of the evening of the twenty-fifth day of the new month, in the yard under the lock chapel, on a slate that hangs on a nail where anybody can read it, while a page of the Concord's fourth volume lay out on the sill of the old landing two hundred steps below, held flat against the weather by a piece of ordinary river stone.**

That page is the entry of the fourth day of this flood, and it is the first entry of the Narrow Mark, and it is the one that names Adrian Vale as the first witness of the route, and it is in four hands and a notary's seal, and it is the only page in the history of this book that anybody in this city has ever needed to keep dry.

He asked for it to be carried down. Vask said no. Renna Ord said no. Peth Lomas carried it down two hundred steps in the wrong weather and put a flat stone off the river on it and went back up, and it is entered that he was asked whether the stone was heavy enough and that he said it was the wrong kind of stone for a weight and the right kind for a page, and that nobody has asked him the difference.

A boy of nine was standing at the top of those two hundred steps at about the eighth hour of the evening of the twenty-fifth day of the new month with a woman of sixty-one who lives on the upper road above the salt works, and Sabra Vint has come down those steps three times in nine years and this was the third.

She said a number to him.

"**Nineteen**," said Sabra Vint.

And Tam Crane, who is nine, and who is the son of a widow who owns the building the loft is the top of, and who followed a man at six feet from the fifth day of this flood until that man was buried on the sixteenth day of the new month in the ground behind the ordinary wall, looked at her and said:

"What is nineteen?"

And Sabra Vint said: "Stones," and stopped, and then said, in about nine seconds, and it is in the book in her own words, and it is the third time in nine years she has said a thing about her own paper to a person who did not ask:

"There are nineteen stones on the upper road and I write down the shapes on them every year and I have done it for nine years and I am sixty-one and I would like to stop writing the shapes down. And nobody living can read what is cut on them, not me, and not the mason, and not the man from another world. And somebody has got to be the one who writes down what they think it means, and I have not done that for nine years, and that is the only thing I have done wrong in nine years, and today it went on a wall in a room above a market in two hands and neither of them is mine."

And the boy said, "Are they magic?"

And Sabra Vint said, "**I do not know, and I have had nine years to find out and I did not ask, and that is a different thing from not knowing, and it is the only thing I am putting on that wall.**"

And about nine people on that stair heard the whole of it, and a docker of fifty-eight was one of them, and Renna Ord has entered it, and has entered no reason.

And then the boy asked the question, and he asked it to the man, because the man was on the step.

"Tam," he said. "Are the next ones going to be players?"

---

Adrian Vale looked down the two hundred steps at the dressed gate and out past the outer wall at the water, which was black and moving and had been moving under this city for longer than this city has had a name for the thing that moves it, and the lamp on the third step of the lower gallery was lit and nobody had gone down to it.

He did not find an answer that did not make the child smaller.

That is the entry, and it is in the fourth volume, in his own hand, at about the eighth hour of the evening of the twenty-fifth day of the new month: **that he was asked a question by a boy of nine and that he did not have an answer and that he is going to go and find one, and that the answer is not going to be the word.**

And then he said, out loud, on a stair above a lock, in the local language, to about nine people, the thing he actually had, which was this:

"Your friend is asking me whether a person who comes through that wall is a special kind of person. And I want to tell you the true answer to that, because I have been in this city fifty days and I have said the fair one about nine times.

"**No. Nobody who comes through that wall is a special kind of person. They are people, and they are in trouble, and about half of them are better at this than I am and all of them are more frightened than they are saying.**

"And the second half of it, which is the half I have not got: they come through a road that four people agreed to keep open this morning, in the wrong weather, and it opens for one at a time, and the man who opens it has the only mark in this world and he has said no eleven times to people who wanted something from him and he said yes once to a page.

"So he is not the next one of a kind. **He is a man who was asked, in a room, and said yes, and that is the whole of what the wall is.**"

And then the boy said, "But you were the first one."

And Adrian Vale said, "**I was not. I was the first one anybody could see.**"

And that is the last thing entered about him on the twenty-fifth day of the new month, in his own hand, in a book in a room above a market. And there is a slate on a nail in the yard under the lock chapel with a date on it that is the end of the flood season, and a page on a sill two hundred steps down with a stone on it, and a copy of the licence that anybody may come and read, including the list of what the seventeen do not have, which is the longer list, and which is a term and not a favour.

And the main breach is closed and dressed. The ground under the lock chapel is a black mark in a place, watched five times a day by a form with five items on a nail, and is not repaired. The black line in the chapel stone said nothing on that day, and the black line in a man's palm is forty-nine days old and has not moved one sixteenth of an inch. Nineteen stones are on a wall above a market with nobody's name on them and the meaning of them unwritten. A man of nineteen years has eight graves behind an ordinary wall, has said one true sentence about them in nine years, and has not been asked and has not refused. A woman of thirty-one from Nine Elms asked twice whether there is a person on the other side of that floor and was answered once in the worst possible way, and this evening a man of nineteen walked into a room with no window and came out of it eleven minutes later and said four words, and nobody wrote down what it means.

And the forty men still go under the near gate twice a day, at the ordinary volume, out of the line and not together, because that is the ordinary work of this lock and it is what the bottom field pays for, and the working on the tide is once a month now that the join is shut, and the cost column was entered for the seventh time on the twenty-fourth day of the new month before the tide turned and not after, and it has no figure on the end of it, and a woman in a village four miles away has to decide in March how much of her life to put in the ground. The date is on the page. The page is the wall. The wall is not going to save anybody, and it is going to be there in the morning.
