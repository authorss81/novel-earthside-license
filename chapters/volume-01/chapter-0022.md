# Chapter 0022 — The Second Set of Hands

They walked it, and they punted it, and the walking was six miles of the Reach road and the punting was four hours behind Osten Fenn of Long Water, who is twenty-two and holds the second gate and who had never once in his life been asked by anybody to do anything and who was asked, on the Monday evening, by Tamsin, in nine words, in the local language, standing on his own gate-step.

He did not say yes. He said, "Why me," and Tamsin said, "Because you have been putting thatch on the second ditch for nine days on your own time and you have told your father and your father has told a woman at Silt Row, and the woman at Silt Row is the reason we have a road instead of a rumour."

He thought about it for about as long as it takes to fill a pipe.

"Two of you," he said. "The boat takes two and a pole, and I pole. And if anybody asks, you are reed cutters looking at a water-count, because a man with a notebook in his coat and a woman off this road at Verrow is a thing that gets repeated, and this month there is already a man in this city telling people that the holder of the pull has bought a boat."

---

Owen Park learned that he was going on the second stile, at about the second hour of the walk, in the cold, on the morning of the twenty-second day of the flood, which was a Tuesday, and they slept the night of the twenty-second on the bank at Verrow in four ground beds under an awning belonging to a reed-cutter's wife, because the letter had asked for the twenty-third and the letter was a document and a document outranks a man's convenience.

Adrian had been carrying it for eleven hours. He had carried it up two flights of stairs at about ten o'clock the previous night and had stood in a doorway and had said, "Owen. The Reach has asked for a person to come and stand in a room on Wednesday. I have to say to you that I could send you and not tell you, because the letter says a person of my own choosing who has not been asked, and I have been thinking about that for eleven hours and I have decided that I am not going to do it, and that the deciding took me eleven hours, and that I would like it noted that it took me eleven hours and not eleven seconds."

Owen had been sitting on the end of a cot with a bootlace in his hands and had not looked up.

"Say the rest of it."

"The rest of it is that I do not know who it should be, and that I have been waiting since last night for the answer to arrive and it has not, and that when it did not arrive I understood that I have been waiting for it to be you, and that I would have sent you."

Owen put the bootlace down.

"Ask me," he said.

"What?"

"You have not asked me anything. You have told me about a letter and told me about eleven hours and told me what you were not going to do, and none of that is a question, and I have been in this country twenty-two days and I have never once been asked a question by anybody except a woman by the wharf and she asked me with tweezers in my hand." He stood up and put his coat on. "Ask me properly, in the words you would use if I said no, so that I know what saying no costs."

Adrian asked him properly.

Owen said yes in about a second, and then said the other thing, standing in the doorway with his coat half on, and it is the sentence he kept.

"The letter says a person who has not been asked and does not know he is coming. And the reason they wrote it that way is that they have been reading about us, and they think there is a man in this city who sends people. And I have been in this country twenty-two days and I have been *looking* for that man, and I have not found him, and I want to tell you something about that before we get on a boat." He pulled the coat on the wrong arm and did not notice. "In my — in the city I come from, there is a word for a person who volunteers for things. It is not a compliment. It means a man who cannot sit still and cannot be given a job, so he makes himself one, and every organisation that ever existed has got about nine of them and two of the nine are running the place and the other seven are the reason it burned down. And I have been that man my whole life. I am not proud of it and I have not stopped. And the reason I have been standing in a yard at six in the morning saying four words in a language I cannot speak for nine days is that it is the *only* thing I have ever found that I could not make mine, and I have been terrified every single morning that somebody competent was going to come along and do it properly and thank me for holding the place until they arrived."

Tamsin said, from the stair, in the local language, "Then you will want to know what happened to the two men who were in the yard at seven o'clock on Saturday."

"Which two men."

"The man who said the words first and got it wrong, and the keeper's boy, who is nine and did it because his mother has decided he may." She came down two steps. "Neither of them was replaced. There is a mason's name on a board in that yard now, and a docker's name, and Ovin Sarr, and a market woman who has not once done it at six but has done it four times at noon, and it says at the top *this is not a rota*, and it means it, and it will still mean it in March when you are bored. So you will not be thanked, and you will not be relieved, and in about four days somebody in that yard will learn it and you will not be the best at it, and the arch will not care at all."

"Good," said Owen Park.

They walked at six miles an hour for three hours and Adrian spent the first hour and a half being the least useful man in the party, and the second hour understanding why the first hour had gone like that.

Tamsin was not being useful. Tamsin was *speaking*, and she had been speaking the entire length of the Reach road in the local language at a rate Adrian had not heard her manage in twenty-two days, and the effect of it was that by the second mile two men cutting hedge had stopped and let her pass and then followed her for a quarter of a mile, and by the fourth mile a woman had come out of a gate with a bucket and had given Osten a drink without being asked, and by the fifth mile somebody had put a boy on a horse to ride ahead to Verrow to say that the anchorwright was on the road with two strangers.

"You have got nine hours' use out of her and she has not even stopped for the water," Adrian said.

"She has stopped for the water," said Tamsin. "I have been stopping for the water. What you are watching is a woman going along a road she knows, at the correct speed, being recognised at every gate, in the correct order, on the correct day." She went over a stile without slowing. "Do you know what I have actually been doing, in your terms, since the ninth morning?"

"Keeping a public bridge register."

"I have been keeping a *count of who speaks to me and in what order*, and carrying it in my head down a road, and deciding before I reach a gate whether I need it there. That is the whole of my trade. It is not reading a join and it is not law. It is knowing which nine people out of a village are the nine you have to reach before the tenth one is useful." She looked sideways at him. "And I have been doing it to the wall. Every day for three weeks. That is what you have been watching me do, and you thought it was translation, and it has never once been translation, and the Reach does not think of me as a woman who can speak for the wall. The Reach thinks of me as a woman from Saltwake who keeps a register and who has a name on a document from when I was twenty-one, and about half of them know exactly what that document is and have a very good idea what is on it."

"You read the eleven-line table out in a yard," Adrian said.

"Yes, and about forty people in this Reach now know that my name is on line seven, and there is a version of me that is the anchorwright who proposed taking the Reed margin in the year I was twenty-one, and it went round in a week." She said it without any weight at all, which was the most frightening way she had said anything. "It has not damaged me yet. It will damage something in about a year. Do not apologise for it; it is mine and I brought it down the road in my own hand."

---

They got to Verrow at about the third hour of the afternoon, and the meeting-ground is a raft of cut reed and boards moored in the middle of a channel with a bank behind it, and there were about three hundred people on that bank.

None of them were there to see Adrian.

That was the first fact, and he understood it about ninety seconds in, and it was the single most disorienting hour of his life in Veyra. Tamsin was let through a gap in the bank in about four seconds. Osten was let through by name, twice, by two different people, in the same tone. Adrian was let through last, behind all of them, in a slow line, by a girl of about fifteen who looked at his boots and then at his face and then at his hands, and who said, in the local language, "Are you him," and when he said yes she said, "You are fourth," and moved along the bank four feet and let four people past him before she let anyone else past at all.

He was put at the front. Not at the table — on a board at the front, on the raft, with his boots in the water, opposite nine men and eleven women who were sitting on a low platform under a reed awning, and the bank was behind him, three hundred people, and a hundred and forty of them had never been closer than a quarter of a mile to a wall.

The first hour of that meeting was about a footpath.

He understood it in about ten minutes and then had to stop listening to the substance of it, because it was genuinely more consequential than anything he had been permitted to say in a room in twenty-two days. A gate at Four Elms was being re-set on a date. Three households at Silt Row had asked whether the extra flour on the banks of the second ditch was going to be a thing this year or a thing that had happened. The meeting-ground wanted to know whether the holder of the pull's post — the man who could not leave a yard — was going to be in the yard on the twenty-eighth, because the twenty-eighth was a tide and the meeting-ground wanted somebody from Saltwake standing in this city who could be asked a question, and a person who could not leave was a person who could be found.

And Adrian discovered, in that hour, that the reason the Commons had not wanted *him* was not a courtesy and not a slight.

It was that he had asked for something in a room on every single day for twenty-two days.

---

Owen Park had been put on a board at the front beside him, and Owen had not said one word for seventy minutes, and at about the seventy-first minute a woman of about fifty called **Hessa Ruun** stood up off the low platform — she is the water-count of the Reach, which is the closest thing the wetland has to a notary and nothing like one — and said, in the local language:

"Twenty-two days this city has had a man in a seat and six people out of a wall, and the meeting-ground has been sent two things. The first was a seat with a cost column. The second was a question." She looked at Owen, not at Adrian. "We have read about the man who went out to the bottom field four mornings in a week with no tools and told nobody. The meeting-ground is aware that he is one of the six. The meeting-ground is also aware that he is the only one of the six who has stood in a room in this city and not asked for a thing."

"He asked for a thing in the second room," said Adrian. "On the Saturday. He asked whether there was a man in a join and he was told to look at the words instead, and he did, and the words turned out to be better than the man in a join."

"Yes," said Hessa Ruun. "And that is why he is wanted, and it is not because he is clever. It is because he is *portable*." She said the word the way a woman says a price. "A man who has asked for nothing can be sent. And this meeting-ground has been sending people to meetings for thirty years, and I am the one who does the sending, and I have learned one thing, and it is this: the man I can send is never the man who wants to be sent. He is the man who has not spent anything yet. Every man in this village who has a wife or a boat or a gate has spent something, and a spent man cannot be sent, because he will be at home on the day. This one has been here three weeks and he has a room with a lease he has not signed and a landlord's condition he has not met, and he is holding a stone every morning in the rain for free, and if I sent him to a room in Aurel next week he would come, and I could not sleep, and neither could he."

The bank behind Adrian made the noise three hundred people make when they have found out that the thing they came for is not the thing.

Then the woman from Nine Elms stood up, and Adrian felt Tamsin go completely still beside him the way she had gone still on a walk-round in the rain in the third week.

She was seventy and she had wet reed-grass in the hem of her coat, and she had walked eleven miles to a raft to say four sentences, and she had said them at a hearing ten days earlier and they had cost this meeting its instrument.

"I have read the thing you are all about to say," said Ilda Marsh, in the local language, "because Wenna Cray read it out at Silt Row on Monday night, and I do not blame the girl, and I would like it entered that I did not blame the girl. And I am going to say the thing that all of you have not said, and then I am going to sit down, and you may count me." She did not look at Adrian. "You want the season to be a charge on the instrument. A standing cost. Paid by everybody who uses it. Everybody in this Reach pays it whether or not the door has ever opened, and the door has never opened, and the field went under in the first week and has been going under ever since."

She put her hand flat on the reed awning pole.

"Nine Elms has refused that water twice, in front of a notary, in words, as a thing that stands. And there is a difference between refusing a road and refusing to pay for one, and every person in this village knows the difference, and I am the only one who has to live inside it." She looked up. "If the season becomes a charge on the thing in the wall, then our refusal stops being a *no* and becomes a *non-payment*, and a non-payment is a debt, and a debt is a thing this Reach can be brought to the meeting-ground about, and I have watched a household in this wetland lose a gate over a debt of eleven elms in the year before the flood. You have not made an instrument, Mr Vale. You have made a bill, and you have put a village's name at the bottom of it, and you did it by accident, on a road, through a woman who speaks for you."

Nobody spoke.

"That is the third time somebody has told me this month that I have made a thing by accident," Adrian said, to the platform, in English, and then, because he had learned to do it and had not wanted to, again in the local language, badly. "I am going to hold that for later."

"You will hold it for later and it will still be true in March," said Tamsin, in English, level, from the board beside him, and the bank laughed, and it was not a cruel laugh, it was the laugh of a hundred and forty people who had been in a room with two of them before.

---

The count took forty minutes and it was a count and not a vote, and Adrian watched it happen and understood, about twenty minutes in, that he was watching a thing that had been invented to survive a famine and was now being used to decide a question none of the people using it had been born for.

Hessa Ruun read the demand out at the end, in four lines, and it was better than anything Adrian had brought with him.

> **First: the season of the bottom field, and the water of four villages, shall be a standing charge upon the instrument entered in Saltwake's book, and not a debt upon a man. It shall be paid in kind and in labour and counted by this Reach, and the count shall be read into Saltwake's book by a man this Reach chooses.**
>
> **Second: the charge continues while the instrument stands, and does not stop when the instrument is not used.**
>
> **Third: the charge may be paid in gate-labour, reed-cutting, ditching, and flood shift, at a rate set by this meeting-ground and not by the holder.**
>
> **Fourth: any village may refuse the charge. On the refusal of a village, the whole of the charge returns to the private debtor, and to no one else, until the village withdraws its refusal.**

There was an enormous amount of noise from the bank after the fourth line, and the noise was not approval.

"That last one is mine," said Ilda Marsh, from the platform, without any particular triumph. "It took nine days and I would have got a different one. Make no mistake about who wrote it. If you take the first three and leave out the fourth, I will speak against the whole at Saltwake on Thursday."

"Take it," said Hessa Ruun, to the platform, not to Adrian. "It is in."

And then Hessa Ruun turned round and looked at the man on the board in the wet boots, and Adrian understood that he had been on the raft for ninety minutes and had not been asked a single question, and that this was going to be the subject of his week.

---

Hessa Ruun asked it in the last hour, with about a hundred people still on the bank in the dark behind him, and she asked it in the local language first, which she had not done all afternoon, and then in English, and she was a better speaker of the second than Adrian had expected.

"Mr Vale. You have come to find out what a receiving community wants." She put her hands together. "It wants the same thing it has wanted for nine hundred years. It wants the whole of the cost to be in the open and the whole of the saying to be in the open, and it wants the thing that is dangerous to be somebody else's *job* and not somebody's *idea*, and it wants to be able to say no without being turned into a debtor. That is all. That is the demand, and the four lines are the demand, and you may take them to your city."

"I cannot take them as they stand," Adrian said, in English, and got the local language wrong twice and was corrected twice by Tamsin without either of them stopping. "Line two. If the charge continues while the instrument stands and the instrument stands while nobody may use it, then a receiving community pays a permanent tax for a door that has never opened, and in four years a household in this Reach will say to a child *why do we pay for the wall* and nobody will be able to answer."

"You are right," said Hessa Ruun, "and that is the price of what we have asked, and we have priced it, and it is line two and not line one, and you may tell the meeting-ground that the man from the wall came to this raft to argue with us about the cost of our own demand and that he argued well, and that we held the line anyway." She sat down and stood up again. "Now. The warning, and I am going to give it to you because you have been honest for ninety minutes and honesty is cheap here and I would like to be in your debt for once."

"Go on."

"You have just made, in a document that will be copied, a category of person who owes money to a piece of stone." She said it simply. "Nine Elms has said *no* twice about a road. As of today, Nine Elms can be brought to this meeting-ground about a *bill*. And there is a man in your city — I know his name, everybody in this Reach knows his name, he has been in that house for seven weeks and asks people questions and gives answers away — who reads concessions. He reads them for a living and he is a Crown scholar and he is not a clerk, and a Crown scholar who reads a concession can put it in front of a court as though it were a covenant, and a covenant is a thing a village signs." She looked at him. "You have given that man a document. In about a month he will find a way to use your own gift against the only village in this Reach that has ever refused you anything, and he will do it in a room, and he will be *correct*."

Adrian wrote it down on his knee with a pencil stub, and then, after a moment, crossed out the words *Crown scholar* and wrote *a man in a good coat who has not been in a room for ninety minutes*, and Ilda Marsh read it upside down from four feet away and made a sound that was almost a laugh.

---

They left the raft at the fourth hour of the evening and Osten poled them back up the channel, and the three hundred people on the bank had gone to make their own way home, and it was cold enough that the three of them were the only people on the water.

Owen Park had said perhaps forty words in ninety minutes, which for Owen Park was a kind of shouting, and he had used all of them to ask questions about gate-labour, and the last one, asked of Hessa Ruun as she stood on the raft in the dark, was: "What happens to a man in your village who cannot pay a charge because the charge is on a road he refused?"

"He is not in the village," she said. "That is the answer, and I have given it away, and I have given it away because a man from a wall asked it and because it is a good question and because I am sixty-one and I will not need the answer." She got onto the bank. "Go home. Both of you. Do not stand on a raft in the cold and think you are doing something."

---

Owen was quiet for three hours, and Tamsin poled, and Adrian sat in the bottom of the boat with a blanket over his knees that Osten had put there without asking, and did the arithmetic he could not stop doing.

The mark he had given into the maintenance seam on the fourth morning of the flood could, conceivably, in a world where anybody knew anything, have been a road home for six people.

It was in a wall. In the outer channel, eighteen inches long and three fingers wide, under the cap of an old landing at the foot of a two-hundred-step walk-round with a rail that was half rotten. It was in this city, in this country, three hundred yards from where they were sitting in a boat.

He had put it there in front of nine people. He could, at that moment, conceivably have taken it back; nobody present could have stopped him and nobody could have proved it afterward. He had written that down on a page, in a market, in the third week, and Vail had entered it.

And it was *in the wall*, and there was no procedure in nine hundred years of this city's record for taking a thing back out of a wall, and a mason with thirty-four years in Saltwake had looked at a joint that had been opened and closed two hundred years ago and said that closing it had been done well.

*It is in a wall, and it is not a road, and I am going to be asked about it in a hall, and the honest answer is that there is nobody left alive who knows.*

And then the other thing, which was worse, and which he wrote down at the second hour of the punting with his hands cold:

*And in about a fortnight I am going to have to stand in a room and answer for a season of a field, and the only thing I have ever had that anybody could call a road is in a stone in this city, and the woman in that city has just handed me a piece of paper that makes a village liable for the cost of a door, and a man in a good coat will be able to use it, and I built it for him out of good manners.*

They came up at Long Water at the third hour and got back into Saltwake at about eleven, on the second night, on the twenty-third day, and Owen Park walked the whole of the Reach road and the whole of the market in a silence that nobody broke, and at the bottom of the second stair, with a lamp in his hand, he turned round and said:

"Adrian. I want to ask you for a thing and I want to do it properly this time, so I am going to tell you what it costs, and then I am going to ask."

"Go on."

"Every morning for ten days I have stood at the end of the bottom field in the dark and there has been a light down under the ditch at the third stake. Not a reflection. It is the shape of a thing and it stands on its edge and it is about the size of my hand and it walks about four feet when I walk four feet, and it is there at six and it is gone at seven, and it was there on the first morning and it was there this morning, and it is in the Concord's book marked *unverified* because a man in this city wrote *unverified* next to it, and he was right to." Owen's hand was on the stair rail. "I have been in this country three weeks and I have not asked to go anywhere. I have been getting people to go for me. I worked that out on the boat and I have not slept about it since and I am telling you now because I said I would do things properly and this is the proper version."

"Then ask."

"Come and stand in that field with me. Four in the morning, Sunday, at the end of the third ditch, and stand on the stake where I stand, and do not bring a lamp, because it goes out when I bring one, and tell me afterwards whether you can see anything." He was not asking for help and he was not asking for a decision. "That is all. That is the whole thing and it is a small thing and it will take twenty minutes of your post."

Adrian stood in a lamp-lit stairwell in a market in a city that was not his and understood that the request was not small, and that he was not going to be able to say why, and that the reason was going to have to be worked out before Sunday, and that Owen Park had just handed it to him as a favour.

"Nobody has asked me that before," he said.

"No," said Owen Park. "That is why I have given you a whole day to think about it instead of asking you at a quarter to six in the rain."

---

At eleven at night, in the loft, with a headache behind the left eye that he had earned, Adrian wrote the last page of the twenty-third day, in the flat hand, and it is the page he would still have in a year.

*The twenty-third day. Nothing gained, and two things acquired, and they are not powers.*

*I have a term sheet with four lines on it and the fourth line is better than anything I have written, and it was written by a seventy-year-old woman from a village that has refused me twice, and she got it out of nine days of saying no to her own village.*

*I have been warned, in a document that is going to be copied, that the concession I have just been handed will be used in a month by a man who reads concessions, against the only village in this Reach that has ever refused me anything, and that he will be correct when he does it, and that I built it.*

*The Reach does not want a door. The Reach wants the whole of the cost in the open, the whole of the saying in the open, the dangerous thing to be a job and not an idea, and a no that does not turn into a bill. That is the demand. I have been carrying a design in my head for three weeks and a woman in a boat has described it to me in nine words and one of them is the word I have been using wrong the entire time, which is *job*.*

*And a man who has asked for nothing in a room has asked me for one thing, and it is twenty minutes in a wet field at four in the morning, and he gave me a whole day to refuse him in, and that is the first courtesy anybody has shown me in this world that I have not had to earn by being useful.*

*Which is the sentence I am going to have to do something about, and the something is: he has been getting other people to go instead of asking to go, for three weeks, and I have watched him do it, and I did not stop him, because it worked, and because I am the man who finds out that somebody has not turned up, and I did not find this one out until he told me himself on a step in the dark.*

*Ask the people who maintain the gate. Ask the keeper. Ask the woman who keeps a count.*

*And write down, tonight, before I do anything clever about it: a man in a good coat has a date I do not know. A boy of nine told me on Monday. If I use that date to find out who he is, I am doing the only thing in this world I have never done, and I will not be able to tell afterwards whether I did it because a man in a good coat has been editing the wall, or because a man in a good coat is inconvenient.*
