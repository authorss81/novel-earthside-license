# Chapter 0041 — The Board Comes Off

On the sixteenth day of the new month, which was the forty-first day of the flood and a Sunday, a rule that made a piece of ground in a city of eleven hundred people answer to one man was taken off a board in daylight by the woman who had put it there, with a cloth and a bucket and about a hundred and forty people standing in a yard watching, and in the same yard, six hours later, a man who has been the answer for a floor since the twentieth day of the flood was readmitted to the position for a term that ends on the forty-ninth, and the afternoon belonged to a man of thirty-eight days in this world who is under a board in the ordinary ground behind the ordinary wall, in a grave that is the twelfth in that ground, and whose name was said out loud in two languages by two men, one of whom cut the second language upside down because he is not a mason.

---

The ground behind the ordinary wall is not a burial ground. It is the ground the city puts things in — a boundary stone that came out of a wall in the time of somebody's grandmother, a fence post, the foot of a gate that went into a river in the year before the flood — and it has eleven graves in it because eleven people of this city have been dead in one another's lifetimes and have not been rich enough to be buried anywhere else, and eight of the eleven have no name stone, and one of the eight is Hettis Roke's grandfather, who held the gate at this lock for thirty years and is under a lump of turned earth with nothing on it but a piece of the gate he used to stand in.

There are twelve graves in it now, and the ground was frozen about a hand's depth on the Sunday morning, hard at the top and soft underneath, the way ground goes in a flood month when the water has gone out of it and the cold comes back for a day.

Nobody in Saltwake knows how to cut a name that is not in any language they speak. About four people know how to write one — a woman of twenty-four with a slate has been doing it in a market for sixteen days and has got quite good — and what they cannot do is cut.

Ottoline Vask was in the ground at about the ninth hour of the morning with a lump hammer and a wedge and a boy of nineteen holding a bucket, and she said the thing she said at the end of a hundred of these, which is that a name on a stone is a mason's job and a mason does not do it in a frost and that a person who has not been asked for a thing should not be asked for it on a Sunday, and Adrian Vale asked her, and she cut the slab.

The slab is a flat one out of the waste pile at the yard, about two feet by a foot, dressed on the face only, and the face had a mason's chisel mark on it from something that came out of a wall in the first week of the flood. It was set at the head of the grave on a bed of gravel so that it stands about four inches proud and it will lean by about the second winter, and nobody has tried to stop it and Vask has entered, in her own book and not in anybody else's, that she has left it so that it can.

---

The name is cut twice, and the two lines are in different hands, and the second one is upside down relative to the first.

The first is **OWEN PARK**, cut by Peth Lomas, mason, of Saltwake, fifty years old, who cut a true sentence out of a person's own mouth into the side of a building in the seventh week of this flood and put his own name under it, and who said when he was asked to cut this that he would do it because he could not do anything else with his hands.

The second is **OWEN PARK** again, in the second language, cut by Hettis Roke, Hearthguard of the outer ward, nineteen years in, with a chisel he did not know how to hold, and it is upside down, and it stays upside down, and Roke looked at it for a while and said that it was not going to be done again and that if anybody in that ground had a problem with a name being the wrong way round then they could come and say so to him and not to the man under it.

Nobody in that ground said so to him.

---

There were about a hundred and forty people in that ground at about the second hour of the afternoon and there is no wall round it and no gate and no names on the gate, because the ordinary wall is the wall of the salt works and the ground is behind it and anybody in the city can walk into it, and nobody has ever had a reason to, and about ninety people came because a woman of sixty-one was there and about forty came because they were there on the fortieth and had not gone home, and about nine came because they had been asked by nobody and are that kind of person in a city of eleven hundred.

Hesper Saye read the four pages.

That is the whole of what happened and it is the reason a woman of fifty-eight who has never in thirty-four years read anything she wrote about a living man to a dead one stood at the head of a grave in the cold and read four pages of a dead man's own account of his own death out loud in the language of the room, in full, and did not summarise it, and did not stop at any part of it, and did not cry, and the reason she did not cry is not in the book and is her own and she has not given it to anybody.

It took about nineteen minutes. About eleven people in that ground did not know what to do with their hands for the whole of it, because a reading is not a ceremony and there is nothing to do at a ceremony, and a woman of about sixty in a felt hat worked out about halfway through that the thing to do with your hands in that situation is to hold the other end of the coffin-board, and about nine people were holding a coffin-board at the end of it, and the carpenter's boy, who is seventeen, has entered in his own words that nobody told him what to do and that he did that because the man at the other end of it looked like he needed somebody to.

The four pages are the entry he dictated on the thirty-eighth day of the flood in a room by the salt wharf in about eleven sentences at about the fourth hour of the afternoon, and it contains the light, and the road, and the sentence on a board, and the account of himself, and it contains the sentence about the man in a good coat who asked for *the* true reason and was given somebody's, and it contains the reason for the name, which is that he did not have a false one and was not going to be given a good one.

It also contains, twice, in the middle of a paragraph about a wall in a city four hundred yards from a lock in another world, the words *unclosed*, which is a word the reader of it has been saying out loud since the fourteenth day of the flood in porches and doorways and once in a yard, and which nobody in that ground had ever heard her attach to anything except a wound, and about four people in that ground understood that she had said it on purpose and about ninety did not.

And when she came to the part about the shoulder — which is the eleventh item of a six-item list of rescue terms read aloud in a yard in the rain on the evening of the thirty-seventh day, and which is the reason the last clause of the account says that the record of the cause of this man's death may not be shortened into the crossing alone, and that any shorter account of the thirty-eighth day that omits it is entered as an error and read back — Hesper Saye stopped for about two seconds, and then read it, and her voice did not change, and Roke was standing eight feet away with his waxed slate under his arm and did not write anything down at all for those two seconds or after them.

> **— and there is one more thing and it is the one I would want in a book and I am going to say it plainly because I have about an hour and I would like somebody to have to write it down.**
>
> **A man is a scar in a person.**

It is in the account in those words and it is a sentence a man of thirty-eight days in this world put together on a table in a room by a salt wharf, and a Hearthguard of nineteen years said the other half of it out loud at a grave, to about a hundred and forty people, in the local language, and then gave the reason, because a sentence with no reason in it does not survive anybody.

> **A line in a place can be mended with work and money, and a mason has mended one and it took her two years. A mark in a person cannot, because nobody steps over a hand.**

That is Ottoline Vask's, and she said it from about eleven feet away with her hands in her sleeves, and she was not at the grave for the reading and came to the edge of it about halfway through and stayed, and Roke has entered that she came about halfway through and stayed and has entered no reason for it.

---

Two people asked Hesper Saye afterward whether she was all right.

She is entered as having said, to a man of about fifty with a barrow, *I have been all right since the eighth hour of the morning of the thirty-eighth and I will be all right on the Wednesday.* And the man with the barrow entered it, and the reason he entered it is that nobody was going to enter it and there were a hundred and forty people standing about nine feet from a woman who had read four pages without stopping.

She has told four hundred men a thing about a shoulder or a scalp and has been wrong about ninety of them. That is the same figure in every room it is said in and it is not a rounded figure and it is hers.

---

The board is on the door of the lock chapel, it has been on that door for four hundred years, and it is washed every Sunday morning by the woman who keeps the chapel. On the Sunday of the forty-first day of the flood about a hundred and forty people stood in the yard under the arch and watched her wash it.

> **A thing that is taken off a board in a yard with nobody in it is taken off in a market by Thursday.**

She chose the seventh hour because that is the only hour in a winter week when the sun is on the wall opposite the board and this yard is in daylight. Four separate people asked her beforehand whether she was going to do it in front of anybody. She said yes to all four and gave all four the same reason.

The bucket was the one from the dig. The cloth was new.

On one side of the board, in chalk, four inches high, in a square hand that has been on that board for four hundred years and is not Nyla Ferrow's and never was, are the keeper's four words and her fifth. Her mother's hand is under them, and her mother's mother's is under that, and there are three generations of a family in the chalk on one side of a door, and the fifth word is not in the oath and has never been in the oath and is a keeper's word and is a *keeping* and is said at this arch every morning and has been said by four generations of women who kept this ground.

On the other side of the door is Ottoline Vask's list, four items, in a mason's square hand, and it was not washed and it is not washed, and the two sides of that door are not the same object and have never been the same object and the keeper says so out loud about twice a month.

On the keeper's side, above the four words, there were two things on the sixteenth day of the new month, and both of them came off.

**The first** was written by the keeper, in her own hand, at about the twelfth hour of the night of the twelfth day of the new month, in about two minutes, at a man's request, and it was three lines long and four inches high, and it said:

> **The ground under this yard does not begin without Owen Park.**
>
> **It is his ground and he asked for it and I wrote it.**
>
> **It is a rule and it is a man's name on a floor.**

**The second** was written by Adrian Vale, in his own hand, on the seventh day of the new month, at the request of a man in a good coat, and it said that he refused the workway because it worked, and that he liked saying no, in a room, in front of people, in the rain, and a mason of fifty put his own name under it in his own hand.

She read out what came off. That is in the entry, and it is in her words, and it is a keeper's procedure and there is no other one: she stands under the arch with the cloth in her hand and reads the words out loud before she takes them off, in the yard, in the open, in the language of the room, so that anybody who cannot read has them in their ears before they go.

She read Owen Park's three lines twice. She read Adrian's sentence once, and then she stopped, and about a hundred and forty people in a yard in a city of eleven hundred watched a woman of about seventy decide what to do with a thing that was not hers.

"Two of these are mine," she said, in the local language. "I wrote them on the night of the twelfth of the new month at about the twelfth hour and it took me about two minutes and I did not ask him to say it twice and I have said since that it is a rule and it is a man's name on a floor, and that is my line and not a mason's, and it is the true line and I would write it again.

"**And the other one is a man's own hand about himself, and it has been on that board nine days and about two hundred people have read it, and I am not taking it off, because washing a man's own writing off a board is a decision and not a keeping, and I have been keeping that board for thirty years and I do not decide what a man says about himself.**"

And she washed her own three lines off with the cloth and the water came down the board in a grey run and stopped at the four words and the fifth, which are the family's and have never been washed off, and there was chalk in the run, and about nine people in that yard said afterwards that they had not known there were three lines under it and it turned out there were three lines under it.

And Adrian Vale stood in that yard at about the seventh hour and a half of the morning of the sixteenth day of the new month and took the rest of it off himself with the cloth, and read it out loud first, in the local language, badly, with one mistake in it that Peth Lomas corrected once and did not correct twice, and that is in the entry:

> **I refused the workway because it worked. I liked saying no, in a room, in front of people, in the rain.**
>
> **I have written it here because a man asked me for the true reason and I did not have his.**

That second line is not on the board. It is in the book, in his own hand, added by him at the seventh hour and a half of the morning, and it is the reason a man who asked for the reason for the name in a room on the thirty-eighth got the reason and the fact both.

---

The review of the seat was taken at about the tenth hour of the morning of the same day in the converted salt store on the river market, and there were about forty people in it, and it took two hours and ten minutes, and it is the shortest review in the history of the Lantern Concord and the only one anybody in that room had ever been to that was about whether a man should keep a thing he had asked for.

Nineteen lines had been put up in a yard on the twentieth day of the flood by a notary who has taken forty-one names down to nineteen because a city can act on nineteen and not on forty-one, and about nine of the nineteen were in the room and the other ten had sent a word, and Sera Vail read out the two words that came back and entered them, and the two words were *keep it* and *no*, and one of the ten had sent both, to two different people, on the same morning.

Vask read the terms. Vail read the entry of the post. The post is not a debt and it is not a fee and it is not useful and there is no end of it in sight, and it was named by the keeper of that ground in the twentieth day of the flood, and the local word for it is a word that means a place a person stands, and a keeper's name for a man whose mark is in a building's stone is not a visitor to it, and a visitor can leave.

> **For as long as the ground is open, the ground does not begin without him. Every beginning, he is there or it does not begin.**

Read that out in a room of forty people on the Sunday morning of a day when a hundred and forty people had watched a rule about a man come off a board in that same yard four hours earlier and it is not an instrument, and it is not a mechanism, and every person in that room knew in about four seconds what it was, and about nine of them had said so out loud before the reading was finished.

He is the reason and it cannot be proved to be. That is the ninth clause of a statement of what a person said they saw and he wrote it himself on the twenty-fourth day of the flood about a mark in his own hand, and on the thirty-eighth day of the flood a man on a table in a room by a salt wharf said it back to him about himself and asked for it to be in the book, and it has been in the book for three days and it is the reason a statement of error exists.

---

**The nine who argued for keeping it.**

**Aldis Kerrow**, assessor of strangers, a very good coat, who said that a seat with a person in it is the only thing this city has produced in forty days that a clerk in a building in Aurel can be sent, and that a form with a name in it is a form and a form is worth having in a place where everything has been done in a yard by count, and that he was not defending the sentence, he was defending the paper.

**Ilda Marsh**, seventy, of Nine Elms, who wrote the fourth line of the Reed Reach's term sheet and used it on the thirty-sixth day of the flood and put the whole of a season on a man from another world, and who said that she had spent her life being the woman who says *no* in a village and that the cost of that is that a village with nothing in it has nothing to point at, and that this is a season and she is owed one season and she is not going to be the woman who makes it two.

**Delya Crane**, a widow of nine years, who owns the building the loft is the top of and holds the ground floor on a rent-free lease, and who said four sentences and they are in the book: that a building with nobody answerable in it is a building she carries by herself; that she has been carrying two rooms and a ground floor and a widow's box since the fourteenth day of the flood; and that she is not sentimental about it and would rather be argued with than carried.

**Marek Osley**, a salt factor of about forty-eight, who put his tablet face down and said the mechanism and said the column, and who was for keeping it on the grounds that **a liability can be got rid of in a room and only a liability can be got rid of in a room**, and that was the sentence that made the argument turn, and he said it three times and got a laugh the second time and did not get one the third.

**Owen's foreman.** Hesk, who has never once said the four words under the near gate, and who said that a rule that names a man is the only kind anybody has ever put on a board in this city that somebody actually asked for, and that the man who asked for it died four days after asking for it and asked for it anyway, and that Adrian Vale entered his name in a book on the twenty-fifth day of the flood when he had every reason not to and that is the only reason Hesk was in that room and was willing to be in that room.

**Sesh Brack**, of the ground floor of the same building, a market woman, a landlady, the teacher of the keeper's fifth word to about forty people in five weeks, with a bound hand, who said she would not decide it and would say only the thing that had been left out, which was that the yard had been empty for ninety seconds on Saturday morning and a woman of fifty-eight had said five words in it and about six other people had said them after her, and that whatever this room did about a seat on Sunday, that had happened, and it had happened with no name at the top of it and no rule about it and no board.

**Renna Ord**, who is counted on the other side, and is on the other side because of a rule of hers that is older than any page in the book: **a crew is a man who has owed somebody a shift.** She said that a rule that names a man is a chain, and a chain is a thing you ask a man to hold by name, and that on the thirty-seventh day of the flood a man held a chain by name in a yard in front of two hundred and forty people and four hours later he was under a floor.

**Nell Bray**, docker, felt hat, about sixty, one of the nineteen lines, who argued for dropping it in eleven words and then did not argue for anything else and sat down, and who had said on the fortieth day in a yard of about two hundred and ten people that a record is not a fence, and who was asked in that room whether she would say it again and said no, because it had been said.

**Nyla Ferrow**, who keeps the lock chapel and has said four words to Adrian Vale in a month and every one of them mattered, and who came down to the salt store for this and who said, in the local language, in about nine seconds, and let Peth Lomas read her sentence out in English afterwards because the room had three people in it who would not have understood it:

> **I wrote it because a man asked me for it in my yard and he asked me properly. I have washed it off this morning with a cloth in front of a hundred and forty people because it is a rule and it is a man's name on a floor and I wrote both halves of that sentence as well and I am not going to write it twice.**
>
> **Ask the room what it wants on the ground and do not ask it about a man.**

---

**The nine who argued for dropping it** were Renna Ord and Nell Bray and Wenna Cray of Silt Row, who is forty-one and was on page one against his name on the eleventh day of the flood, and Osten Fenn, twenty-two, of the second gate at Long Water, who entered the first statement of error in the history of this book, and Ottoline Vask, and Sabra Vint, sixty-one, of the upper road, who cannot read and who had walked down two hundred wet steps the day before and back up them and had asked nothing of anybody, and **Hesper Saye**, who was asked whether she wanted to be in that room and said no and then stood in it for two hours at the back with her hands in a bowl, and **Peth Lomas**, who said in about six words that he had put his own name under a true sentence on a board six days ago and that he was not doing it a second time in a different direction.

And one of the nineteen was in the room and did not argue for either, and his name is Adrian Vale and he is the holder of the pull and he is barred by the standing correction from entering a statement of error against his own seat.

---

He said it at about the eleventh hour of the morning and it took him about nine minutes, and Vask has entered that this is the first true sentence said in a room in this city that was about the thing and not about the man who said it, and that she has entered that she thinks it is going to be the last.

"I have known this since Thursday," he said. "I have known it since about the second hour of the afternoon of the thirty-eighth day of the flood and I have not said it in a room in three days, and the reason I have not said it in a room in three days is that I have been doing the other thing, which is being the answer, and the other thing has been very good for me for three days and a woman of about sixty in a felt hat stood up on the Saturday evening and said that a record is not a fence and I have not been able to think about anything else since.

"**Two posts. They are not the same post and they are the same shape of rule, and only one of them is standing, and I am the one who has to say so, and I am saying it.**

"One of them was on that board this morning at the seventh hour in front of about a hundred and forty people and the keeper took it off with a cloth, and it was the rule that the ground under this yard does not begin without Owen Park, and it was asked for by Owen Park, out loud, in that yard, in front of about twenty people, on the evening of the twelfth day of the new month, and it was written by the woman who keeps that ground in her own hand in about two minutes, and I want that in the book in exactly those terms because it is the only rule anybody in this city has ever put on a wall that somebody actually asked for.

"And the other one is the thing in front of you, and it is the same sentence with my name in it, and **I did not ask for it, and nobody asked me whether I wanted it, and it was given to me by a woman who was pricing me for it, and it has been in a book since the twentieth day of this flood, and it is the reason I could not stop a working on the thirty-seventh day of this flood that had a man in it, and not because I was lied to and not because I was overruled.**

"It is not a mechanism. There is nothing in it. It has no power and it stops nothing and it holds nothing. **It is a liability with a man in it, and the reason a man went into a floor in this city four days ago is a rule that made a man the answer for a floor.**"

And Sera Vail, from the second table, in the flat voice, without raising it, stopped him there in a room of about forty people.

"That is a finding," she said. "You may not enter it. It is in the book on the thirty-ninth day of the flood, in a woman's hand, four pages long, and it says in its own terms that a record is not a finding and cannot be used as one, and it says that nobody may write *he died because*, and it says that the person who named must answer it in public in a room or withdraw it or stand by it and say that they can say no more.

"**I have watched four hundred and ten people in that hall want one sentence for four days.** Say it again as what you said."

"I said it as what I said," said Adrian Vale.

"Say it as what you said, in the words you used, with the hours in it, and put your name under it, and I will enter it as a person's account of why he thinks a thing should be dropped, in his own words, and I will read it back at the end of this sitting, and the room can agree with it or not agree with it, and the room cannot make it the reason a man died."

---

They read it back at the end of the sitting and the room did not make it the reason a man died and the entry says so.

And then the room did the thing, and it is in the book in the flat hand with the count of nineteen and the two words that came back from the ten who were not in it, and it took about nine minutes, and there was no show of hands and Vail has said since that she has never in twenty-six years run a show of hands in that room and was not going to start with one.

> **The seat is readmitted for a final term. The term ends on the forty-ninth day of the flood.**
>
> **And the post is changed in the following terms, and the change is entered as the reason and not as a favour: that the ground under that yard may not begin without his being told of it and asked for, and that he is not the answer if he cannot be got; that the cost column of the yard's standing labour is not altered and continues; that the new page for the second group is not altered and he may not sit in the seat while it is not written up as well as any other page; and that the post is not renewed by anybody without a room, in daylight, with a date on it.**
>
> **— and entered by the notary, in her own words, and read out twice: that the holder asked in this room to be removed from the terms of the post and was not, and said why, and that his reason was that a rule that names a man is a chain, and that she has entered his reason and is not able to enter the request, and that the reason she is not able to enter it is that a man who asks to be taken off a duty in the room where the duty is being decided has understood the room, and that it is in the book what a man can do with a room when he has understood it.**

And Adrian Vale read the whole of it before he signed it, in a converted salt store on the river market, on the sixteenth day of the new month, in about eleven minutes, including the cost column, which is the fourth page and which he has never read in full in five weeks.

It is the first entry in this city's book that he has signed having read it. The last one was the eleventh page, on the eleventh day of the new month, in four seconds, without reading it, and a reed cutter's sighting of a light in a reed channel went under a counter-signature and into the record of a city as the first thing anybody from another world has ever corroborated about a thing one of them said he could see, and Vail asked him to read it before he signed it and he signed it in four seconds and did not notice himself not reading it and that is the finding, and the finding is in her hand because he is barred from entering a statement of error against his own seat.

She entered the eleven minutes. Nobody in that room had ever seen him take eleven minutes over a page.

---

The answer he did not get is in the yard, in a doorway, at about the fifth hour of the afternoon.

Roke was outside the salt store with a waxed slate under his arm and about forty people in the yard, and Sabra Vint came down the yard from the market steps at about the fifth hour with nothing in her hands, which she does every time she has come down those steps, and she has come down nine times in nine years and this was the eleventh.

He read her paper aloud in a yard on the fortieth day in front of about two hundred and ten people and it took about nineteen minutes and he could not read one character of it, and it has been to a man in a good coat and to a woman of twenty-nine who reads Crown Law and to a mason of thirty-four and to a notary, and four of them cannot read it.

She asked him in the doorway. She asked him the way she has asked nine times in nine years, which is directly, and without any of the four words that people in markets put in front of it, and she said:

"And your eight."

And Roke gave her an answer that is true, in about thirty words, in the local language, and it is entered in the room's book by Peth Lomas, who was standing nine feet away, and Adrian Vale read it on the page on the Monday morning and did not sleep well on the Monday night.

> **Eight of the eleven behind this wall have no stone, and one of them is my grandfather, who stood at the gate at this lock for thirty years, and I have known the number of them since I was nine years old. And I did not ask you in a yard on the fortieth because a man who stands up in front of two hundred people and uses a woman of sixty-one's nine years to get at his own eight is doing a thing I would think less of him for, and you have been walking down two hundred steps in the rain for nine years on your own account and I was not going to be the reason you had to do it in front of anybody.**

And Sabra Vint said: "That is a true sentence. It is not the answer, and I have been asking about stones for nine years and I have never once been answered with a sentence about somebody else's nine years."

And Roke said, "No," and that is entered, and he did not say anything else about it, and he is not going to, and the thread is not closed.

---

At about the eighth hour of the evening the keeper put her four words and her fifth back on the board.

She did it with the same chalk and the same square hand and she did not ask anybody to help and there was nobody in the yard except about four people who had come back for no reason they gave. She put the fourth word down last, and the fifth in the middle, and the fifth is not in the oath and has never been in the oath and is a keeper's word and is a *keeping*.

On the keeper's side of that board, on the sixteenth day of the new month, at about the eighth hour of the evening, after about a hundred and forty people had stood in a yard in daylight and watched two rules come off it, there is the four words and the fifth, and there is nothing else, and one of the two things that came off it was put there by a man who had asked to have it put there and one of them was put there by a woman who had been asked by a man who is under a board eight feet away from where she was standing, and neither of those two facts is on the board, and the board does not keep them, and the board is washed every Sunday morning.

On the other side of that door, Ottoline Vask's four items are in a mason's square hand and are not washed, and item four is *anything that answers*, and the chapel stone under the arch in this yard is a raised pale slab laid across the flagstones and worn into a shallow bowl by four hundred years of boots, and the black line in it has not moved and has not spoken and is not repaired, and about nine people in that yard at about the eighth hour of the evening looked at it and did not say anything about it and did not step over it without meaning to.
