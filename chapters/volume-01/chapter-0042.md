# Chapter 0042 — One Night to Choose

On the seventeenth day of the new month, which was the forty-second day of the flood and a Monday, the Hearthguard of the outer ward and the Lantern Concord together gave the city of Saltwake one night to choose among three things, and about two hundred and forty people were in a converted salt store when the third of them was read out loud, and the room wanted the third one inside eleven minutes, and the reason the room wanted it is that it was the only one of the three with a stipend in it and a lawyer named in it, and neither of those is a reason and both of them are the reason, and a man in a good coat had said a fourth thing in a yard on the fortieth day of the flood that is not on the list and is the same shape as the third one, and the notary of this city entered under both of them on the fortieth that they do not agree with each other.

---

The three were read at about the eighth hour of the evening in a converted salt store on the river market and there were about two hundred and forty people in it and the doors were open on both sides because it is a store and it is cold.

**Aldis Kerrow** read them. He is the Concord's assessor of strangers and he has a very good coat and a narrow mouth and no more affection for the situation than Sera Vail, and he read three documents in a room of about two hundred and forty people on the evening of the seventeenth day of the new month, and he read them in the order in which they were printed, and he read them slowly, and he did not editorialise once in about eleven minutes, and Vail has entered that he did not editorialise once and that she checked, because she has sat at that table beside a man from Aurel since the fourth day of the flood and she knows what a man reads when he is about to do something with a room.

**The first.** That the main breach in the Lock of Salt gate be closed, and that the ground under the lock chapel be closed over with a dressed lid and a date cut into it, and that the closing be done by a working on a named tide, and that the work carry the ordinary terms of a working: a pattern, a hand, a willing witness and a cost. And, in the fourth clause, in the words of the Hearthguard and not of the Concord, that **any person in the ground under that chapel when the work is done is not brought out of it, and that the reason is not secrecy and is that a working's residue goes where the water is and there is no way of standing next to it.**

**The second.** That the main breach be left as it is, and the Lock of Salt be worked as it has always been worked, on every tide, in the ordinary way, by nineteen men of the day crew and twenty-one of the flood crew, and that the city accept the standing of the join as a known quantity; and, in the second clause, that the join in the gate is stepped by the ordinary maintenance of a lock and not by the tide and not by the flood, and that the four steps of three-quarters of an inch each fell on the four days the lock did **unusual** work, and that a city which intends to go on doing unusual work at this lock will keep stepping a join toward a gate that has to be held by forty men twice a day.

**The third.** That the containment of the main breach be held by **one named person**, answerable for it alone; that the person be a clerk of the Lantern Concord **answerable only to Aurel**; that the person hold the containment of that breach, and that the answer of that person on the containment not be answerable by any other person in Saltwake; and that the Crown recognise the arrangement, appoint a Crown advocate for it, and pay a stipend against it quarterly.

---

The room wanted the third one inside eleven minutes.

It is entered that it took eleven minutes, because a man of about sixty in a felt hat was in that room with a slate and a docker of about fifty-eight with a barrow was at the back and both of them have said independently that it was eleven, and Vail entered both figures and did not reconcile them and has said that she is not going to reconcile them.

What happened in the eleven minutes was this. About ninety people put a hand up for the third one in the first four minutes. About thirty put a hand up for the second one. Nobody put a hand up for the first one, and about forty people in that room changed their minds about the first one in the eighth minute when a woman of about forty-four in the second row said the thing that is entered on the next page.

And about eleven of the ninety who had put a hand up for the third one did it in the first ninety seconds, and every one of the eleven had put a hand up for the third one before the third one had finished being read.

That is in the book in the notary's own words, and it is the reason a show of hands in that room is entered as a show of hands and not as a count, and it is in the entry in these terms: **that eleven persons raised a hand for the third instrument before the reading of the third instrument was completed, and that the notary is not able to say why, and that a person raising a hand for a thing they have not finished hearing is a thing this book has one procedure about and has used it three times.**

---

**The woman of about forty-four** is Elsa Skell, of Corrow, forty-four years old, who works the bottom field and who stopped speaking to Adrian Vale on the eighteenth day of the flood and told him in a gateway that a man who owes a season has no business in a village's book, and who said in a hall on the twentieth day of the flood that her number goes down and his goes up and only one of them will be right in March, and who put nineteen unnamed people putting flour on ditch banks at four in the morning into a hall as a number that was not a season.

"There is nothing in the first one," she said, in the local language. "I want that said by a woman who owes a season and not by him. There is nothing in the first one and I am the last person in this city who has any business saying so and I am saying it.

"**Close it and put a lid on it is what you do to a bucket.** It is the right thing to do to a bucket and this is not a bucket and there is a black mark in a place under that floor that a mason entered in a book on the twenty-sixth day of this flood as damaged and not closable, and if the join in the gate is worked one more time then the water in the river below that floor is moving in a direction, and on the twenty-fifth day of this flood a good working at that gate moved it in exactly that direction and put eleven people out of a floor in front of three hundred and ten people, and the man whose shoulder went that morning is under a board in the ordinary ground and it was his hand that went on that flagstone and he took a child's weight off it.

"So the first one is a lid on a bucket with a date cut into it, and the date is for us, and it is not for the river, and I want it entered that I said it and that a woman of the bottom field said it and that I have not spoken to him in twenty-four days."

Nobody argued with her about the first one. About four people in that room said afterwards that they had wanted the first one and had not said so, and one of the four is a mason of thirty-four years who has not been in a room with two hundred and forty people in it since the twenty-fifth day of the flood.

---

The third instrument is the Crown's, and it is dated, and it has a form behind it.

Renner Sallow is the second clerk of the Registry at Aurel and he has sat at the second table on the Concord's side since the fourth day of the flood, and about nine people in that city had noticed him and one of them is Sera Vail, and he read two hundred and forty words of a Crown letter aloud in a hall of four hundred and ten people on the thirty-first day of the flood and turned the whole room round.

Adrian Vale asked him one question, in that room, at about the ninth hour, in the language of the room, and it was a bad question and he asked it anyway and it is in the book.

"Is the third thing in the form the Registry wants?"

And Renner Sallow said: "It is in a **different** form from the notice of the twenty-eighth day of the flood and it is **answerable**, and it would satisfy that office, and I can tell you that in about nine seconds, and I am telling you in nine seconds, in a room, because you asked me in front of two hundred and forty people and not in a corridor, and I want that entered as the reason and I would like it entered that I have wanted to be asked in front of people for a fortnight."

He was right about that and it is in the book, and what it did to the room is in the book too, and the entry is: **that the Crown's second demand, which requires before the next quarterly sitting either a registered holder with a Crown-recognised workway and a Crown advocate or a statement of permanent closure, and which has not been answered in the form required and whose next sitting is in about a hundred and ten days from the date of the letter, would be answered by the third instrument, and that the third instrument and the second demand are two documents from one office and are not the same instrument, and that the notary has entered that difference and is the only person in the room who has read both.**

And then Adrian Vale stood up in a room of about two hundred and forty people and said the thing that is the whole of the seventeenth day of the new month and took him about two minutes, and Tamsin Quill did not translate it and did not correct it, and about nine people in that room have said that he got the whole of it right in a language he learned forty days ago.

"**Three things have been put in front of this city in five weeks and they are not one thing and two of them are the Crown's and one of them is not the Crown's at all, and I am going to say what each of them is, and I am going to be wrong about one of them and I am going to say that I am going to be wrong.**

"**The first** is a mark in a wall in the outer channel under the cap of an old landing two hundred steps down the walk-round. It is eighteen inches long and three fingers wide and it is closed and it is not a door and it has carried exactly one person in its life and that use was entered as a breach and is not a precedent. It has four witnesses and a cost and a receiving community that may suspend it, and it may not be used until a fresh inspection, no suspension, and an answer, and the answer was *no* on the thirty-first day of this flood. It is not what the third instrument is about and it is not what the first instrument is about and it is not the same size as the mark on my hand and it is the same **proportion** as the mark on my hand, and if somebody in this room has been told that those two things are the same size then the person who told them is wrong and it is my hand.

"**The second** is the notice of the Registrar of Crossings and Tides of the twenty-eighth day of the month, which wants a form filled in, and this city answered it in its own words on the tenth day of the new month, in nine lines, and the nine lines are in a sealed book and they are not the form required, and a form has a seal and a quarterly sitting and a lawyer in it and a man who cannot be filled in and a position that can be filled by somebody who can be held to it.

"**And the third** is tonight's third instrument, and it is the Crown's, and it is a different document from the second one and it is the same office, and it is a good offer, and I am not going to stand in a room and say it is a bad offer, because there are about ninety people in this room who have been unpaid for forty days and there is a stipend in that document and there is a lawyer in it, and I am not going to be the man who says no to money in a room in a city where a season of a field has been entered in a book five times.

"Nowhere in those three things is the thing a man in a good coat said in a yard on the fortieth day of the flood in about a hundred and thirty words, and I want that said, and I want it said by me, because there is not one page in this city's book that accuses him of anything and I have not put one there, and I am not going to stand in a room and pretend that is a rule I made, and **he said a thing in that yard and the notary entered underneath it that it is not the notice from Aurel and that the two documents which say the same thing do not agree with each other, and she was right, and I read that page on Sunday morning and I have been carrying it for two days and I am going to find out what it is in a room, from somebody who is very good at rooms.**"

And about four people in that room did not follow that, and about two hundred and ten did, and the difference is not recorded because a difference of attention in a room of two hundred and forty is not a fact anybody can prove, and the entry says: **that the holder of the pull said out loud, in a room, that he expected to be wrong about one of the three things and named the third instrument as the one he expected to be wrong about, and that the man in a good coat was present and did not contradict him and has entered that he did not contradict him and why.**

---

**And then Tamsin Quill read the fortieth day out loud a second time.**

Nobody asked her to. The reading of the three was finished at about the ninth hour and the room was loud and there were people in the doorway and about forty people were arguing about the show of hands, and she had the fourth volume of the book under her arm, because she had brought it in with her, and nobody in that room had asked her to bring it, and about nine people have said since that they understood at the time why she had brought it and about two hundred and thirty did not.

She read the entry of the ninth hour of the evening of the fortieth day of the flood, in the language of the room, in the flat voice she uses for documents, and it took about a minute and a half, and it is a hundred and thirty words about a lock and a join and a hand, and it is in a very neat small hand, and about two hundred and forty people in a converted salt store in a city of eleven hundred listened to a woman of twenty-nine read out a sentence that a man in a good coat had said in a yard and had not been convicted of saying.

Then she read it again.

And then she said one word.

> **"Standing."**

And Adrian Vale said, in English, in the middle of a room, "I do not know that word in this language."

"It is not your word yet and it will be," said Tamsin Quill. "It is on the arch at the lock yard. It is the third line of the old form of the oath, which binds **the gate and the standing of the man in it**, and the old form was taken out of the oath in this city sixty years ago, and the four generations of women who have kept that ground have said four words and a fifth for sixty years and none of the five is that one.

"And it is the word the Crown's notice uses. Not this one — a different notice, from about two hundred years ago, and I have read it and I have read the wrong one and I want you to understand that I have read the wrong one as well as the right one, and that is the sentence I am about to say and I am going to say it quickly and once.

"**A thing that has a standing is a thing whose answer cannot be answered.** That is what the word is. It is not an insult and it is not a party and it is not a faction and it is not a man's invention. It is a term in a Crown book about procedure and it is older than the Crown, and it is in the old form of a maintenance oath in a yard in this city, and it is the reason the old form was taken out sixty years ago, and it is in tonight's third instrument in the fourth clause, and it is in the Registry's notice in the fourth item, and it is what a man in a good coat said in a yard on Saturday evening in about a hundred and thirty words, and **it is not in one office and it is not in one office's coat.**"

And then she said the other half, which is the half that made the room go quiet, and she said it in the language of the room and she looked at Adrian Vale while she said it, and he has entered that she looked at him on purpose and that he understood afterwards why and that he would not have understood it in the moment.

"**The old form of the oath was stopped in this city because a man in it died, and the reason he died is that the gate had a standing and the standing was a man, and the man could not be got, and the gate did not open, and about four hundred people were on the wrong side of a river in a flood month.** And the man died in a wet coat and his name is on a list in a building in Aurel and there is no stone. And the man said the oath out loud in this city and he was told the terms and he agreed to the terms and he did the work and the standing was his and there was no second man in the oath, and that is the whole of it, and it is a hundred and forty years old and I have read it four times and I have never been in a room where both halves of it were said out loud at the same time."

Nobody in that room had heard the old form of the oath spoken about in full before. Nine people in this city can still say it. Four of them were in that room. Nobody asked them.

---

At about the ninth hour and about twenty minutes, in a converted salt store with about two hundred and forty people in it, a pale field came up in the air in front of Adrian Vale in the colour of a paper form, and stood there for about nineteen seconds, and about nine people saw it happen.

> **A CONTAINMENT MAY BE HELD BY ONE NAMED PERSON.**
>
> **The person named pays nothing. The city is not asked. Nothing in this is a cost.**

It came about twenty minutes after a Crown clerk in a good coat had read the third of three instruments out loud to a room of about two hundred and forty people, and about nine seconds after a woman of twenty-nine had said the word *standing* in a converted salt store, and the field is in the colour of a form the Registry of Crossings and Tides sends on a river boat four times a year.

"No," said Adrian Vale, out loud, in English, in a room of about two hundred and forty people.

"Say it slowly and say what it is," said Tamsin Quill, four feet away.

"It is a show of hands for the third one," he said. "It arrived about twenty seconds after two hundred and forty people put their hands up for a document with a stipend in it and a lawyer named in it, and it is the best of the fifteen things this thing has ever said to me and I want to say that before I say anything else about it.

"The first fourteen put a cost in the place where a cost goes. This one has put **nothing** in the place where a cost goes. That is not a lie about the stipend and it is not a lie about the lawyer. It is a lie about the third clause. *The city is not asked.* A city being asked is the cost. That is the whole of the cost. The person named pays nothing because the person named is not the thing that pays — **the city is the thing that pays**, in gate-labour and reed-cutting and ditching and flood shift, at a rate set by a meeting-ground four miles away, for as long as the instrument stands and whether it is used or not, and this field has just told me that the not-asking is free, and it is not free, and the not-asking is the entire instrument, and I have been on the wrong end of it for forty days and I have been the private debtor of it since the thirty-sixth.

"It is the same construction as the last one and it is a new one. The last one took away a duty and called it free. This one takes away a question, and a question is what a city is made of, and this field has just told me that the not-asking is free, and it is not free, and the not-asking is the entire instrument, and I have been the private debtor of that not-asking since the thirty-sixth day of this flood. Fourteen things this thing has said to me have all put a cost in the place where a cost goes. This is the first one that has put nothing there, and I would rather it put a lie there."

**Renner Sallow**, from the second table, in the flat voice he uses for documents, and in the language of the room, said: "May I say one thing as a clerk and not as a person in the room?"

"You may say one thing as a clerk."

"That field arrived in your language and not in mine," he said. "I have been in this city thirty-nine days and I have seen it six times and every one of the six times it has been in a stranger's language and not in anybody's else, and I have put all six in a book at Aurel because it is my office to put things in books, and I would like the seventh in the same book with the same note on it, and I would like it entered that I said so in front of two hundred and forty people and that the man in the good coat heard me say it and did not move."

---

**The city was given one night.**

That is the whole of it and it is in the book in nine lines, sealed, on the seventeenth day of the new month, and the nine lines are Vail's, and the ninth line is the reason: **that a city of eleven hundred people had been asked to choose among three instruments in about twenty minutes and that the time given to it was one night, and that the notary considers one night to be enough and has entered that she considers it to be enough, and that a person who says that one night is not enough is a person who wants a second month, and there has not been a second month in this city since the eleventh day of the month.**

The show of hands was entered as a show of hands, with the number of hands and the number of people in the room and the hour, and the notary's own sentence about it is in the entry: **that a show of hands is not a count and I have not run a count in that room in twenty-six years and I am not going to start with one at the end of the fortieth day of a flood, and that a room that has been asked to choose among three things in twenty minutes and has shown ninety hands for one of them has chosen what it was given and not what it wanted, and that the entry of what it wanted has not been taken because I do not know how to take it and neither does anybody else in this city.**

The third instrument is entered as **the preference of the room** and not as the choice of the city, and the first instrument is entered as the preference of about four people who did not put a hand up, and the second is entered as the preference of about thirty hands and as the instrument that has no office and no lawyer in it and no place in the Registry's file at all.

---

About forty people in a yard said afterwards that they would have chosen the other one.

That is on the page in nine words and it is not a correction and it is not a retraction. It is entered as: **that about forty persons in the yard of the salt store at about the eleventh hour of the evening of the seventeenth day of the new month said that they would have chosen otherwise, and that about nine of them named the first instrument, and that one of the nine was a woman of about forty-four of Corrow who has a season in a book and who had said in that room four hours earlier that there was nothing in the first one, and that the entry of a person's changed mind at the eleventh hour of an evening is made and not discarded, because a city that discards it has learned that a hand up in a room is not a promise and is not a refusal.**

And about nine people in that yard said the other thing, which is that a person who has put a hand up in a room and then said in a yard an hour later that they would have chosen the other one is describing a room and not a person, and that this city has no procedure for that yet.

---

The first thing anybody asked Adrian Vale, when the yard had emptied down to about forty people and the ninth hour had gone, was not about the stipend and was not about the lawyer.

It was asked by a docker of about forty, a flood-creel man with a lamp, and it is in the book because the man asked for it to be, and the question is four words long in the local language and it is on the page in English as: *Is anybody near it?*

"**Is anybody near the scar?**"

"Nobody is in it," said Adrian Vale. "The ground was closed and re-boarded on the fortieth day of the flood and the boards are the same boards with two new ones on the north end and the underside of every one of them is chalked with the date, and the date is the fortieth, and there is a tarpaulin over four feet of floor above them, and there is a seam in the ground under the tarpaulin that came open across and not along and it is four feet long and it is dressed stone on both sides of it and it is two hundred years old and a mason has said in a yard that it is not closable and that it is entered as damaged and not closable, and it is not fixed, and nobody is standing on it, and nobody has been standing on it since about the ninth hour of the morning of the fortieth day, and I have been in that yard about nine times in nine days and there has been nobody under that tarpaulin in any of them."

The man with the lamp said, "I did not ask whether anybody is in it."

"No," said Adrian Vale. "You asked whether anybody is near it, and the answer is the same and the reason I want it in a book is that somebody in about four minutes is going to stand up in that yard and say that there is a man on a board two hundred steps from a hole in the ground, and there is. His name is Ovin Sarr, he is nineteen, and he has stood on that tarpaulin at six and at six every morning since the twelfth day of this flood because a rule says whoever is nearest says the five words, and there is no rule in this world that puts a name at the top of anything, and that is the answer to your question and I would like it written down before anybody in that yard decides to be clever about it."

And the man with the lamp said that he had not been being clever, and that he had wanted to know, and that he had wanted to know because he is a flood-creel man and a thing four hundred yards away that is still the old join under a chapel is his business and not the gate's.

---

At about one in the morning on the eighteenth day of the new month, which is the forty-third day of the flood, Adrian Vale went down two hundred steps to the lower gallery of the Lock of Salt, alone, with a lamp and a folding steel rule.

He has been going down on and off for forty days and he was not going to stop on the night the city was given a night, and there is a note in the book at the second hour of that morning that says a man came down two hundred steps at one in the morning and read the number and did not write in the box, and that he read the number and it is the same, and that **he did not take it as a reading and did not write it, and the reason is in the entry and the reason is that the tide is at the top at about the fourth hour and forty men of this city's own crew are under the near gate at six and at six and a number taken in that gallery at one in the morning on a working tide is not a number, it is a number somebody was in the way of taking.**

Eight and five-eighths. The same. The four chalked marks have not moved, and the docker's *nine* is on the wall of the second bay under it, in a docker's hand, across the join, toward the near gate, and it is not this number and has never been this number.

There are two ruled lines in the column between the twenty-second reading and where the twenty-third one is going to go, drawn in his own hand, and a ruled line is a fact and a missing line is a fight, and about nine people in this city know that he has not been down there in three days and not one of the nine has said so.

He stood with the rule in his hand and did not use it. He looked at his right palm, in the lamplight, and the two inches of hair-thin black in the heel of it are the same width they have been since the second morning of this flood and it is forty-one days old and it has not moved one sixteenth of an inch, and he wrote that down in the notebook at about one in the morning of the eighteenth day of the new month because the third instrument of the seventeenth day of the new month is a thing that holds a person's standing and he wanted one number in that notebook that had not moved.

The box in the last column of the tide table is empty against the forty-third day.

It was empty against the thirty-third for six days and a docker with a barrow filled it in on a Saturday morning and the *k* is pressed too hard and nobody has ever asked her what she decided and she has told about nine people in a market that she decided nothing and that on Tuesday she will not be able to say what she wrote.

It has been empty against the forty-third for nine days.

Nothing gained. A date, and a question he cannot answer, and a word in a woman's mouth that he has written down in a notebook and has not told anybody and has asked a notary not to enter for two days, and the notary entered the request and the hour and did not ask for a reason, and has said that she will ask for a reason on the twentieth day of the new month and that she will ask for it in a room and not in a corridor, and Adrian Vale has written down that he is going to have to give her one.

The tide was at the top at about the fourth hour. At about the sixth hour nineteen men of the day crew and twenty-one of the flood crew will go under the near gate and say four words twice, at their ordinary volume, out of the line and not together, and a man with a shoulder that was never set cut a slab for a grave on Sunday morning and does not know it, and a foreman who has never once said those four words will stand on the sill and look at the stone and not at the water.

And the man who fills in the box will not be Adrian Vale, and he will not be a woman with tweezers, and he will not be a man in a good coat, and he will not be a notary, and he will not be nineteen years old.

He will be nineteen years old, and he will be holding a stick of chalk, and nobody will have asked him what he decided.
