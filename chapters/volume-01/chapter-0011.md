# Chapter 0011 — The Second Sitting

The hall did not empty.

That was the first thing Adrian understood about Saltwake, and it took him a long while yet to understand the whole of it: when two hundred and eleven people have been in a room since before light, and the thing they came for has been written down, they do not go home. They stay, and they find the next thing, and the next thing is always smaller and always worse.

By ten o'clock there were four people at the back who had arrived after the reading, and forty who had gone out to open their stalls and come back, and a queue along the north wall by the roll of public business, which was the queue for the notary. Sera Vail was letting them at it one at a time and saying, in English, in a voice with no give in it whatsoever, "If your business is that the gate is open, that business is with the Hearthguard of the outer ward and they are outside. If your business is that you would like a different answer, there is no procedure for that and I would like it noted that you would like one. If your business is a number you have been given, wait."

They were not waiting. Adrian counted nine of them who were not waiting, and understood within a minute that this was now his problem, because a man with a name in a chained ledger is a man in a queue, and he had been in the ledger for ninety minutes and had not yet worked out what that did to his Tuesday.

"You are going over there in about four minutes," Tamsin said, "and when you do, four of those people will tell you things that are not true in order to be nearer you, and one of them will tell you something that is true, and you will not be able to tell them apart."

---

The morning's business was procedural, and it went exactly as badly as Adrian had feared.

The Concord's assessor of strangers had a name, which Adrian learned at some point on Friday morning and afterwards could not remember learning. It was **Aldis Kerrow**. He had a very good coat, a narrow mouth, and the air of a man put at that table by somebody intending to leave and not doing it.

"The terms are read. Site, witnesses, receiving community, cost, all entered. What is not entered is any of the four." He put a finger on the page. "None of those is a calendar. This city does not have a door because a man said so at a hearing. It has a door because somebody stood in the mud at low water on a Tuesday and did a job four other people agreed had been done correctly. Until somebody stands in the mud, the four of them are a sentence in a paragraph."

"Whose mud," said a voice near the door, and half the room turned round.

Marek Osley was a salt factor of about forty-eight with a clerk's stoop and a waxed tablet he had not put down since he came in. Adrian had not met him before Friday morning, and he had the look of a man who was going to be a recurring problem for as long as Adrian was in this city.

"I am not arguing about the door. I have no interest in the door. I am arguing about the week." He turned a hundred and forty degrees and addressed the room. "There is an inspection in these terms. Very good. Who is on it, what does it cost, where do they stand, and when. And there is a place on that page where a date can go, and a date has not gone in it, and until a date is in it the page is *provisional*, and provisional pages do not leave this hall. I have to carry it to eleven villages before the ember tide. Provisional, I carry a rumour. A date, I carry a schedule, and a village can plan a season against a schedule."

"Which of those keeps the field under water?" said Brann from the middle of the floor, and there was a laugh, and it was not entirely a friendly one.

"The field is under water because of a man in a join. I keep the count. I do not have opinions about joins. I have a page with a hole in it and eleven villages who are going to ask me what the hole is."

"The notary does not construe the terms she has written," said Kerrow, and every head in the salt store turned to Sera Vail.

She had not moved. She had the ledger open with the chain through the ring, and she had not dipped the chalk once since the reading.

"I did not write those terms this morning. I read them. Every one of them is a *description* of a thing that already exists somewhere else. A site exists as a wall. A witness exists as a person who has agreed. A receiving community exists as a village that has been asked. I write down the description. If nobody goes and makes the description true, then what I have written is a very tidy account of nothing, and it will stay in that book for four hundred years looking extremely official, and not one person in this city will be able to open it and say *this is a lie*, because nobody will remember." She dipped the chalk. "So no. If I construe them and construe them wrong, it becomes a ruling, and a ruling is binding, and I would rather be a woman with a bad pen than a court with a good one."

"The record is the record," said Kerrow, to nobody.

"The record is the record, and a record is not a working. A working is a hand on a join." She squared the ledger. "I have a pen."

---

So Adrian tried to make a rota, and it took forty minutes, and it produced nothing, and he understood exactly why by the end of it.

The problem was not that he did not know what an inspection needed. He knew. Four witnesses, a name for each, an hour at which the tide permits it, a place to stand, and somebody with the authority to say *no*. He could have written that in eleven minutes on Earth and had it reviewed by four teams. The first four items meant nothing and the last two meant everything, and he had spent nine years building a career out of the first four.

"Low water," said Ovin Sarr, who had come down off the walk-round at nine, nineteen years old, eating a heel of bread from somewhere. "Low water is two hours before the turn, and the turn is not the same two days running. You put a day in. The day says *at the turn*, and if the turn is wrong you move it, and moving it is a job, and the job is on somebody. If the somebody is not there, the inspection does not happen and the thing stays shut." He grinned. "An inspection is not a form. It is four people turning up in the wrong weather."

"Who are the four people?"

"That is the other page," said Ovin, and did not explain, because he was nineteen and had a flood to get back to.

Adrian went and found Renna Ord, who had told him what *skyborn* meant and who now had a boat-coat on and about four minutes of patience. He asked her what a crew was.

"Then you do not know that a crew is not a list," she said in English, slowly. "A crew is a man who has owed somebody a shift. You ask a man to come. If he comes you owe him. If you do not pay, you do not get to ask him again for nine days, and everyone knows you did not pay. So we do it in an order, and the order is not written down, it is *known*, and the knowing is older than the writing, and a man who has been here four days cannot get at it with a page. That is the thing you are going to be bad at. You will be bad at it for a year."

---

Then the pale field came up in front of the roll of public business, the colour of a paper form, about eighteen inches long.

> THE HOLDER MAY BE SUMMONED.
>
> A holder who does not come may be replaced at the next sitting.
>
> Being angry is not a reason to make a holder. Being angry is a reason to be a witness.

It stood there while two hundred and eleven people argued about a salt page.

Two thirds of it was not a lie. He had said the words himself in the rain, that if he was gone longer than a day the seat fell vacant and stayed vacant, and Vail had put it in the book, and it was in the book, and the first line of the field said almost exactly that.

The second line did not say that at all.

*A holder who does not come may be replaced at the next sitting.* The book said vacant and stayed vacant. The book said a vacant seat is not a fault and that the seat may be filled by no one but the man whose mark is in the stone. The book did not contain the word *replaced* anywhere in it, in any page, in any column, and he had spent four minutes on Friday morning reading the whole of that entry out loud to two hundred and eleven people.

So one of those two things was a sentence and one of those two things was a door somebody was trying to open, and Adrian had been handed it in a hand he could not check by a thing he could not argue with, and it had arrived at exactly the moment in the morning when a tired man with a waxed tablet wanted a page with fewer holes in it.

And it was the most useful thing the wall had said to him in four days, and it was aimed exactly at Marek Osley, who wanted a date in a box. *Being angry is not a reason to make a holder. Being angry is a reason to be a witness.* A man who keeps a count, watching a page with a hole in it, was already thinking that the seat is a job, and a job can be applied for, and a procedure for replacing a man is a thing that could be put on this page and defended in front of eleven villages.

It was also, if Adrian was honest, aimed at him. It was telling him the truth about the terms he had accepted, which was a kindness, in the specific load-bearing way in which somebody tells you the load-bearing wall is on your side of the room.

He did not tell the room. He wrote it up afterwards, in the flat hand, on the page where he kept the things the wall said that he had not repeated, and wrote: *It offered me nothing today. It agreed with the man who wants my seat. Remember: it is more useful to me when I am frightened than when I am owed.*

---

Brann came up at about eleven.

He did not shout, which was the thing Adrian had braced for and did not get. He walked the length of the salt store with his hands at his sides, stopped six feet away, and looked at the pale trace on Adrian's open palm, which Adrian had, without thinking about it, been holding up since the field appeared.

"On Tuesday," Brann said in the local language, slowly, and Tamsin translated it word for word because she had understood, correctly, that this was not a conversation that was going to survive a summary. "You came out of a wall at first light and by midday you were out on the walk-round telling us about a join. I was in the yard. I was shouting for the change of the watch. It is in the Hearthguard book with the hour on it and Roke put it there and I have never once asked him to. And while I was shouting, nobody was looking at the outside of this lock, and a thing came out of the wall and took six people." He put his thumb on his own sternum.

"And then on Wednesday night you made a promise in the chapel with nobody standing in it, and the next morning that stone had a black line in it that is going to be there when my great-grandson is a man, and I have not worked out which of those two nights I am angrier about, and I have had three weeks."

Adrian said nothing at all, because there was nothing to say, and every person in the front six rows of a salt store watched a Hearthguard say the true thing about him twice in three weeks and not once ask for anything for it.

"I am not saying either of them was your fault," Brann said. "I am saying I have been carrying the shape of both of them since Tuesday and there is nobody in this city I can be angry at, and you are the only thing in this room with a name in a book."

"Yes," Adrian said.

Brann said the word again, in English, as though it had come out in the wrong language and he was pleased about it. "So put it in the book that I said it. I have asked Vail already. She says a man cannot be entered for saying a thing that is true."

"You are not going to be entered for it," said Vail, from the table, without looking up. "You are going to be entered for it."

He went back to the back of the hall, and the hall let him go, and Adrian stood in front of a salt-tasting hearth and found that his eyes had gone hot, which was a stupid reaction, and that he was glad of it, and that he did not trust it.

And then a woman who had been standing very still on the end of the third bench for forty minutes said, in the local language:

"Does the seat do anything about the water?"

Her name was Wenna Cray and she was of Silt Row, and she had walked down from the Reach on Wednesday night, and she was the first person who had come into that hall who had not come to look at a wall.

"My lower field is under water now," she said, while Tamsin was still catching up. "Not last year's. Not the spring one. This afternoon. It came up over the ditch while the man in the seat was reading out his terms. I am not asking for a season. I am asking whether anybody in this room knows that it is still happening, because the water has been coming up since Wednesday morning and it has not stopped, and I have asked four people and all four of them said *the man in the book will know*."

Vail had the chalk out before she had finished translating.

"Name, village, and what you are asking for."

"Wenna Cray. Silt Row. I am asking whether the holder of the pull is going to do anything at all this week, or whether I should go home and start telling people he is a name in a book."

The hall made a noise Adrian had never heard two hundred people make, which was not agreement and not disapproval, and which he would spend the rest of his life failing to describe except as *the sound a room makes when it has found the one person it can be angry at*.

"Entered," Vail said. "Page one, against the name, with the hour. And I am going to read the entry back in a clerk's voice without softening it, and the man it is entered against may not edit it." She dipped the chalk and added four words. "And that he was asked in public."

"That is the queue," said Mina Okoye, later, at the back of the hall, in English, flatly, not looking at him. "Two hundred and nine people in this room were waiting for a decision. She is the first one who has come to the counter. There will be a great many more, and they will come to you and not to the book, and the book is the only thing that stops you being a man with a queue."

"You do not know yet," Mina said, when he opened his mouth. "You have known things for three days."

---

And then a docker of about sixty in a felt hat got up in the third row and made everybody wait, which took a certain amount of doing.

She was Nell Bray. She had held a join in a flood on Thursday and then held the anchor-side pattern of a working for eleven minutes while the man in the book stood in the rain saying *four inches left, and lower*, and she had signed her name on a page a hundred and forty people had watched her sign.

"Before the man in the seat says anything else," she said in the local language, and then again in English, because she had noticed the room and made a decision about it in about a second and a half, "I am going to say the thing about the seat, because he does not know it, and he is going to be asked about it every week for as long as he lives."

"The notary—"

"The notary has said she does not construe. I am not a notary. This is not a ruling. It is the oldest thing I know and it is going in front of the man in a sentence whether he likes it or not."

She turned round and looked at the seat, which was a gap of floor between the long table and the third bench with nothing at all in it.

"A seat is a place to be angry at. That is all it is. It is not an office. There is no office. There is a place in the room where a man stands, and the standing is not the point, and the being important is not the point. And if somebody comes in that hall in six weeks with a complaint about a seam, he does not want to argue with four dockers and a mason about whether a stone is warm. He wants somebody to be *in it*. He wants one name. And the reason we sign four is not so that we can hold it. It is so that when the thing goes wrong there are four people who have to be angry, and not one."

She looked at Tamsin, and at Roke at the side wall with his slate.

"You four will be the ones he comes for. Not him. Never him. We will have to be the ones who say *it was right* or *it was wrong*, and one of us will say wrong, and one of us will say right, and if we all agree we have not been asked, we have agreed, and then the man in the seat is decoration."

Then she said the sentence Adrian was still turning over at ten past one that afternoon, in an empty salt store, with nobody to explain it to:

"A seat has to be legible to a person who does not like you. Anything else is a chair."

Adrian stood in the gap in the floor with nothing in it and felt something give way in his chest, and it was not despair.

He had braced for the seat to be a small humiliation. He had made his peace with a small humiliation at four o'clock that morning in the rain, written it down, and had a plan for surviving it. What was actually in the floor was a thing he had spent nine years building *against*, in rooms where a designer who could not be fired was the most dangerous person in the company. The reason any of it had ever worked was that anybody could point at it.

The queue, the nineteen-second entry, Brann coming the length of the hall, a woman from Silt Row asking whether it was going to do anything: it was the first time since Tuesday that something had happened to him that he could not have designed. It was not worse, and it was not better, and he did not have a word for it at all, which for a man who had made his living for nine years out of having a word for things was the whole of the trouble.

---

The thing he did with the rest of the morning was so small that nobody thanked him, including himself.

He wrote the first inspection date.

Not the procedure, not the roster, not the four names. Those belonged to Renna Ord's arithmetic and to Ovin Sarr's two hours before the turn, and Adrian understood by half past eleven that the only thing he had that nobody else in the room had was the ability to *commit a city to a day in front of a witness*, because the seat was the only thing in that salt store with his signature on it.

It took four minutes. He had Kerrow check the day against the tide, which Kerrow did from a table he had been carrying for six weeks and which Adrian did not get to see. He wrote the day in his own terrible hand, in Latin letters, and had it read out in the local language by a man standing in the aisle. The day was a Saturday six days off, at low water, and there was a week to arrange it, because Renna Ord's arithmetic does not happen in a morning and she had told him so in front of witnesses. It was the first turn the watch could take without a man standing in it twice, and the day after that on which the labour shift fell due from the gate crew — and the reason both were true was that Renna Ord had not been lying: the order was known, and the order was older than the page.

Then Vail read it back and asked whether he accepted that the inspection was the *whole* of the use of the instrument that week.

"Yes. And I want the day written in the cost column too, because the cost column has a labour shift on it and the shift is the same day, and the four witnesses are the four men who would otherwise be on the wall. I want a man from the wall to read the word *shift* in a book in eleven villages."

Marek Osley wrote it in.

That was the whole of it. Four minutes of writing on a Friday morning, and afterwards two hundred and eleven people went home, and the page had a date in it, and nothing in the world had opened. Adrian stood in an empty salt store at ten past one with a numb ear, a headache, a notebook, and a queue.

He wrote the last entry of the day at the top of a clean page, in the flat hand:

*Saturday, low water: first inspection. It is a date in a book, not a door. Nothing has opened. Wenna Cray of Silt Row will be back on Friday, and the Friday after, and I have no answer for her and I am not going to invent one, and the not-inventing is going to be the whole of what I do this week, and it is going to look from outside like doing nothing.*

*Brann is right and I could not have got it out of him. Nell Bray is right and I did not know it. Vask was right on Wednesday. And the only man in this city who has never once been right about me is me.*

*Queue: one, and rising. I am the counter now. Do not let that become a chair.*

---

He was two steps from the door when somebody at the back of the hall, on the last bench by the wall, in a wet coat that had not dried since Tuesday, said four words to the man beside him in a low ordinary voice, and the second half of the name in it was not a Veyran sound at all.

Tamsin, who was beside him, turned round. So did he.

The man on the end of the last bench was about sixty, salt-stained to the elbow, with the hands of a man who has counted things for forty years. He was looking at the man in the good coat beside him, and he was not looking for a fight, and he had no idea he had said anything.

He said it again, and then he said the rest of it, in the local language, in the tone of a man repeating something he had heard twice in two days and had never been asked where he got it.

"Shah. Priya Shah. That is the one who writes everything down."

Priya was sitting on the end of the fourth bench, eight feet away, with a notebook open on her knee and a pencil in her hand, and she had not understood one word of it, and she looked up anyway, because six people in a strange country had spent four days learning to look up when a room made a sound.

She did not go white at first. She went still, in a way Adrian had seen her do exactly once before, on Wednesday, when somebody had asked her to read a line off a wall to four local adults and she had understood at the same moment as they did that it was a wall.
