# Chapter 0017 — The First Dissent

The inspection did not happen on the Saturday, and by Wednesday the town had made a meal of it.

It had not happened because Ovin Sarr had been in the yard under the chapel with a bar and a lantern and two masons from dawn until dark, and three of the four witnesses had gone to help him find out whether a void under a flagstone was a drain, and because the turn on Saturday was an hour out, and because a woman at Nine Elms had sent word up the river on Friday night that the Reach water was two inches high and rising and would be two inches high and rising at the turn.

Nobody had prevented the inspection. That was the part that made the Saturday so bad. There was a date in a book and the date had been kept and the four witnesses had not refused; they had been somewhere else, doing a job the same book had also costed, and Renna Ord's arithmetic had turned out to have a collision in it, and the crowd had walked two hundred steps down a wet stair to look at eighteen inches of dead straight seam in a wall.

About ninety people came on Wednesday. Nine had come on the Saturday, and nine had gone away again within four minutes having concluded, correctly, that a shape in a wall which does not open is less interesting than a market.

Ninety people is not a hearing. Ninety people is a queue for something, and Adrian could feel the shape of it from the walk-round, standing with his hand four inches to the left of the seam with the rain coming sideways, and the whole of the second hour was the town explaining to itself what it had come for.

---

Marek Osley had brought eleven people and a written argument, and the written argument was better than anything Adrian had heard in nine days from anybody who was not paid to write.

He got to the front without pushing, because he was a man of forty-eight with a waxed tablet and the specific authority of somebody holding a piece of paper, and he read from it, and he read it well.

"Item the first. That instrument is written in a book as sitting in a socket in the load-bearing wall of the outer channel of this lock, under a cap, at the foot of a two-hundred-step walk-round with a rail that is half rotten. Item the second. That the wall it is in was scabbed three times in living memory and is carrying a flood. Item the third. That a socket is a shape and that shapes in load-bearing walls have a name in this trade, and the name is *a thing that has let go once*." He looked up. "I have put the cost of it in a book, in a column, with a name against it, and it is eleven measures of opened salt a season out of the Lock's own stores, and I am the man who has to find eleven measures of opened salt every season, and last Thursday I wrote in a book that eleven measures of opened salt go into a shape in a wall that does not open."

He waited, and let them have it, and Adrian was impressed and frightened in roughly equal measure.

"I am not a man who says *it is dangerous* because it is frightening. I am a man who says: a hazard in a wall is a thing you mend, and you mend it by cutting it out and putting good stone in, and I have a mason who will cut it out for eleven measures of salt and four days, and I have had that number since Thursday and nobody in this hall has said the number out loud."

"Cut it out and the wall goes," said Ottoline Vask.

"Then the wall goes and the field is replanted and the season is spent and we are all in a worse position than we are." Osley was entirely calm. "I have written down what it costs to cut it out. I have not written down what it costs to leave it in, because nobody will give me that number, and I have asked four people and every one of them has told me they do not know, and I am telling this hall that a man keeping a count is not permitted to carry a hazard of unknown size in eleven villages for a year while waiting for somebody who knows."

"You are permitted to write down that you do not know," said Tamsin, in the local language. "That is what a count is for."

"Yes," Osley said. "And when I have written it down eleven times in eleven villages, it will still be a number I do not have, and in the spring somebody will cut it out. I am not the villain in this. I am the man who is going to have to answer for it in March."

---

Jonah Pike was the only person on the walk-round who said the other thing, and he said it after Osley had finished, and he said it to the crowd and not to the room, and he had thought about it, because Jonah always had thought about it by the time he opened his mouth.

"Can I say the thing that is true and is not in anybody's argument," he said, in English, and then, when about forty people looked blank, in the local language, slowly, which he had practised three times that week in front of Sesh Brack and which was still not good and which was the reason Tamsin stopped him twice and let him finish the third time.

"I want to say the route is worth something. I know that is the impudent thing to say on a day like this, in this weather, with that man in a good coat reading out his column. So: the route is worth something. Not to me. I want to say that to the man in the seat, and to the four of them, and not to this crowd, because I am not talking to this crowd, I am talking to the wall.

"You have got a shape in a wall that one man put his name into, with four witnesses beside it and a cost in a column and a woman in the Reach who has said no in a book in her own word. That is the only door in this country." He put his hands in his pockets. "I have spent thirty years arranging for people to be fed and housed and safe in places that had no door in them, and every single time, the thing that goes wrong last is that somebody can turn up with a warrant and a wagon. There is no warrant in this. There is no wagon. There is a man with a mark, and a name, and a thing he has to be asked about, and four people who can stop him, and a village that can say no. In nine years one of you is going to build a gate somewhere in this river, and it is going to be built in this seam or it is going to be built in a fight, and I would like it built in the seam, in a piece of ground where everybody who is affected is already written down by name."

"Who is going to build a gate," said Renna Ord.

"Someone. Not me, and not this week, and that is the part I want understood. Somebody." Jonah shrugged. "The reason I say it is that Osley is right about the number he does not have, and I want it noted on the same day that there is also a number he has not asked for, which is: what is a gate worth when there is no gate and no price on it and no list. It is worth nothing. It is worth a fight. And I have never in my life got anybody fed in a place where it was worth a fight."

Nobody answered him, and Adrian understood that in about nine days' time somebody would, and that it would be a Crown clerk, and that Jonah Pike would not be the one who got the credit for having said it first, because the whole of Jonah's life had been a demonstration of the fact that being right early and being right publicly is worth about a third of what being right with a warrant is worth.

---

The nine households of Nine Elms sent Ilda Marsh and Petra Oake, and Ilda Marsh was seventy and had wet reed-grass in the hem of her coat on the second consecutive day, and Petra Oake was thirty-one and had walked up from the Reach in the rain and had not been a part of the Chapter 10 reading and now said the sentence that Tamsin had told Adrian about in the rain on the Friday morning, eleven days ago, in almost those words.

"Our refusal was given at a hearing."

Adrian felt Tamsin go very still beside him.

"A hearing is a thing that happens," said Petra Oake, in the local language, and Tamsin translating, and her voice was better than Tamsin's, which was humiliating and fair. "A commitment is a thing that stands. On the Friday morning you read our words into a book and you read them and everybody was pleased, and it has been eleven days, and in eleven days nothing has used the water, so the refusal has cost us nothing, and I would like somebody in this hall to tell me what the difference is between a thing we said once in a room and a thing we have agreed to keep saying, and then I will tell you which of the two we meant."

The hall was quiet. The rain did the rest.

"It is the same thing," Adrian started.

"It is not," said Tamsin, in English, to nobody, and then, in the local language, to the hall, with her hands on the rail: "It is not the same thing and she is right, and I told him it would come, and I told him on Friday morning in the rain in about these words and he wrote it in a notebook, and here it is on a Saturday two weeks after he was handed a seat."

---

What Adrian did next took eleven seconds and it is the decision he is going to have to defend for a year.

"Put it in the book," he said, in English, first, to Tamsin, and then he said it again in the local language, badly, with his hand out at Petra Oake, and Sera Vail got the slate round him.

"Not as it stands. Enter it this: *The refusal of the nine households of Nine Elms was given at a hearing and has not yet been given as a commitment, and the holder of the pull has said that a refusal of unknown standing is not a refusal he can rely on, and that this instrument will not be used at all until those nine households have spoken it as a commitment in front of this notary, and that from the moment they do, it stands until they withdraw it, on any day, without argument.*"

Petra Oake looked at him.

"And I am going to say the rest of it out loud," Adrian said, "because you did not come here to be managed. The rest of it is that if you do that, the instrument stops for good. Not for a season. For good, until a receiving community accepts the whole of the area, and there is no reason in the world to expect one to do that, and I have wanted a door in this wall since Tuesday at four o'clock in the morning in another hemisphere and I am telling the nine of you that your refusal is the reason I am not going to get one, and I would rather say that out loud on a walk-round than have it be a thing somebody works out in a month and is angry about."

"Enter that," said Vail, to Petra Oake, "or do not. I am writing what he said. I am not writing what you said until you say it."

And Petra Oake said it. It took about forty seconds and it was in her own words and it was not the words on the Friday page, and Vail wrote all of it, and at the end of it Ilda Marsh said one sentence that made about ninety people on a walk-round in the rain go quiet, which was:

"We said no on Friday so that you would have to come and ask us a second time. Nobody has ever asked us a second time."

---

And then the Concord's assessor of strangers, who had walked two hundred steps down a wet stair that morning with a waxed slate and had a great deal of trouble getting back up it, asked Adrian the question that everybody in Saltwake had been asking since Friday and had not yet had an answer to, in the exact words Tamsin had predicted on the night the black line opened in the chapel stone.

It was not a grand question. He had written it out beforehand and read it out, in English, and then in the local language, from the slate, so that nobody could be helped by the speed of it.

"Sera Vail will read back. The instrument in this wall is entered in the book of this city with a holder, four witnesses, a receiving community, and a cost. It cannot be used. It will not be used this week. The question I am required to put is this, and it is the only question I have been asked to put, and it is a fair question and it has been fair since the day you came out of the wall: *what is the Narrow Mark for?*"

The whole walk-round waited for it.

And Adrian looked for the pale field, because he had looked for it every day for a fortnight, and it was not there. Nothing in the air, nothing at the edge of his vision, no form, no consequence, no choice. He had begun, on Wednesday, to expect it the way you expect a lift, and it did not come, and he found that the not-coming was louder than anything it had ever said, because the field had been in the room on Friday morning and Saturday morning and Monday night, and now nine people were asking it a question in public and it had nothing whatever to say about it.

---

But he had prepared for it, because he had known for eleven days that it was coming, and because he had spent two nights on it, and because he had written and rewritten it in the notebook eleven times and had crossed out nine of the drafts and had deliberately kept all of the crossed-out ones, which is the most Adrian Vale thing he had ever done, and which he noticed himself doing, and which nobody except Tamsin ever noticed.

He had written a one-sentence version. He had a good one. It was a *sentence*, it was *true*, and it would have made the whole of the rest of his life in this city simpler, and it would have been the fourteenth thing in three weeks that had been made out of a man wanting a thing to be legible.

He said it out loud instead, in the local language, slowly, and Vask repeated every third sentence in English for the record, and it took two minutes and eleven seconds and it was the worst two minutes of Adrian's month.

---

> I am the holder of the pull. I am not the person who fills the seat and I do not choose the people in the room. I came out of a wall in this city on the first morning of the flood and the wall did not ask me, and I do not know what I am for. That is the first part of it and it is the whole of the first part.
>
> What I can tell you is what it is *not* for, and I will say those things out loud because I have been asked and because a man who only says no is half an answer.
>
> It is not for me. I have no mark to go home on. I have had one since the first week and it is in a wall. There is no road. There has not been one road the whole time I have been in this city and nobody has lied to me about that except me, in my own head, at four o'clock in the morning, for about a week.
>
> It is not for the six people I came out with. It is not a road. It is one name per opening and it cannot be used, and I want that said in this order, and I want it said by me.
>
> It is a way of costing a thing. That is what the last two days have taught me and I did not want to be taught it. It is a place in a book where eleven measures of salt and a season of a field and a day a month of four men's time can be written down with a name against them, in a room, in front of people, so that when something goes wrong there is somebody to be angry at and it is not a mistake.
>
> That is all I know. I do not know whether the mark I gave into that wall on the eighth day could have brought anybody home. Nobody will ever be able to tell me, because it is in the stone, and I put it there in front of witnesses, and I could have taken it back, and I did not, and I would like it in the book that I understood at the time that I could have taken it back and I could have got away with it.
>
> I do not know what it is for. I know that it is not a door, it is not a rescue, it is not a receipt, and it is a seat that a person can be angry at, and I would like to be permitted to keep the seat and to be examined about it for as long as the city wants, and I would like to be asked that question again in a month, and again in a year, and I would like somebody in this hall to write down that the man in the book does not know what the mark is for, in case a man in a good coat ever comes back and tells one of you that it is for the road.
>
> That is the whole of my answer and it is not an answer anybody wants. I have been the man in this city for ten days and I have found out one thing and it is that I do not know.

---

The hall did not like it.

That was the first thing, and it was not hostile; it was the sound of ninety people finding out at the same moment that the thing they had come to look at was not a thing, and then deciding, collectively and in about four seconds, to be disappointed instead of angry, which is what crowds do and which Adrian found much harder to sit in than anger.

The second thing was that nobody left.

A young docker at the front said, in the local language, loudly, "Then what is the point of it." And Owen Park, who had come down the walk-round at ten with his scalp freshly dressed and had said almost nothing for two hours, said, in English, in a completely level voice:

"He was asked a question and he said he does not know. That is the first true thing anybody has said on this wall in a fortnight. I want it noted that the man who has been standing here with a stone in his hand for two hours did not make one up, and I want it noted by whoever is writing, and I want it noted by the six of us, because in about a month one of us is going to tell a story about Saturday when the inspection did not happen, and I want the story to be accurate."

"Because you are right and it is expensive," Adrian said, quietly, and Owen looked at him, and neither of them said anything else, and it was the first time since the flood that Owen Park had been on Adrian's side of anything, and Adrian did not enjoy it as much as he had expected to, which he wrote down.

---

Vail did the other thing before she did that one, and she did it on the walk-round, in the rain, with a slate and a chain hooked through a ring in her belt, because the last inspection had been performed at eight forty in the morning and the record of it had to be made where it had happened.

"Inspection of the seam called the Narrow Mark," she said, and read it out, and the rain went into it. "Wednesday. The fourth witnessing group of the instrument: Tamsin Quill, Ovin Sarr, Nell Bray, Hettis Roke, each present and each signing for himself. The seam was viewed, sounded along, and compared by the masons of this city against the entry made when the fragment was set in it. It is not narrower. It is not wider. The lip is as it was entered. The standing of the instrument is therefore satisfied for the period, in that a fresh inspection has been performed within the term and recorded, and the instrument remains entered, posted, costed, signed, and unusable."

"That is the technicality," said Kerrow, from somewhere behind her, flatly.

"That is not a technicality, that is the term," said Vail. "It requires a fresh inspection before any further use. There has been a fresh inspection. Therefore the condition is met, and the instrument is alive, and I am obliged to keep it in the book, and the reason I am telling this crowd what keeps it alive is that about forty of you came here to find out whether a shape in a wall can be got rid of by standing next to it, and it cannot. It is not mine to scrap. It is in a book with a cost in it, and the only people who can take it out of the book are the four who signed it and the people it is in, and neither of those has said one word about taking anything out." She dipped the chalk. "And the first inspection of this instrument was not performed. The term says a fresh inspection, and the Saturday's was not performed, because three of the four witnesses were in a yard under a chapel with a bar and a lantern and the turn was an hour out. I am entering that as a fact and not as a fault, for the reason I entered the gate as a fact and not a fault on Thursday, which is that a book which only records failures is a book about a bad year."

"So the Saturday is in the book," said Roke.

"The Saturday is in the book," said Vail, "and the crowd that came and found nothing is in the book, and the fact that nine of them came and ninety came today is in the book, and none of it scrapes anything, and I am sorry, and I am not going to pretend otherwise."

---

Sera Vail read the answer back into the ledger, all of it, both languages, in a clerk's voice, and the reading took nine minutes, and while she read it the sky over the outer channel went the colour of a wet slate and the crowd thinned.

And then, at the back of the walk-round, on the wall side, up out of the rain, in a coat that had been repaired in about nine places, a woman of about sixty with grey hair tied in a knot at the back of her neck said a thing to the Hearthguard on the step beside her, in the local language, and the Hearthguard turned round and looked at her, and Adrian felt the crowd stop the way a crowd stops when a dog gets up in a room.

She came forward. She was small and she was not a reed woman and she was not dressed for a walk-round, and she had come down two hundred wet steps in a coat that had been repaired nine times to look at a seam in a wall.

"My name is Sabra Vint," she said, in English, which she had learned in a schoolroom in a way that was forty years out of date and completely legible. "I live on the upper road, above the salt works, in the house with the burnt lintel. I have not been down here in nine years and I came down today because a man in a market said the number nineteen out loud, in front of a stall, on Wednesday, and I have been waiting nine years for one person in this city to say it twice."

"What number," said Roke.

"Nineteen," said Sabra Vint. "And I want to say the rest of it here and not in a market, because I have watched a thing like this get started in a market.

"There are nineteen stones on the upper road, above the ordinary ground, in a line, and there are eleven graves in the ordinary ground behind the ordinary wall, and eight of those eleven have no stone. I have known since I was thirty-one years old that and I have never in nine years been able to make nineteen and eight be one number, and I have tried, and I have got nineteen stones on the upper road and eleven graves in the ordinary ground behind the ordinary wall, and eight of those eleven have no stone, and I have written the shapes of those nineteen stones on a sheet of paper every year of my life, because there is no writing on them that any living person in this city can read, and the paper is the only copy there is." She had the paper. Of course she had the paper; it was in a waxed case in the inside of her coat and she had it out and folded in four before she had finished the sentence, the way you do with a thing you have carried a long way.

"I have come to look at the nineteen stones," she said. "Not this. Yours. Somebody else said those, in a market, on Wednesday, and I have been down here for two hours and I have looked at a wall instead, and I would like somebody in this room to tell me whether a man who reads a wall for a living might one day read nineteen stones, and if the answer is no then I am going back up those steps, and I am going to have said it in a room, and that is what I came for."
