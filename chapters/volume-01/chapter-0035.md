# Chapter 0035 — A Mark for a Man Who Asked

Ceri Vaughan was thirty-six and he held the second gate below Long Water and he went out at about the ninth hour of the night of the thirty-sixth day of the flood to open it, because the water was not where it was and a household at Long Water could not promise its gate and a household that cannot promise its gate does not keep its share of the water, and he went out to keep a promise, and he has not come back.

---

The boy came up the market at about the eleventh hour at night and Adrian was in the loft and Tamsin was in the loft and Kirsty Lund was asleep on a bench with a school exercise book under her arm, and the boy said a name and Adrian wrote it down before he asked what the name was.

**Ceri Vaughan**, thirty-six, of Long Water, gate-hand at the second gate below Long Water, out at about the ninth hour to open it, not back at the eleventh hour, boat on the bank, lamp not on the bank, and there is a boy of nine at Long Water who saw him go.

"That is the second gate," said Tamsin.

"That is the second gate."

"Osten Fenn holds the second gate at Long Water and Osten Fenn is twenty-two and he is in Saltwake because he entered a statement of error against this city's book on the fourth day of this flood and was right, and he is asleep upstairs in the Salt Weavers' chapel over about nine of the eleven." She was already putting her coat on. "Where is Renna Ord."

"She went up the Reach road this morning with four men and a boat because a miller sent a message to the well-head, and she has not come back."

"Then there is nobody." Tamsin stopped with one arm in a sleeve. "Adrian. There is no man in this city free at the eleventh hour at night to go up a road eleven miles and look for a gate-hand in the reed at Long Water, because every man in this city who knows those paths is on a gate, and the reed shift has been doubled since the twenty-seventh and the doubling was paid for out of a crew that was already in a book."

"I know."

"Then say the other thing."

"The other thing is that there is a shape in a floor four feet from where you are standing that eleven people came out of on the twenty-fifth and one of them went into on the twenty-eighth and came back in eleven seconds, and I have said out loud in a hall to three hundred and ten people that nobody was being fetched and nobody is going to be sent." He put the notebook down. "And I have said *I do not know* about what is on the other side of it in a room over a boat-yard, and I have not got a sentence, and I have had four days."

---

Nobody went up the road that night. That is entered in the book because three people asked for it in public over the following two days and the book says, in the words of Sera Vail: *that no person in this city went up the Reach road in search of Ceri Vaughan on the night of the thirty-sixth day, and that this was not because no person offered, and that the entry is entered because in about four months somebody is going to say that a road was available and that it was not used, and that on the night in question the road was available and was not used, and that the reason is four hours long and it is in this book on the thirty-seventh and thirty-eighth days.*

The reason is four hours long and it is this: Renna Ord came back at about the fifth hour of the afternoon of the thirty-seventh, having been at the well-head at Long Water since the Tuesday morning, and she came into the hall in a wet boat-coat at about the sixth hour and gave the number, and the number is eleven, and the eleven is the number of men who know the reed paths between the second gate and Four Elms, and eight of them are on a gate and cannot come off without a gate going unpromised, and three of them have been on a gate for two days and have not slept, and the doubling of the reed shift came out of a crew that was already in a book in this city on the second week of this flood, and a man in a good coat is not a reed cutter.

And the second thing she said, at the same time, in the same room, is the sentence that is in the book at the sixth hour of the thirty-seventh day and that Owen Park read out of the book twice in the next two days:

"You have a thing in this city that a man can go through. You have had it since the fourth day. It does not go to Long Water, because a man cannot be in two places, and I have watched a man try to be in two places in my life and it is not a thing that works." She put her hands flat on the table. "And the thing I want said out loud in this room, today, by somebody who is not me, is that a man who goes into a shape in a wall in Saltwake is not going to come out of it four miles up the Reach road with a gate-hand under his arm. **Whatever is on the other side of that floor, it is not the reed.** And everybody in this room knows it, and everybody in this room is going to go on knowing it, and about four of us are going to say it in the next two days, and the reason the rest of us are not going to say it is that a woman of thirty-four is sitting in a house at Long Water with a boy of nine."

---

The woman had been to a room on the Wednesday.

Adrian found out about it on the Thursday morning, at about the seventh hour, from a boy of nineteen who had been up all night and who came into the yard and said it flatly without any preamble in the voice of a man delivering a document.

"I was in a house at Long Water yesterday from the second hour to the fifth," said Eli Mercer. "I did not go up the road. Rennet Vaughan asked me to come and I went and I have been telling people I went and I want that in my log, and I would like it entered that a man of nineteen went into a house where a man is missing because a woman asked him, and did not offer to go, and did not say the thing everybody thinks I said."

"What did you say."

"I said what I have been saying since the twenty-eighth, which is that I do not speak for anybody and that I have nothing here." Eli Mercer was standing under the arch with his hands in his pockets and he had not slept. "And then I sat there for three hours and I did not say one useful thing, and at about the fourth hour a man in a good coat came in."

Adrian put the notebook down on the trestle.

"He had been there before me," said Eli Mercer. "He came at about the third hour and she knew him, which is the part I want you to hear first, because she **knew him** — she knew his name, and she had met him at a market in the second week, and she asked him in and made him sit down and there was a table with a paper on it, and he did not put the paper on the table for about forty minutes. He asked her what her husband was like when he was cross. He asked her whether the boy of nine had said anything. He asked her what the second gate below Long Water was for, and she said it was for letting the top water off into the low cut, and he wrote that down in a little book, and then he asked her how she knew a man was drowned."

"And then what did he say."

"He said: *there is a place in this city where eleven people came out of a floor eleven days ago and I was one of the four who went in on the day after and I came back in eleven seconds, and there is a mark in this world on a living person, and the mark can be set again, and it has been set once and it cost a season of a field and four men's day, and it can be set again lawfully, with witnesses, in a yard, properly, and it would take about forty minutes and it would cost something and I do not know what and it is not a cheap thing to do to the man who carries it.*"

Eli Mercer stopped.

"And then he said: *and I want to be careful about one thing, and I am going to say it now and not on Friday. A man who goes into that shape does not come out of it in the reed. I do not know what is on the other side of that floor. Nobody in this city knows. But you should know before anybody asks you, because a person in a house with a boy of nine can be told that a place is a place by a man in a good coat who has read about it in a book, and I have read that book and I have been in the room and I know the four openings and the size of them and there is nobody in it.*"

"He said that," said Adrian.

"He said that to me, in a kitchen, with her in the room, before he said anything else to her about it." Eli Mercer took his hands out of his pockets. "And I want to say the other half. He was in that kitchen for an hour and ten minutes and he asked her eleven questions and answered none of them, and at the end of it he said *I will come back on Friday*, and he came back on the Wednesday afternoon, and he came back because I was not in the room and she had told a man at a gate, and he said that to me in the yard on Thursday morning in about nine words, and he said: *that is what I do. I go back to a house a second time. I have been doing it for eight weeks and nobody has ever made me say what it is.*"

"Write that down," said Adrian.

"It is written down. It is in Priya's log in my words and in my handwriting and I have asked for it to be read out, and I am asking now, in this yard, with about forty people in it, and there is a man of nineteen standing here who has been eleven times in a book and has never once in a book been accused of anything, and I would like the reading to be this afternoon." He put his hands back in his pockets. "And I would like somebody to ask him the question, because I cannot."

---

The meeting was at the third hour of the afternoon of the thirty-seventh day of the flood and it was in the yard because the yard is the room where a thing gets said in public by whoever is nearest, and it was not a meeting, and Vail entered it as a yard and not as a sitting, and about two hundred and forty people stood in it in the rain.

Owen Park asked to be in it. He had not asked to be in anything for a month. He had been in a yard about nine times in a fortnight and had stood at the top of a stair and had walked a channel and had not said a word in a public room since the twenty-eighth, and on the thirty-seventh day of the flood he came down the market at about the second hour with a bag over his shoulder and a bad coat and a right arm in a sling he had put on himself on the Monday, and stood at the edge of the yard with about two hundred and forty people looking at him and said:

"I went up the road this morning. I went to Long Water and I walked eleven miles and I came back and I carried two sacks of flour up from the road for that house and I did not say anything about the mark, which I have, which you do not know about, and I am going to tell you about in a minute because I am not going to use it as a way in. I asked that woman what she needed and she said flour and a hand with the second gate. I am not able to do the second gate with this arm and I did not offer and I am not going to offer, and I have not said one word to that woman about a wall."

He shifted the bag.

"Here is the whole of it and it is about eleven sentences and I have had it since about four o'clock yesterday morning. I have held the only other one in this world for nine days and I went into a wall with it on the twenty-eighth and I came out in eleven seconds and everybody saw me and I told a woman of sixty-one at the bottom of a stair that I was not looking for her granddaughter because I had not been asked, and that is the only thing I have done in a month that I am proud of.

"And a man in a good coat has been to a house at Long Water twice and has not told that woman that the other side of that floor is a room eleven people stood in, and everybody in this yard has had that in their hands for a week and nobody has said it to her.

"**I am asking to be sent.** Not for the Reed. Not to Long Water, because a man who goes into a wall in Saltwake is not a man who comes out of it in the reed and every person in this yard knows that and I have said it twice. **For the other side of the floor.** Because if there is a person on the other side of it then it is a place, and if it is a place then it can be looked at, and if it can be looked at then in about nine months a person is going to be lost somewhere in this Reach and somebody is going to say *well, there is a room*, and they will be right, and today I would like to be the man who goes first and comes back and says what is in it, in front of two hundred and forty people, and then nobody ever has to lie to anybody's mother again."

Nobody in that yard said anything for about half a minute, which in a yard of two hundred and forty in the rain is a long time.

---

There were three objections and all three of them were right and all three of them were outvoted.

**Hesper Saye** came first, and she came first because she came first at the review, and she said it in about nine sentences in the local language and Tamsin translated it and it is in the book in her own words.

"I said *no* in a yard on the twenty-seventh and I put my name against it in front of a family and I was right. I will say no again and I will be wrong, because this time there is a man of thirty-six in a reed at Long Water and the last time there was a girl of eight who was not. So hear my no and then hear the rest of it, because I am not going to stand in a yard and stop a thing and say nothing else.

"His right shoulder went on the twenty-fifth and cannot be set and he will not lift forty pounds with that arm for the rest of his life and he will not know that he is doing it, and about one time in nine he will find out, and I have said that to four hundred men and been wrong about ninety of them. A mark is set in a hand. Which hand. He has not said and I have not asked and I am not going to ask in a yard.

"And his head opened on the first day of this flood and has not closed in thirty-six days and I have a word for that and the word is *unclosed* and I said it in a porch to nine people in the second week and I have not taken it back and it is not a wound that is not killing him. It is a wound that something is standing in. And I do not know what and neither does he and neither does anybody in this city, and a man with a mark in his hand is a man with a place where a thing can stand in a person." She put the bowl down on the trestle. "That is my no. It is not a reason against him. It is a description of him."

**Renna Ord** came second, and she came second because she had been on a road for two days and had come back at the fifth hour and had slept about three hours, and she was the plainest authority in that yard and both of them knew it.

"You are asking to be sent into a room," she said, "and nobody in this yard has asked you a single question about the room, and I have asked you eleven about the reed.

"You have not told this yard what you would do if you went and could not come back. You have not told them what you would bring back. You have not told them how anybody would know that you were not in there. You have told them that you would come back and say what is in it, and there is no man in this city who can promise a person that, and I have watched about nine men promise it in my life and about four of them were telling the truth when they promised it.

"And here is the part I am going to say and then I am going to sit down. You have spent four men in a month. A mason over lime. A boy with a flask of tea. A mason and a boy and a man from Silt Row and a mason, that is four, and the fourth was a reed cutter of forty-four on Monday and he has seen a light and cannot say a sentence about it and is on a page in this city's book because you asked him politely in a doorway over a barrow. That is what spending a man is. It is not a punishment. It is a hole in a shift, and it does not close, and the next time I need that man he is gone for nine days and everybody knows it.

"So: no. And if this yard votes yes then I will take my barrow up the road at four in the morning with forty men who have not slept, and I will be there for the four hours, and it will be the fourth time anybody has done that in a month for the same reason, and the man I am doing it for will be the man in this yard." She shouldered nothing; she had not brought it. "And I want that entered in the terms if it happens. My name, and the four hours."

**Mina Okoye** was last, and she did it in a room, at about the sixth hour of the evening, with Owen Park, and it took about nine minutes and there are four sentences of it in the book and Adrian has never been able to write about it except in these terms.

"You told me in a doorway in a yard on the twenty-fifth day of this flood that a hold is a working without a witness and the residue of one goes into the person who did it. I said it. You wrote it down. I have watched about nine people in this city use that sentence since and I would like to say what I have learned about it, which is that it is the truest sentence any of us produced in a month and it has stopped nobody, and everybody who uses it uses it as a description of a thing they have already decided to do.

"You are going to hold a mark by yourself, in a floor, in the dark. That is the same operation with a worse arm and a better sentence. I have no medical objection. I have a medical objection and I am going to make it and you are going to agree with it and it is going to make no difference, and I want both of those facts in a book together so that nobody can later say that a woman with tweezers did not do this part."

"I know," said Owen Park.

"You do not know. I have not told you the part. I am not going to, because you would use it." He looked at the floor. "Then I will tell you the part that is not medical. The thing about your head is that it has been open for thirty-six days and the reason it has not closed is a thing with a witness on it, and I have spent a month watching you not tell anybody, and the reason you have not told anybody is that a person who has told nobody cannot be asked what he saw.

"And on Thursday morning at about the fourth hour you are going to go into a place, and I do not know what comes out of a place, and I do not know what a mark in a hand is for, and I do not know whether a head that has not closed for thirty-six days can be closed by a thing coming the other way. And I am going to stand in this room and not say any of it, because a man has asked in front of two hundred and forty people to be sent somewhere, and a woman in this room is not going to be the reason he is not."

---

The count was taken in the yard at about the seventh hour of the evening of the thirty-seventh day of the flood and it is the second count in the history of the Lantern Concord and the first one that was not a count of the whole hall, and it is entered with the reason and the hour and the fact that about two hundred people did not stand in a line about it.

It is entered as: **for the establishment of a temporary return mark and a single first passage, one hundred and forty-one; against, sixty-four; abstentions and not-standing, not counted, thirty-one.** And the entry says, in the notary's words: *and that the count was taken of persons who wished to express it, and that about thirty-five persons were in this yard and were not asked, and that this is entered because the form of the count is the form of the count of the thirtieth day and this is the second time this city has counted its own opinion and both counts are wrong in the same direction and I do not yet know in what direction.*

---

The mark was set at about the eleventh hour of the night of the thirty-seventh day of the flood, in the low room by the salt wharf, in about forty minutes, by four people.

**Hesper Saye**, who does no private work, and who set it having said *no* in a yard four hours earlier and having said in the same yard that her no was a description and not a reason against him.

**Tamsin Quill**, at the anchor side, doing the one thing in this city Adrian can never do and has never once pretended he could.

**Hettis Roke**, on the step, with a waxed slate, saying the hour, as he said it in a gallery on the twenty-fifth.

**Peth Lomas**, fifty, a mason, who read a keeper's board out loud in a yard two days ago and put his own name under a rule.

The cost is entered and Adrian is not going to describe it here because it was not his, and it is this: the standing labour in the yard doubled for the day, from two to four; a season of the bottom field, entered against the instrument, being the **fifth** entry of a season of that field in this book in a month; and the day of four men's time that the fresh inspection of the twenty-seventh came out of, entered for the second time.

It is entered, in the notary's hand, with a note, and the note is in her own words and she read it out twice:

> **And that the cost of the fourth item is entered for the second time and the reason it is entered for the second time is that it has been taken twice, and that this is the fifth time a season of the same field has been entered in this book in a month, and that a cost that has been entered five times is not a cost any more, it is a notation, and I have entered it five times and I am the notary and it is my column, and I am not going to say anything about it in a yard tonight because I am going to be asked to read something else in a yard tonight that I am not going to be able to read.**

The mark went into the heel of Owen Park's right hand, under the two inches of nothing, and it is the second return mark ever set in the world and the first one ever set on a person who was not going through on the day it was made.

Which is the whole of what the fourth item of the terms says, and Adrian wrote it, and Vail read it, and it is the sentence he has asked to have read back to him in a year by a person who does not like him:

> **That the mark is established on the person named, for the person named, and not upon the route; and that the route is continued, unrecognised, and may not be used, and stands in no relation to this mark; and that the two things are not evidence of one another and may not be used as evidence of one another, and that the existence of this mark does not make the instrument a road and the instrument's refusal does not take this mark out of the person's hand.**

Ottoline Vask said thank you to him out loud for that, in front of nine people, at about the twelfth hour, in the yard, in the rain, and she does not say thank you.

"You have entered the difference," she said, in the local language, and then, in English, badly, which she almost never does, "and it is the only thing anybody has done in a month that stops a man turning a thing into a reason, and I want it read back in a year by somebody who does not like you, and I am putting my own name against that, and my name is a mason and I have been in this city thirty-four years and I do not do that."

"Thank you."

"Do not thank me yet," said Ottoline Vask, and went up the steps to read the terms of the next thing, and stopped halfway, with her hand on the arch, and did not turn round, and said:

"Adrian. On Friday you are going to ask me to read six terms in this yard about a rescue, and I am going to say no, and I am going to say no because of something you entered tonight at about the eleventh hour, and it is going to be the fault of a clause in a book that a man with no rights in this city wrote at four in the morning and had read back to him by a notary, and I have thought about that for about nine minutes walking up here and I have not worked out how to be angry at you about it yet, and I will have worked it out by Friday."

---

He asked for it at about the twelfth hour, in the yard, with about twenty people left in the rain, and Adrian was the nearest and should have said the words and did not get to them.

"Adrian."

"You have not had anything to drink."

"I have had water. I want to ask you for a thing and I have never asked anybody for a thing in this world, and I asked you for a field once, on a stair, on a Friday, and you gave me it, and it cost you ninety minutes of standing in the dark failing to see something and you said so in a hall to a woman of sixty who had not asked.

"I am going to ask for the post."

The yard got quieter in a way that Adrian noticed and did not understand until four days later.

"The keeper named it on the nineteenth day of this flood," Owen Park said. "You have been there for every beginning of everything that has happened in this ground since. She said: for as long as the ground is open, the ground does not open without him. Every beginning. He is there or it does not begin. And it is not money and it is not a fee and it is not useful and there is no end of it in sight, and she put it in the local language and he accepted it in English and got the verb wrong and a mason corrected him and let the rest stand.

"I want it for Thursday. I want the ground not to begin without me. I want it in a keeper's hand on a keeper's board in this yard where anybody who comes up here has to read it, and I want it said in a way that says a man is *in* it. Because I have been four men in a month and one of them has seen a light and cannot say a sentence, and the reason that happens is that nobody is answerable for it, and if I am the one the ground is answerable to then nobody can send anybody anywhere without me and there is a man in my hand and a rule on a door and those are two walls instead of nothing."

He said it as a man making a reasonable request about a procedure, which is the most frightening register a person can speak in.

"Do not give it to me because I am frightened," he said. "Give it to me because I am the only man in this city that a thing like that can be given to, and I am the man who asked, and a man who asked is a man who gets it, and if a thing like that is only ever given to people who do not ask then it is not a rule, it is a reward for being the right sort of person, and I have watched that in nine different buildings and it is always the same and it is always a building where nobody can leave."

And Adrian Vale stood in a yard in the rain on the thirty-seventh day of the flood and understood, in about a second and a half, that he was going to give it to him, and that he was going to give it to him because Owen Park had asked, out loud, in a yard, in front of twenty people, and had explained exactly why, and had told him the sentence that says *do not give it to me because I am frightened*.

And he gave it to him.

Nyla Ferrow wrote it on the board at about the twelfth hour of the night of the thirty-seventh day, at the top, above everything, in her own hand, four inches high, and it took her about two minutes and she did not ask him to say it twice:

> **THE GROUND UNDER THIS YARD DOES NOT BEGIN WITHOUT OWEN PARK.**
> **Said by the keeper of this ground on the twelfth day of the new month, at the request of the man named, in a yard, in front of about twenty persons, and not in a room.**
> **It is a rule and it is a man's name on a floor.**

And it came off on the Wednesday, at about the fourth hour of the morning of the thirty-eighth day, on the Sunday after, in daylight, with a cloth and a bucket, and a man in a good coat was there when it came off, and he washed the board himself and did not stop the keeper from washing the other side, and there are about a hundred and forty people in this city who read that sentence and eleven of them can still repeat it, and not one of them knows what it was for.

Ottoline Vask saw it go up. She said nothing about it then, and she said nothing about it at about the fifth hour the next morning, and at about the seventh hour of the evening of the thirty-seventh she went up the two hundred steps of the walk-round to the cap of the old landing with a lamp and a bar, alone, and sounded the outer wall for about forty minutes, and came back down, and did not tell anybody what she was doing there, which was the first of her six rules and the only one she ever broke and she broke it because of a sentence in chalk on a door.
