# Chapter 0037 — The Reach of the Step

The fourth hour of the morning of the thirty-eighth day of the flood is the dark hour and there is no tide in it, and a man said so at the top of two hundred wet steps at about two in the morning, in the rain, in the voice of a man putting a tool down, and about twenty people stood in a yard and heard him and about two hundred and forty read it in a book on the Friday.

---

She put him four feet back at about half past two in the morning, for the fourth time, and this time she told him why, and it was not the reason he had spent four days building.

"You are in the wrong four feet," said Nyla Ferrow. "Come round to the north side. There. That is the four feet you get and there has been four feet for you since the nineteenth day and you have never once asked me what they were for."

"I have assumed they were the lip."

"The lip is nine feet from the arch. You have been standing four feet off the boards since the twentieth day of this flood because the boards over that hole came up flat on the twenty-fifth with a flagstone stood on end and there is a man in this city who has been asking me every day since whether the ground will open while you are standing on it, and I said no, and I am going to keep saying no, and I can only say no to it if you are four feet back." She wiped her hands on her apron. "That is the whole of the first one and I have never given it to you because a man who has been told a thing is a man who has to keep it, and there are about nine people in this city who have to keep things and I would rather be the one who does not."

"And the second reason."

"The second reason is that there is one place in this yard you are allowed to be, and I gave it to you on the nineteenth day of this flood in the local language, and you accepted it in English and got the verb wrong and a mason corrected you and let the rest stand." She did not look at him. "A man whose mark is in the stone of a building is not a visitor to it. A visitor can leave. And the only way I have found in thirty years of keeping a room to keep a man out of a hole is to give him a place that is not the hole, and to be standing near it, and to be the one who enforces it. That is the whole of what a keeper is. I do not decide anything. I stand near the place a person is allowed to be and I make sure they are in it."

"It is not about me."

"It has never once been about you," said Nyla Ferrow. "I have been in this yard for thirty years and there has always be a man in it and it has never been about him. It is about the yard."

She turned and went up the steps, and then stopped, and came back, and said the thing she had come out to say, which is in the book.

"And you gave him the other one. On Wednesday, at the twelfth hour of the night, in this yard, in front of about twenty people, he asked you for it out loud and you gave it to him, and I wrote it at the top of the board in my own hand four inches high because it is my board and it is my hand and I am the only person in this city who can put a rule on a keeper's wall, and I did not write it because you asked me to. I wrote it because a man asked for it, in a yard, and a thing asked for in a yard is a thing a keeper owes him.

"**The ground under this yard does not begin without Owen Park.** And it is a man's name on a floor, and it is the second rule on that board in five weeks, and the first one is yours, and it is yours because a woman with a bowl wrote a true reason on a door in chalk and a mason put his own name under it and neither of them was asked.

"It comes off on Sunday. In daylight. I will wash it myself. And I am not going to argue with either of you about it, because a rule that a keeper puts up and takes down on her own is the only kind there is."

---

Osten Fenn came up the market at about ten minutes past two in the morning with four men and a lamp, and he had walked eleven miles in the dark in the rain and he was twenty-two years old and he had not been sent, and about nine people in Saltwake had gone to bed at midnight knowing where he was.

They found Ceri Vaughan at about half past one in the morning, on foot, on a bank eleven miles the wrong way, on the far side of a cut that is on two maps, sitting under a thorn with his lamp out and his coat buttoned to the throat.

He was not lost. That sentence is in the book at the third hour of the thirty-eighth day in a woman's hand and a twenty-two-year-old's, and it is four words long, and it is the second time in eleven days that a man in this book has found somebody by being where the water was not:

*He knew where he was the whole time.*

He had been at the second gate since the ninth hour of the night before, walking a bank he has walked for twenty-two years, and past the third cut the water came up on his left instead of his right, and he turned round and went the other way and kept going the other way for eleven hours with a lamp he did not light, because he had decided at about the second hour that if he was going to walk into the wrong water he was going to do it by daylight and not be a body somebody found in a morning.

Nobody knows why. Osten Fenn entered his own answer in nine words and the notary read it back and said it was the fourth time in a month that a person in this book had said the truest thing anybody had heard all day:

"He would not have wanted to be a body somebody found."

---

Vail was awake. Vail is always awake, and she was at the counter in the hall at about the third hour with a slate and a chain and about eleven people in the building, and she entered the finding and read it out, and then she did the thing that made it a fact in this city instead of a rumour, which was that she entered the *reason* and entered it in the same entry, and the reason is in the hands of the man who found him and not in the hands of the man who was waiting to be sent.

And then she said the other thing, which is that the entry carried a second paragraph, and the second paragraph was read out in the low room by the salt wharf in front of a family, and it is the whole of what happened next in about two hundred words.

> **Entered at the third hour of the thirty-eighth day of the flood, at the counter, in the hall of the Lantern Concord, with about eleven persons present, and read twice.**
>
> **Ceri Vaughan, thirty-six years, of Long Water, gate-hand at the second gate below Long Water, was found at about the half hour of the morning at a bank eleven miles from that gate, alive, uninjured, having been out since the ninth hour of the previous evening. He was found on foot by four persons of the second gate, one of them a man of twenty-two who was not sent. He said he knew where he was the whole time. That is entered as what he said and not as what it was.**
>
> **And: the rescue proposed on the thirty-seventh day, costed on the thirty-seventh day, consented to on the thirty-seventh day, and entered in this book on the thirty-seventh day, is for a man who has been found.**
>
> **The finding and the terms are entered in the same book, on facing pages, in the same hand, within four hours of each other, and neither of them is above the other, and this is not a coincidence of arrangement. It is the second time in a month that this book has put two things side by side that were not going to be put side by side on the thirtieth and it is the first time it has done it about a thing that was going to happen in an hour.**

It took her about a minute and a half to read and nobody in that building said anything at the end of it.

Then she said: "And I am entering a fact and I am going to read it out and then I am going to sit down, and I have never entered a fact of this kind in twenty-six years and I have thought about it for about an hour.

"**The holder of the pull was present.** The holder of the pull is the person with the standing in this city to stop the crossing entered on the thirty-seventh, and the standing exists, and it has not been used, and the holder of the pull was in this room when I read the finding and did not say the word.

"That is entered. I am not entering it as a fault and I am not going to explain it. I am entering it because a book that records what a person said and does not record what a person did not say, on the morning of the only day anybody will remember, is a book with a hole in it, and in four hundred years somebody will point at this page and say *there*."

---

Adrian was in that room. He had been in the room for eleven days of decisions and he had not said the word in nine, and the word was one he had said to Jonah Pike in a wet doorway on the Sunday and had not managed, and to a hundred and forty people in a yard, and to a family of a missing man.

Owen Park was in that room too, with his coat on and his bag over his shoulder, and he had a mark in the heel of his right hand that had been in his hand for about four hours.

He did not say the word either.

He said: "Adrian. He was at the second gate and he went out to open it, and the water came up on the wrong side of him, and he walked the wrong way for eleven hours on purpose rather than be a body somebody found in a morning. Do you know what that is."

"Yes."

"That is a man doing a hold." Owen Park had his good hand in the pocket of a bad coat. "A hold is a working without a witness and the residue of one goes into the person who did it, and he did it alone, in the dark, in a flood, with nobody, and he has been doing it for eleven hours and he came back on his own feet, and there is nobody in this world he can be angry at, and he will never be able to say a sentence about what it cost him, and neither will you and neither will I."

"I know."

"So then say the word," said Owen Park. "You have got the standing. You said a count in a yard on the Sunday and a hundred and forty people and you were the only one who could say no and you did not. Say it now. One word. *No.*" He waited. "Say it and I will stand here and I will not say one word against you and I will thank you in front of these eleven people and I will not go, and I will go in the morning like a man who was told, and I will not be the man who was not told."

And Adrian Vale stood in a room by the salt wharf at about ten past three in the morning on the thirty-eighth day of a flood and had a word available to him that he had been failing to say for nine days, and it was a short word, and it was the shortest sentence in his vocabulary, and he had used it four times in thirty-eight days and every one of the four had been right, and the fifth was the only one that would ever cost anybody anything.

He did not say it.

What he said was: "I have been trying to say that sentence to a room since Sunday and I could not do it on Sunday and I have not been able to do it in nine days, and I have written in a notebook that the reason is that I did not want to be the man who killed it, and I have written in the same notebook that the reason is also that it was unfair to say to a man who came out of a wall without asking, and both of those are true and neither of them is the reason."

"Then what is."

"The reason is that I have a post," Adrian said. "Nyla Ferrow named it on the nineteenth day and I accepted it in the wrong language and it is this: for as long as the ground is open, the ground does not open without me. Every beginning. I am there or it does not begin. It is not money and it is not useful and there is no end of it in sight. I have been at every beginning of every single thing that has happened in that ground for nineteen days, and I have never once been somewhere else, and I have come to be a person who is *answerable in a place*, and I have wanted that so much that I would rather put a man into a hole than say one word and find out that I am not a person who is answerable in a place but a person who stands near a place."

Nobody in that room had anything to say, and the last person to say anything was a man of thirty-six with a mark in his hand.

"Then go and stand near it," said Owen Park. "That is what she says the whole of a keeper is. Go and stand near the place you are allowed to be and be there at four o'clock, and do the thing you have done four times, and say the number, and be four feet back, and if I do not come back then you will have a thing in a book that says you were there, and it will be the truest thing anybody in this city has said in a month." He shifted the bag on his shoulder and his right arm went about two inches and he did not notice, and there were three people in the room who noticed.

"And Adrian. I am going, and I know the man was found, and I want to say the true thing about why, and I have only had about a minute so I am going to say it badly.

"It is not because a man is in a reed at Long Water. There is not a man in a reed at Long Water. It is because there is a *person on the other side of that floor*, and a woman of thirty-four at the front of a yard asked for a person in front of two hundred and forty people on Monday night and the whole of this city's answer to her has been four days of arithmetic, and a mark in a hand is the only thing anybody has that could answer it, and I have got one, and I have had it for about four hours, and in about nine months a woman is going to lose somebody and somebody is going to have to go, and this city is going to have to have been somewhere." He got the strap off the shoulder. "I have spent thirty-eight days being the man who has asked for nothing in a room. That was a thing I built. It worked. It is the only thing I have ever built that worked. And tonight there is a hole and a mark and a man in a reed who walked the wrong way for eleven hours on purpose, and if I do not go then it turns out that thirty-eight days of my life were a man building a thing to get a reward, and I would rather it turned out that they were a man who was owed something and turned up."

---

The list on the chapel door was read in the yard, in the open, in the rain, at about ten to four in the morning, by Ottoline Vask, in her own square mason's hand, in her own voice, at the volume she uses for mason's clauses, and it took about ninety seconds, and it is the whole of the sixth item as it stands in the book.

*One. The stone in its place. Two. Water, in any vessel, for any reason. Three. Any writing. Four. Anything that answers.*

Vask read item four and stopped for about a second, the way a woman stops at the name of a person she has known a long time, and then she said, to about twenty people, at ten to four in the morning:

"That is the list. That is the whole of it. I have not added to it in nineteen days and I am not going to, and I read it because the item says it is read before the bar goes in, and about four hours ago a man in a good coat sat in a room and had a lawyer's opinion about when there is time, and the clause as it stands says *before*, and the clause as it stands is in a book in a notary's hand and the notch is in a mason of this city's hand, and it is eleven hours, and I have read the list."

And then, with her hand flat on the tarpaulin:

"And I am going to say the other thing, and I have said it twice in a yard in three weeks and I am going to say it a third time and then I am going to go and stand at the bottom of the walk-round and I am not coming up. **A rescue is a working and a working needs a pattern and a floor that has come open once under three hundred and ten people is not a pattern, it is a hole.** I said it in a yard last night and I was outvoted in four minutes and I am not going to be thanked for saying it now, because I read a list on a chapel door about nine minutes ago and a man asked me for a thing and I am not going to be a woman who says *when there is time* and then does the other thing when there is not."

---

He went through at about four minutes past the fourth hour.

There was no bar in anything. There was no pattern, and there was no working, and no witness standing in one, and the cost was a season of a field entered for the fifth time in a month by a notary who had written in the same entry that a cost entered five times is not a cost any more, it is a notation. There were four witnesses. There was a list read ninety seconds beforehand, in the open, in the rain, at the volume it is read at. There was a man of thirty-eight days out of another world four feet back with a rule and a stub of chalk, and it was entered in the yard in front of twenty people by a docker that he was not in the opening and had no place in it and no vote in it.

And none of that matters, and all of it is true, and the difference between this and the sixth hour of the twenty-eighth day of this flood is the whole of the next nine hours.

The sixth hour of the twenty-eighth was a **route**. A route is a pattern: a place people already agree on, used already, for another reason; somebody who can be asked to hold it, who was not chosen for it; a person who goes, named by themselves, who can say no on the day; and a thing on the other side the person can recognise, which is not a person and not a room, and is small enough to carry and does not have to be carried. A route holds a person and puts them back where the terms say.

This was not a route. This was a floor with a tarpaulin on it, four feet from a chapel arch, and a dressed joint that came open on the twenty-fifth day of this flood and took eleven people out of a building without asking, and it is entered in this city's own book as **SCARRED**, which is the first place in the history of this city entered as damaged and not closable, and the local phrase for it is *a black mark in a place*.

A scar is not a pattern. A scar is where a rule broke and went.

And it went to whatever was nearest.

---

There were four things nearest and there was one named person in the terms, and the difference between one and four is the whole of what happened.

Owen Park was on his knees on the tarpaulin with his right hand flat on the lip of the joint, the way a man puts a hand on a thing he has been told is a door, and he was the nearest.

**Eli Mercer**, nineteen, of no address, of the second list, who was the second person in this world to have a return mark in his hand and who had voted for the crossing in a yard nine hours ago in about eleven sentences, was four feet away on the other side of the same joint, on his knees, with his own hand on the stone.

He was not in the terms. He had not asked to be. He had said in a yard that he was not going up there, and he had voted for a man to go, and at about twenty to four in the morning he had gone round the side of the tarpaulin to take the other end of it in his two hands because a thing is held at two ends, and he had put his hand on the dressed joint on the far side of the scar from Owen Park, and he had said the word *hold* out loud to nobody, in English, in a yard, about a tarpaulin.

Two marks. One scar. One named person.

The joint opened along four feet of dressed face at about the fourth hour and five minutes of the morning of the thirty-eighth day of the flood, and there was no water, and there was no light from anywhere and no shadow, and it was not a report and it was not a crack, and the eleven people who came out of that floor on the twenty-fifth day of this flood were in a room and this time there was no room and this time the floor did not open.

What it did was take.

It took a man on his knees with a mark in his right hand, and it took about four seconds, and there was no sound Adrian has ever been able to describe and he was four feet back with a rule in his hand and a stub of chalk and he did not mark anything, and it is entered in the book in his own words with the hour on it and it is four lines long:

> *It did not go somewhere. It did not open. The floor of that yard came up about two inches along four feet of dressed face, all at once, the way a board comes up when there is nothing under it, and there was a space under it that was the colour of the stone, and a man went into it sideways with his right arm first.*
>
> *I did not mark anything. I had a chalk and I had the number in my hand and I was four feet back and the number was nothing because there was no join, there was a hole, and I have been reading a join for thirty-eight days and there was nothing in that yard to read.*
>
> *And the fourth item of the terms says that if the join does anything he did not expect he is the man who says the number and not the man who decides. I said the number. The number was that there were two men on that floor and one of them was not in the terms.*

The space under the floor was open for about a minute and a half, and at the bottom of it, four feet down, was the side of a dressed wall laid on edge, and it was dry, in a flood month, on a bank above a river, and the only person in this city who has ever lifted a stone out of a bank and found it dry is a mason, who was up a chapel at that moment and has not been down the hole and does not intend to.

Then the boards came back down and did not break.

---

Owen Park came back into the yard at about twenty past the sixth hour of the morning of the thirty-eighth day of the flood, on his hands, out of about four feet of a space under a tarpaulin, and he was on top of the tarpaulin on all fours with the rain going into his collar and about forty people standing in a yard at six in the morning in a flood month because about forty people in Saltwake do not sleep when there is a man in a hole.

He was alive.

That is the first fact and it is entered first and it is entered in a notary's hand at about the seventh hour, and there is a paragraph about it in the entry that reads like a man writing a document she has not been asked to write and having to write anyway:

> **And: he was alive, and this city entered that fact before it entered any other fact about the morning, and I want it in the book in that order, because in about four years there will be a version of this day in a market and in the version he will be described as a thing that went into a hole, and a man is not a thing that went into a hole, and on the morning of the thirty-eighth day of the flood at about twenty past the sixth hour a man of this city came up out of a floor on his hands, and about forty people were in a yard, and a woman with tweezers got to him before the notary did and said one word and it was not in the local language.**

Hesper Saye got to him and said, in English, in a yard, in the rain, to about forty people: "**Wait.**" And then she said, to a docker, "Get the door off its hinges and get two men, and do not tell me a door is a door, I have been in this city thirty-four years, it is a board and it is the only flat thing in this city."

He was badly hurt, on top of the shoulder that went on the twenty-fifth.

The shoulder is the thing. It went on the twenty-fifth day of this flood at the eleventh hour and eleven minutes and it had gone back in twice and it could not be set because there was nothing out of place, and Hesper Saye had said in a porch to about forty people that it would be asked to hold again and it would not be able to tell him afterwards and neither would he, and that he would not lift over about forty pounds with that arm for the rest of his life and would not know he was doing it, and about one time in nine he would find out.

It had been asked to hold again. Saye said that in a yard at about the seventh hour in eleven words and would not say any more about it until the Friday.

And the scalp — the scalp that opened on the first day of this flood and had not closed in thirty-seven days, which is a wound with clean skin over it and nothing happening underneath, which is a thing with a witness on it that has not been taken up, and which Hesper Saye had a word for and had said the word in a porch to nine people in the second week and had not taken it back since.

It was not bleeding. It was not cut. It was exactly the same size it had been on the first morning of this flood, and there were people in this city who had been looking at it for thirty-seven days who said afterwards that it was about a third of an inch further down the bone than it had been in the second week, and there were other people who said it was not, and both of those are in the book because the book has had four contradictory accounts on one page since the twenty-ninth and Sera Vail entered them on purpose.

And the mark in his hand, which had been a mark for about four hours, and had been established on the thirty-seventh day at about the eleventh hour of the night by four people with a cost entered and a notary's seal on it, for one named person and not for the route — and Adrian had entered the difference, and a mason had thanked him in public for it.

It was not a mark any more. It was the same branching shape in the same proportion, a thumbnail's worth of branching, and it was black, and it was not the colour of a mark.

It was a **scar**.

And it was the first one in this world that was a scar in a person rather than a scar in a place, and a mason of thirty-four years in Saltwake said the two sentences about it at about the seventh hour of the morning of the thirty-eighth day in front of about eleven people, in a yard, in the rain, and they are in the book in her words:

"A line in a place can be mended with work and money. I have mended one. It took me two years and it is not as good as it was and it holds.

"A mark in a person cannot be mended with work and money, and the reason is not the money. The reason is that nobody steps over a hand."

And Adrian's first act, in that yard, at about the seventh hour of the morning of the thirty-eighth day of the flood, was relief. He has written that down and it is the shortest entry in the notebook and it is two words and it is the only entry in it that is not about anybody else:

*He was alive.*

And his second act was to understand, in front of eleven people, that a thing had come through with him or out of him, and that the two words for those are not the same word, and one of them is a scar in a person.

He said it out loud in the yard, in the local language, badly, and Tamsin corrected him twice and he made her do it a third time because the third time was the one that mattered:

"He is alive and I want that in the book first and I want nobody to touch him for about an hour. And then I want the room cleared to eleven people and I want it said in the room and not in a market: **either a thing came through with him, or a thing came out of him, and those are not the same sentence, and one of them is a scar in a person, and I do not know which, and I am not going to stand in a yard and pick.**

"The mark in a hand is a place a person can be found from. A mark in a hand is not a place anybody has ever found. And the difference between the two is about four hours and about a floor, and I have spent thirty-eight days learning that a difference of that size is the entire distance between a road and a hole."

---

The room was the low room by the salt wharf, and it was in it from about the eighth hour of the morning of the thirty-eighth day of the flood, and everybody in that room was pretending that he was not dying.

Sera Vail was pretending, with a slate, because she was writing. Hesper Saye was pretending, because she was working, and a woman of fifty-eight has been working in that room since the second week of this flood and has been wrong about ninety of four hundred men she has told things to. Mina Okoye was pretending, and she was pretending by standing at the foot of the table with her hands at her sides and not touching him, which is a thing she has done four hundred times and which took her about a year to learn and which she learned in a hospital in another hemisphere and never in this room.

Tamsin Quill was pretending, by not saying anything, and Adrian noticed that she was not saying anything in the local language, which is the thing she does to him when a thing has been decided and has not been said.

Hettis Roke was pretending, with his waxed slate, and he wrote for about forty minutes in a room with a man in it and read nothing back, and Vail entered that fact: *that the Hearthguard of the outer ward wrote for forty minutes in a room on the thirty-eighth day of the flood and read nothing back, and did not summarise, and the reason is entered and the reason is that he has entered two hundred and forty pages in nineteen years and this is the first one he has ever not read out, and it is in this book in his own hand and unsummarised, and any person may read it.*

And at about the ninth hour of the morning, at the end of the table, on a stool, with a school exercise book and a piece of paper in the other hand, there was a boy of nineteen years old with his right hand held out in front of him at about the height of a cup, palm up, in a room with a dying man in it, and there was a mark on it, and the mark was not a scar.

It was the other one. It was the first mark ever set in this world, on the twenty-eighth day of this flood at about the eleventh hour of the night, by four people, at a cost, and it had carried a person eleven seconds and had come back with a numbered paper of eight things whose numbers run 1 to 4 and then back to 1 and 2 and 1.

Eli Mercer was holding his hand out in a room and looking at it and had been for about twenty minutes, and nobody had asked him to do it, and when Mina Okoye finally asked him, at about a quarter past nine, what he was doing, he said, in English, to a room, in a voice that did not crack:

"I'm checking whether it's still a mark. It is not the same colour as yesterday. It is not black. It is — " he turned it in the light. "It is the colour of the one Owen's became. Just not all the way. About a third of it."

"Give me your hand," said Mina Okoye.

He gave her his hand, and she held it, and she did not put it down for about four minutes, and then she said, in English, to Adrian, across a room, with her hand still on a nineteen-year-old's palm:

"Adrian. I need you to write down what I am about to say, because I said something on the first morning of this flood and it was wrong. I said *nobody is hurt in this world that was not going to be hurt.* I said it to nine people in a porch and it was a stupid sentence and I have been carrying it for thirty-eight days. **That is not true. The thing that is true is that nobody in this world is hurt in it alone, and that is a different sentence and it is a much worse one, because it means that the day a person in this city is hurt, everybody in this city is in the wound, and there is no instrument for that and there is not going to be one, and a scar in a person is the first thing anybody in this world has ever seen of it, and it is in the heel of his hand, and a scar in a place does not have a hand in it, and a scar in a person does, and that is the whole difference between a floor and a man and about four hundred people have just walked into it and cannot get out."

And Adrian wrote it down, in a room by the salt wharf, on the thirty-eighth day of this flood, at about a quarter past nine in the morning, in the flat hand, in one line, and the line is:

*A scar in a person is a scar in a place with a hand in it.*
