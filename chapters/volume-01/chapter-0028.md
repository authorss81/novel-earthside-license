# Chapter 0028 — What the Scout Is

Eli Mercer came back out of eighteen inches of dead-straight dead-straight seam in the wall of the outer channel with a piece of paper in his hand, and he kept it, and on the twenty-ninth day of the flood it turned out that the paper was the reason the Lantern Concord spent six hours in a converted salt store doing the most careful thing anybody in that city had done since the flood began, and also the reason a man in a good coat was, for the ninth day in a row, the single most useful person in the room.

He had been out of the world for eleven seconds. He had a piece of paper.

He handed it to Adrian in the yard, in the rain, at about eleven minutes past six on the morning of the twenty-eighth, in front of a hundred and forty people, and Adrian did not look at it, and Priya Shah took it out of his hand and looked at it, and then went into the chapel above the market with it and shut the door.

"It is not a *thing*," Eli Mercer said, to about nine people who had not gone to work. "I want to be clear about that first, because everybody is about to make it a thing. It is a receipt. I have been in a hundred games. You get a receipt."

"There is no game," said Adrian.

"Brother," said Eli Mercer, "there is *absolutely* a game, I don't know what it is, and I have got a piece of paper from it. Can we do this in a room, please? I would like to do this in a room with the door shut. I've been awake for about twenty hours and all of yesterday was in them."

---

The room had the door shut for six hours and about forty people in it, and Sera Vail ran it, and she ran it the way she runs everything, which is that she decides what is admissible and reads it back and does not allow a person to withdraw a true thing and does not allow a person to keep a false one.

Her first ruling was about the paper.

"No," she said. "Not entered as a document of this city. It goes in as *a paper carried by a person out of an opening and brought back into this one, at the sixth hour of the twenty-eighth day, and produced by him in this room, and neither its making nor its content is witnessed by anybody in this city.* That is a long entry and it is the shortest true one. It is not a record of another world. It is a record that a person held a thing."

"It has writing on it," said Teodor Blaz.

"Then it has writing on it and I am entering that," said Vail, "and nobody in this room may ask him to read it aloud until he has been told, in this room, that he may refuse, and that a refusal is entered and is not held against him, and that nothing may be done to him for holding it." She looked round. "That is on a wall in a room above the market. It is my second clause. I would like to see whether anybody in this room is entitled to a better one and I do not think anybody is."

Nobody claimed to be, and Vail entered the form's sixth clause into the ledger and read it back, and then said, "Now. Ask him."

---

What Teodor Blaz said, in that room, over about forty minutes, with nine people from another world interrupting him and two of them contradicting him and one of them apologising for the interruption and then not stopping, is this.

"He says it was a room. Not a place. A room, with a flat floor and a low ceiling and no window and no door that any of us could find in the first four minutes. The light in it came from everywhere at once and there was no shadow anywhere, so you could not tell how big it was, and eleven of us have disagreed about the size for two days and the range is between eleven paces and about four hundred paces.

"There were four openings in the long wall. Not doors — *openings*, in the wall, at different heights off the floor, with a raised lip in front of each like a sill, and the fourth one — " he looked up, and Adrian felt something go cold and specific behind his left eye " — the fourth one was already open. We came out of the third one.

"And that's the bit they're all arguing about. Seven of us went to the first one, because it's the widest and you go to the widest. Four of us went to the second because we could not fit. I went to the fourth because I could not get into any of the others and I was last, and I came out of the third one, and so did you—" he said, to Eli, "— and I have been arguing with you for two days and I am not going to stop now.

"The sounds were like a page. Every forty seconds or so, a sound like a page being turned, somewhere above and behind, and nobody could find where. Not words. Not speech. A *page*.

"And there was a smell. Nobody else smelled it. I smelled wet stone in a room with no stone in it, and I have smelled that before, and I have not told anybody before because I did not want to be the one."

Nobody in that room had anything to say for about four seconds.

"Write the smell in," said Adrian.

"Write the *smell* in."

"Write the smell in. In your words. Not summarised. And say that one person smelled it, and say that it is wet stone in a room with no stone in it, and say that you have smelled it before and do not say where, and if you want to say where, say it, and if you do not, that is in the record too." Adrian put his hand flat on the table. "Because a person who smells something in a place with nothing in it and who has smelled it before is not a crank. He is a *witness with a second set of things*, and I have been one of those for four weeks and nobody has believed a word I have said, and the difference between him and me is that he said it on the second day and I said it on the twenty-fifth, and that is the whole of the difference and it is not about truth."

---

Ivenn Marrow arrived at the second hour and sat down at the back with a slate and did not interrupt anybody for two hours, and at the fourth hour he was asked to translate, and the reason he was asked was that of the forty people in the room about eleven spoke the local language and of the nine from the other world about four could make themselves understood in it, and between the two groups there was a gap of about four hours of conversation that consisted entirely of both sides saying *what?* and gesturing.

He translated for two hours and it is entered in the ledger that he translated the whole of it, in the local language, word for word, in the flat voice of a man reading a document, and added one sentence of his own, once, at the end, which was:

"That is what they said. I have not arranged it, and I have not improved it, and I have not removed the four disagreements, and I want it entered that I have not removed them, because in nineteen years of reading other people's accounts I have never once read one and been asked to keep the wrong parts."

"You are the Crown man," said a docker of about fifty at the back. "You have been in this city seven weeks and you are the man who says the three-hundred-year-old paper in the tally house is a fraud, and now you are sitting in a room doing this."

"I said a *word* in that paper is a bad word," said Marrow, without turning round. "I did not say the paper is a fraud, and I have spent a week in this city not correcting that, and I am not going to correct it now, and you may have the entry, and the entry is in the book with the date on it." He put the slate down. "And you are right about the shape of what is happening in this room, and I am going to name it so that somebody who is worried can watch it happen, and I would like it entered that I named it myself: **a man in a good coat is doing the thing he is best at, in a room of frightened people, and it is genuinely useful, and that is the dangerous part.** If I had come here and tried to recruit them I would have been finished in this city in a fortnight. I have not tried. I have translated. And if I go on translating, then in a month there will be a market in this city that says the anchorwright's man from the wall brought back a description of the other side and had it written down, and there will be some of that true."

---

Jonah Pike argued for publication and was ninety per cent right and lost on the tenth per cent, and Adrian wrote down both halves.

"The account of that room is the single most important piece of information any of us will have in a year," Jonah said. "Not the working. The *account*. A city that has one written description of a place it has never seen, from a person who has been there, is a city that can plan. And you cannot plan on a thing that is in a room in a salt store. I said on the sixth day of this month, in a market, that a rumour with no competitor grows, and a rumour that is *in a book in this city* does not, and I have been right about that for twenty-four days and I have been hated for it and I am going to be hated for it again. Put it on the board by the market. Tomorrow morning. Under my name, if you like."

"And what happens to a person who is named in a market in connection with a room in another world," said Adrian.

"Nothing. Nothing happens."

"Two men from a chandlery on the upper road were in that hall on the twenty-fifth evening," Adrian said, "because somebody had said the number eleven out loud on a market board nine hours before, and nobody knows who said it, and I have had four days to try to find out and I cannot. That is what happens. A thing is said, and a number goes with it, and two men from a chandlery turn up, and the thing that has happened is that there are two men from a chandlery in a hall. And the account of that room is worth more to this city than anything any of us will ever find, and it is also the best thing any of us will ever have to lie about, and you cannot put a thing that valuable in a market and expect the market to only do the one thing."

Jonah stood up and put his coat on and said, "That is exactly the argument I have been making in the other direction for three weeks and I withdraw it, and I want it entered that I withdrew it, and I want it entered that I was the one who had to be argued out of it by the person I was arguing with, because that is the first time it has happened and I am not going to pretend it did not."

---

The thing that the six hours were actually for happened at about the fifth hour, and it was Vail's, and she did it because she had been asked to do it by nobody.

"Right," she said, at the fifth hour, with the ledger open. "There are four disagreements in this account and I am going to read all four of them out, in their own words, with the names on, and I am going to read them out in the same entry, one under the other, and I am not going to reconcile them, and I want the room to understand that I am the person in this city who is paid to make a book that agrees with itself, and that I am not going to do it this morning."

"You cannot enter a contradiction in a ledger," said Aldis Kerrow, from the side wall, with his very good coat and his narrow mouth.

"I can enter a contradiction in a ledger. I have been entering them for twenty-six years. It is nine tenths of the trade." She squared the page. "A *single account* is a thing I make. A *record* is a thing I keep, and what I keep is what people said. And the reason this matters, and the reason I am going to read all four, is that somebody is going to come in about four months with a version of that room, and they are going to be a man with a good reason, and they are going to be *right about a half of it*, and if this morning's page has one clean account on it, then in four months the only way to argue with that man is to say *the other page says something else*, and I could not point at it, because I burned it. So I am keeping the wrong bits."

"And if the second group come back in a year with a fifth account," said Kerrow, "this page will be wrong four ways."

"Yes," said Sera Vail. "And it will be the only page in this book that anybody can point at. And I have learned, from a woman with a bowl who writes a form for things that cannot be looked at again, that **a document which is internally consistent and which can only be checked by one person is not a record. It is a story with a seal on it.**"

---

And then, at about the fifth hour and a half, a woman of about twenty-four who had been sitting on a bench at the back with a bundle of papers on her knee for six hours and had said nothing at all, stood up and asked a question that nobody in that room had thought of, including the notary, including the man in the book.

"Has the Reach got it?"

Vail looked up.

"The account of that room is going in this book's book," Peth Lammay said, in the local language, and Tamsin translated it into English and then translated it back again because she wanted it right both ways. "I carry the Crown's second-table mail on the upper river and the Reach run, and I have carried it for eleven years, and I have never once been asked what happens when the answer on the fourth page is no. So I am going to ask it, and then I am going to sit down again.

"There are four villages on the page in front of you, and Nine Elms by name, and about a hundred and forty people from Four Elms, and every one of those people has water in a ditch that this city has been moving about, and they will find out about this room in a market in about nine days, because markets work, and I have watched them work for eleven years.

"So: does the meeting-ground of the Reed Reach know that this page exists, and does Petra Oake know that it exists, and does a woman of seventy in Nine Elms know that it exists before a clerk in Aurel knows that it exists? Because if a clerk in Aurel knows first, then this page is a report, and if a woman of seventy in Nine Elms knows first, then this page is a thing that was done to them as well as about them, and the difference between those two is the difference between an account and a *grievance*, and a grievance is a thing you can use, and I have carried eleven years of things you can use and I would like to have carried one that was not."

Vail put her pencil down, and picked it up again, and said:

"I have an answer to that and it took me about four seconds and the answer is that I have been a notary for twenty-six years and I have sent four hundred things to Aurel in four hundred wooden cases and I have never once sent one of them down a river to a woman before I sent it to a clerk, and I did not do that because I was generous. I did it because it never occurred to me. Thank you."

"And I will take it," said Peth Lammay, "and I will take it to Verrow myself, and it goes on the Thursday, which is the day after tomorrow, and I will not go on the Tuesday because the Tuesday is the day the mail case goes and the case goes to Aurel, and I am not having it in the case." She looked at Adrian. "And you are coming down the road with me, because a page that has got a man in it needs the man in the room when it is read, and you have got four days to learn how to be in a room in the Reach without a notebook, and I have watched you for two days and you have not started."

---

And then, at about the sixth hour of the twenty-ninth day of the flood, Teodor Blaz said the thing that took the rest of the day.

"There is one more thing," he said, "and I have been sitting on it since the twenty-eighth because I did not want to be the man who says the strange bit, and I have decided I would rather be than not say it, and if I am wrong then I am wrong on the page and it is a Tuesday and somebody can tell me.

"The four openings were not the same height off the floor. The first one was about four foot. The second about two foot eight. The third about two foot. And the fourth one — the one that was already open, the one I came out of — was about a foot and a half, and you could get through it sideways, and I know that because I went through it twice.

"And that is not a thing I should know. I am a warehouse supervisor in Leeds. I have never measured anything in my life that mattered. But I looked at them, in that order, from left to right along the wall, going down, and the drop from one to the next was about three parts in two. And then the last one dropped a bit less than three in two, because the first drop is from four foot and the last is to a foot and a half, and three in two three times off four foot is a foot and a half and change, and the change is about two inches, and the fourth one is about two inches lower than three in two.

"Nobody in that room noticed that but me. I have thought about it for a day. Eleven people looked at four holes in a wall and eleven of us did not measure them and I did, and I do not know why I did, and I have never in my life done a thing like that, and I would like somebody to tell me it is a normal thing to do."

The room was quiet.

Adrian Vale was sitting at the second bench on the left with his hands on his knees, and there was a sensation in his right hand that he has never been able to describe to anybody except as *being read*.

He did not say anything. He did not say anything for about eleven seconds, which is a long time in a room of forty people, and then he said, in English, in a completely level voice, to a woman with a slate:

"Priya. What was on the paper."

Priya Shah looked up from the table where she had been copying the four accounts, and she said, "It is a list. It is a numbered list of eight things, and seven of them are words I cannot spell in this language and one of them is a number, and the numbers go 1 to 4 and then back to 1 and then 2 and then 1, and there is nothing else on it. It is a *list*, Adrian. It is a list of what you press."

---

It took him about forty minutes to get to the notebook, and twenty of those were spent standing in a room, because on the twenty-fifth day of this month, in a yard, at the eleventh hour and thirteen minutes, something had come out of the black line in the chapel stone and said twelve words of English in a flat reading voice, and he had written them down at half past four that afternoon in a room above a market, and he had read them over about nine times since, and he had not once put them next to a page in a notebook, and he has been asked since why not and he does not have an answer that he believes.

He did not go and look at the notebook until about the eighth hour of the evening, in a room above a market, with the door shut, and he did it alone, and it took him about ninety seconds.

Page forty-one.

He knew it was page forty-one. He has known it was page forty-one since he sat down, which is a thing he has written about, because he has been carrying a page in a notebook for twenty-five days and he has never once had to open the notebook to find out what is on page forty-one.

> **What a controlled crossing needs, in the order it needs it, if you are building one and nobody has ever built one:**
> **1. A place people already agree on, used already, for another reason.**
> **2. Somebody who can be asked to hold it, who was not chosen for this.**
> **3. A person who goes, named by themselves, who can say no on the day.**
> **4. A thing on the other side that the person can recognise, which is not a person and is not a room, and is small enough to carry and does not have to be carried.**

And underneath, in the flat hand, in smaller writing, and he had written that part at about four in the morning on the fourth night of the flood in a room above a river market with a headache and a nosebleed, and it was a ratio, and it was about heights:

> *and the four have to be stepped so that the first is the easiest and the last is the narrowest, because a person who cannot get into the first one will go and look for a second and a person who cannot get into the second will break the third, and the whole of the design is that nobody ever has to break anything. Ratio between the sills about three in two and the last one a little under three in two. Roughly four foot, two foot eight, two foot, a foot and a half.*

He had written it at four in the morning on the fourth day of the flood, in his own hand, in a paper notebook, in a room over a market, and he had shown it to nobody, and it was not on the computer, and the section of the file that held it had been deleted eight days later in another hemisphere, and it was in a locked office in a building whose door code changed on Mondays, and there was no copy of it anywhere in the world except this page.

He sat on a floor in a room above a market for about four minutes and did not write anything down.

Then he wrote, at the top of a clean page, in the flat hand, and he did not cross anything out:

*The twenty-ninth day, at the eighth hour of the evening.*

*I have been doing this for four days and I have got four answers and I have been keeping them in order of how much I like them, and I am now going to write them in order of how much they frighten me.*

*One. Something read a file that does not exist. This is the reading I liked, because it makes me an instrument and instruments are not responsible, and I have been gripping it since the Friday night of the third week like a rail. It requires a file. There is no file. It has been checked.*

*Two. The thing has been inside me since Tuesday at twenty to three in the morning. This is the reading Tamsin believes, and she has never said so in a document, and she says it the way a woman says a thing she has decided to be wrong about later.*

*Three. It is a coincidence, and the room is somebody else's room, and there are four openings in it because rooms have things in them. This is available. I have held it for about two days. It fails on the ratio and it fails on the order, and the ratio is the ratio, and the order is the order, and I wrote the order at four in the morning with a nosebleed because I could not sleep and because a voice had come out of a stone at the fourth hour and told me a sentence in my own words.*

*Four. **I wrote it down, and I have been carrying it, and I did not know it was the same thing.***

*Four is the boring one. Four is the one in which nothing has happened to me at all, and a man from another world has gone into a hole in a wall and found a copy of a page out of my own pocket, and the copy is not a copy, because nobody has ever read the page except me.*

*And I am not going to be able to tell the difference, and I have to write that down as the finding and not as a feeling, because a feeling is what a man says in a room when he wants a room to give him a different answer.*

*Which means there is a fourth thing and it is the only one of the four I can act on, and it is this: a thing that came out of a stone in this city in the fourth week of a flood, in the words and the order of a man from another world, is a **thing that a page in a man's pocket made possible**, and I do not know how, and the pocket is mine, and it has been in a coat on a bed on a floor in a room above a market for twenty-five days with six people sleeping in it, and I am not going to put six people's names in a book over it. But I am going to write down that the page exists, that I wrote it on the fourth night, that I have shown it to nobody, that it is in my own hand, and that the order and the ratio in it are the order and the ratio in a room in another place, and that this is now a fact on a page that a man from another world can be asked about.*

*And I am going to give the page to Sera Vail, tonight, in a room with two people in it, and I am not going to ask her to enter it, because a page in my own hand in my own notebook is a record of a man and not a record of a city, and the only person in this world entitled to keep it is a notary, and she will decide what it is.*

*And I want it written here, in my own hand, where a man from another world and a notary and possibly nobody else can find it: **that is the first time in twenty-five days that I have handed somebody the thing I was frightened of without telling anybody first, and I did it because a warehouse supervisor in Leeds measured four holes in a wall for no reason he can explain, and because a woman with a bowl has a form on a wall that says a person is owed an answer, and because I ran out of hands to hold it with and the only hands left were hers.***

*Ask the people who maintain the gate. Ask the keeper. Ask the woman who keeps a count.*

*And on Thursday, in a hall, at first light, I am going to be asked for the third time in a month what a mark in a wall is for, and I have had five days to get an answer, and I have not got one, and what I have instead is a page, and a ratio, and a boy's smell of wet stone in a room with no stone in it.*
