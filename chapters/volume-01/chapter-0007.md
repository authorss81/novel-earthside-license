# Chapter 0007 — The First Quest Is a Trap

The preliminary audience was held in the chapel because the chapel was the only room in the Lock of Salt with a witness in it that the Lantern Concord recognised, and the Concord's notary had said so in writing, and the writing was what mattered.

Nyla Ferrow had swept the floor. That was the first thing Adrian noticed. Somebody had gone over the flagstones with a broom, hard, in a great hurry, working toward the door, and the line of standing water Adrian had watched climb across those stones a day and a half ago was gone, and where it had been there was a pale bloom of dried salt in a shape like a hand with the fingers spread.

And across the chapel, at the foot of the arch, there was the other thing the room had: a single pale slab laid across the flagstones rather than in line with them, raised four inches off the floor, worn down to a shallow bowl in the middle by four hundred years of boots going over it. Every person who came into that chapel to keep a gate stepped over it. It was the threshold, and it was one stone, and it was not the floor, and anybody in Saltwake would have taken you to the difference between them in about a second.

He looked at it for longer than he should have. Then he turned his hand over, because he could not stop himself, and the mark was still there in the heel of his palm in the flat pale grey, and it was thinner than it had been on Tuesday without being any smaller in a way he could measure, and there was no longer any doubt whatever in his mind about whether it was real.

Tamsin stood by the arch. Roke stood by the door. A mason called Ottoline Vask had come on her own account, with a mallet in her hand and no permission from anybody, and had put herself where she could see both the door and the floor and said nothing at all for the whole first half hour.

And at the back, in the corner with his back to a wall, there was a man Adrian had not seen before and did not know the name of, and he was the only person in the room who was sitting down.

He was about forty, thin rather than slight, in a good grey coat that had been made for someone who cared about being looked at. He had ink on two fingers of his right hand. He watched Adrian come in the way a man watches a door open that he has been waiting behind.

"Vale," he said. "Sit down. You have been up for two days and this will take an hour and I would rather you did not fall over in front of a notary."

"You know my name."

"I have been in the city for six weeks and I have read four hundred pages about you." He smiled. It was a good smile. That was the thing Adrian noticed. It was a *constructed* good smile, the kind that had been made by a man who had practised the shape in a mirror and then practised it on other people, and it landed on Adrian like a hand on the shoulder. "Ivenn Marrow. I am a scholar of Crown Law, which is a polite way of saying that I read documents that other people wrote and then lie about. I came down from Aurel to look at your wall."

"Do you have a name for the wall?"

"I have three. I brought two of them and I left the third at home because it is the sort of name that gets a man hanged." He put his hands on his knees, lightly. "The first is a *breach*, which is the term your lock people use and which is quite good. The second is a *failed public experiment*, which is the term my trade uses, and which is the reason I am here and the reason I came before the Crown did, because when the Crown sends somebody it sends a clerk, and a clerk writes things down."

"And the third?"

"That there is a permission out there with no site under it, no witness, and no community that has agreed to receive anything, and that everything you have just experienced follows from those three absences." He said it lightly, the way a man reads out a list he has read out before. "You did not arrive through a door. A door has a frame and a frame has somebody responsible for it. You arrived through a piece of language with your name in it, and it took the nearest opening it could find, which happened to be inside a dam."

Adrian sat down. He sat down because his legs made the decision.

"You've been reading four hundred pages about me," he said. "I have been a designer for most of a decade and nobody has ever read four hundred pages about me. What have you been reading?"

Marrow's smile did not move. That was the second thing he noticed.

"Everything the Concord could tell me, and some of it the Concord could not," he said. "There is a report from a Saltwake customs clerk about a light in a wall, dated about three hundred and ten years ago, and it is a fraud, and it is also the only document I have found that describes a breach in language a living person would have used. Your wall, Vale, has been described before. It has been described badly, by people who wanted money, but it has been described. That is not nothing. That is a starting point."

"A starting point for what?"

"For the only question anybody with my training has got, which is: is that a hole, or is it a *permission that lost its site*." He tipped his head. "Because a hole is an accident, and an accident can be mended. And a permission that lost its site is a piece of law with nobody standing in it, and I know where to put it, and it takes an hour."

Tamsin said, in the local language, four words, with a great deal of care.

Ivenn Marrow's smile stayed exactly where it was. "The anchorwright asks whether I have read the records of the last anchorwright who said something like that to me."

"Last one?"

"Eleven years ago, in a village above the Reed, who is not alive, because a man who reads permissions as though they are the whole of a place will always underweight the place." He said it without heat, and Adrian thought: *he is not lying.* That was somehow the worst part. Nothing he had said yet was a lie, and it was shaped so that every true part of it made a door open.

---

This was where the voice in his head started using words again.

Adrian had spent two days in a world with no status display of any kind, and he had been quietly, privately, embarrassed about how much he missed one. And now, sitting on a chapel floor with ink on a stranger's fingers, he looked up at the arched window and the light coming through it went thin and pale, and there was a small rectangular field standing in the air in front of him about eighteen inches long, the colour of a paper form, and it said:

> A WORKWAY IS AVAILABLE.
>
> Wayfinder. To read a road is to carry its name. A wayfinder may not leave a road unnamed while holding it.
>
> A stone is out of its place. It lies below the lock chapel. Two hundred steps of outer wall, then down.
>
> This is offered to you. It is not yet accepted.

It stayed for eleven seconds. It did not move, it did not flicker, and it did not have a header, and it had no cost in it anywhere — not the cost of the workway, which was stated, and not the cost of the stone, which was not, and Adrian understood with a clarity that went right through him like cold water that there was a second cost and that the field had been built to make sure he would not ask about it.

"You are looking at something," Ivenn said.

"I'm looking at a wall."

"You are looking at something that is not there. I have watched men look at nothing for six weeks." He stood up, unhurried, and moved to where Adrian was looking and then, precisely, not to where Adrian was looking. "Let me tell you what I think you have been offered, since you will not say. It has a shape like an offer and it is not one. I have read the old Bright Pilgrim accounts. Every one of them describes a *gift* that was not described as a gift, and every one of them describes a *road* that was not described as a road."

"Pilgrims," said Adrian. "You've said that word twice."

"I have said it because you have not asked, and because a man who does not ask is usually afraid of the answer." Ivenn sat down again, three feet away, cross-legged, entirely comfortable. "Three centuries ago a small number of people came through a wall near this lock. Fourteen or eighteen accounts, no two of them agreeing. The Concord burned most. What survives describes people who arrived with no language, no tools, and no idea where they were, and who were fed, and who were buried here — there is a graveyard on the upper road with nineteen stones on it that have writing nobody in this city can read. That is a true thing. That is not a myth. The myths came later, and the myths are why nobody wrote the accounts down properly, and I would like to be the first man in nine hundred years to write them down properly."

"Out of what?"

"Out of the only fragments with any pattern in them." He looked at Adrian's palm. "You have something in your hand. You have had it since you came out of the wall, and you have been careful with it, and I am not going to ask you to show it to me because showing it would make it a thing that a man saw, and it is better that it is a thing between you and the people who gave it to you." A pause. "But I will tell you what a workway is, since you have not been told. It is a permission profile. It is not a skill, it does not teach you anything, and it does not make you strong. It tells one branch of a working *whose work you will be doing*, and then everyone who is affected is entitled to expect that branch of you. Wayfinder means: you will read roads, and you will carry their names, and you will not be permitted to leave a road unnamed while you are holding it. That is all. It is a duty with a shape. People in this city will be far more afraid of you with one than without."

"Would you take it?"

"Yes." He said it without a flicker. "I have taken two. I am not brave, Vale. I am simply the kind of man who can be useful, and usefulness is rarer than courage, and being useful is what I have actually spent my life doing."

"That's not permission," Mina said.

He turned round. She was standing by the door with her arms crossed, and she had not come in, and she had been listening for some time.

"Say the rest of it," Adrian said.

"It is not permission to use it. Something showing you a route is not somebody agreeing to the route, and it is not somebody agreeing to pay for it, and if you walk on it because a voice in a wall told you the walk was possible, then in nine months' time there will be a village that lost a season and a man who says the sign was clear." She did not raise her voice. "I've watched three people die of things that were on a sign.

"Ivenn," Tamsin said, in English, sharply.

"It is not a permission to use it," Adrian said, to Marrow, and then to the room, and he felt the cold of the pale field still standing in the air in front of him and he pointed at it, at nothing, at the space beside the arched window. "There is an offer on that wall and it is a *thing on a wall*, it is not a contract, and I am not going to be a man who does things because a wall said so in a voice that agrees with him."

"You are going to be a man who does nothing, then, while the next tide comes in."

"No. There's a difference, and it's the whole difference, and I'll show you it in about ten minutes." He turned round to face the room properly, all of it, the notary's slate and Vask's mallet and Roke's chalk and Tamsin at the arch. "That's what I've learned in two days. The difference is: a person can be asked. A thing cannot be asked. It can be *read*. And when I can't tell the difference, I end up doing whatever the last bright surface said to me, and I have done that for a living, and I am not going to start doing it for a hole in a dam."

Tamsin Quill had gone very still.

Then she said, to the room, in English, and Adrian understood that she had decided to say it in English because the record was the thing that mattered and she was not going to let a translator get it wrong:

"While we are being careful, there is a second thing. The stone he has been shown is real. There *is* a stone out of its place, and it is under the lock chapel, and it has been out of its place for longer than this chapel has been a chapel. I know where it is. Ottoline knows where it is. We have all known where it is since I was a child, and there are two families on this road who will not walk across the middle of the yard after dark because of it."

She turned to face Marrow properly for the first time.

"And it is not free, and it has never been free, and the cost is not on the wall in the air." She said it slowly, in the way you say something to a foreign clerk who is going to have to write it down. "The old boundary of the river is in that stone. The boundary that says how much of the flood the lower fields have to take. It was moved once, in living memory, and the year it was moved the bottom fields failed and four hundred people were rehoused and nobody has been allowed to move it since, and there is a table in the Concord hall with every attempt on it, including mine, including a proposal of mine that would have moved it and would have drowned the Reed margin, and my name is on that table, and the reason my name is on that table is so that nobody can do it again quietly."

Ottoline Vask said, from the wall, without moving, "It is not the boundary that would move. If you took the stone up out of its place you would be working on the old river's edge and the water would decide the new one, and the water decides by whichever village is lower, and the Reed margin is lower than the bottom fields, and it is not lower by accident. It is lower because somebody made it lower, and that somebody wrote it down, and that writing down is the oldest piece of paper in this building." She looked at Marrow. "You knew that."

"I knew it," said Marrow. "I did not know it was *here*."

"That is not the same as not knowing it."

---

It came to a test, because Adrian asked for one and nobody could think of a better idea.

He would not take the workway. He wanted that stated in the room, in front of the notary's slate, because he had a strong and entirely unromantic suspicion that the difference between the man who had said no and the man who had said yes was going to be worth something in about four days. So Marrow wrote it, in his own hand, on his own paper, in a sentence, and read it aloud in both languages.

Then Tamsin said: "Then read the road anyway. Without the workway. You can do that when my people are holding the arch, and it will last exactly as long as they do, and it will not be yours."

"You are sure you want to do that with me in it," Adrian said.

"No," she said. "I am sure I want to see whether you can do it without anybody's name."

That was the sentence that cost her something, and he heard it, and he did not have the decency to pretend he hadn't.

Vask's crew set up at the arch. Two masons, a boy with a bucket, and Tamsin, who took the position on the inside of the arch with both feet in the old patch of lime and clay and said the maintenance oath over once in a low voice, and then waited. The promise of the arch woke up. Adrian felt it arrive through the flagstones — the thin cold wire of it, running under his boots — and this time the pain came with it inside four seconds, behind his left eye, and it stayed.

And the road came with it.

It was not a picture. He had braced for a picture, because a picture is what he would have built, and what he got was a *direction with a texture*: down, and out, and left of the yard, and there was a cold place in it that was not water and not stone, and a smell that was not a smell, which was the specific way his mind represented *this was where a decision had been made once and the decision is still standing*.

He got maybe nine seconds of it. He said, out loud, in English, with Vask repeating every word of it twice for the record: "Down and out. Past the low wall on the yard side, left, four steps and then it isn't steps, it's a landing. Under the landing there is a flat-faced stone set in the footing, and it is dry, and it is the only thing for a hundred paces that is completely dry."

"That is correct," said Vask. "That is the landing. That is the flat stone." A pause. "How did you know it was dry?"

"Because it has been somewhere it did not belong for a very long time, and it has been holding a shape instead of holding water, and a thing that holds a shape does not take a drop."

And then Tamsin stopped saying the oath.

She did not announce it. She simply took her feet out of the patch and stepped back onto bare stone, and the promise of the arch let go the way a hand lets go, and the wire under his boots went out, and the direction went with it, and the pain came up hard enough that he had to put his hand on the arch to stay upright.

It did not hurt. That was the strange part. It felt like the *loss* of a room he had been in for a moment, and then the pain arrived all at once, and his sight went grainy at the edges, and there was a smell of hot stone, and Roke was saying something with an edge on it, and Tamsin was standing four feet away with her arms folded and a face Adrian could not read.

"You stopped it," he said.

"You asked me to be asked first," she said. "So I stopped it. Do you understand what you have just done?"

"You stopped."

"I stopped, because you promised me nothing." She came forward. "You said the words on the arch. You said *I will find the stone and I will bring it back before the tide* and you said it in a room with a witness in it, and there was no witness to the promise, because I would not stand in it, and so the promise has nowhere to land." She was not angry. Her voice was level and it was much worse than angry. "That is what you wanted to find out. Now you know."

"Tell me the damage."

Nyla Ferrow, who had been standing in the corner by the door the entire time and had not spoken once, said in English, in a voice like a wet cloth being wrung out: "It is in the threshold stone, Englishman. Look down where I am looking, and do not step on the floor to do it."

He looked at the chapel stone.

It had a black line in it about the length of his forearm, running with the long axis of the slab, right where every boot in the city went over. It was thin and sharp and it was not a crack; it was too straight and too even and it had no shadow, and it had cut the worn bowl in the middle of the slab into two halves that were already not quite the same colour.

Behind it, four feet further into the room, the hairline in the flagstones was still exactly where it had been on Tuesday, a hand's width long, dry, and not going anywhere.

It was, Adrian understood, the shape of the thing on his palm.

Ottoline Vask got down on one knee and put the flat of her hand on the slab an inch away from the line, and did not touch it, and said, "That is a black line, and it has not opened. It means the promise was made and nobody took it. If somebody takes it, it closes. If you make it again, or make another one like it, it opens, and then it is not a line any more."

"Can it be repaired?"

"Not by the four of us. Not in this building, not in this season." She got up and wiped her hand on her apron. "Do you know what it looks like? It looks like a fault in a road. It looks like a place where a lock has failed, in a town, where everybody can see it every single time they come in to open the gate, forever. You will not be able to sand it out and you will not be able to argue about whether it is there." She stood up. "There is no way past this stone. Everybody in this city steps over it. Most of them will not see it, because they are looking at the gate. Some of them will step over it, and some of them will step on it, and both of them will know within a year that something on this stone is not right, and not one of them will be able to tell you what."

Adrian looked at it until his eyes began to ache, which he noticed as a small piece of professional interest rather than a portent.

"Write it in the record," he said. "Tamsin. Now, while I'm standing here. That I made a promise on your arch, that you would not stand in it, that the mark is mine, and that I am not going to ask you to be in anything again until I have told you what it costs. Put the date on it. Put my name on it in whatever way a person who cannot write in your hand has his name put down."

"That is what a record is for," she said.

Adrian looked at the line in the slab, and then at the four people who had just written down that he had made a promise to a room with nobody in it. In a year there would be strangers reading that sentence on a page, and not one of them would know whether he had been brave or stupid, and he found that he did not much care which. A page that has to be argued with is worth more than a man defending himself in a room where everybody already likes him.

It was the first time anything Adrian had done in Veyra had ended up in a place where it could not be quietly revised. He found that he wanted to put his hand flat on the slate the way you would on a window.

---

At the end, when the notary's slate was closed and Vask had banked the fire and the light through the arched window had gone the colour of wet slate, Ivenn Marrow put on his coat.

He did it slowly, which Adrian had already worked out was his whole method.

"The offer stays open," Marrow said. "It costs you nothing to leave it open. I am not going to take it back, because a man who takes back an offer has admitted it was a bait, and I have never in my life admitted to anything in a room I was not writing in." He straightened a cuff. "One more thing, and then I will go. I have been reading about a man who writes about permissions, in a book I have not read, written by a man I have not met, and the sentence that keeps coming back is *a door with nobody on the far side is not a door, it is a receipt.*"

Every muscle in Adrian's neck went cold.

"I have never seen that written down anywhere," Marrow said. "Not in the Pilgrim accounts, not in the Concord rolls, not in the Crown's own index. I have heard it. Once. In a voice, through a wall, in the last year I was at the Reed, and I assumed it was an old saying, and I have thought about it perhaps once a fortnight ever since." He buttoned his coat. "You are not the author, are you. You are a man who has been *given* a piece of language by something that has been listening to people like you for a very long time, and I would like to be the person who works out where it learned it, and I think you would like that too, and I think that is why you have not told the room about it."

"I—"

"You do not have to tell me anything. Not tonight." He went to the door, and stopped, and turned round. "Vale. Two pieces of practical advice from a man who has watched a great many people be careful at the wrong moment."

He held up one finger. "Do not let them put you in a chair. Somebody will offer you a chair and a title and a slate of your own, because it is *so much more comfortable*, and the chair is a version of a single hand, and you will not be able to see it because the chair is nice."

And a second finger. "And when you get up to the hearing, and the tide has come and gone, and the question in front of you is whether this lock can be worked by more than one person — say yes first. Before they have finished the question. Say it first and mean it, and then spend the rest of your life being honest about what it costs, because a man who says yes first is a man who can be trusted to keep saying yes on the day it is expensive."

He went out into the rain.

---

The lantern over the chapel door was still lit, and Adrian and Tamsin and Vask and Roke stood in the yard for a moment under it while the lock made its noises in the dark, and Adrian said, "You should have taken the offer," and Tamsin said, "I have a road to read, and I read it on foot, and I have been doing it since I was nine, and I will not have a man from a wall hand me the first one I am allowed to walk," and Vask said nothing, and Roke said, "Go to bed, Englishman," in a tone that almost sounded like it might, later, in a different mood, become a joke.

Adrian was not tired. He got as far as the arch.

He got a hand up onto the doorframe as he went past, and stopped, because the frame was cold in a way that stone should not be cold in a building where a fire had been banked an hour ago, and the chapel stone was up there behind him, out of the lamplight, and the black line in it was not doing anything at all.

And then a voice came out of it.

It was very quiet and it was not far away and it was not coming from below, and it said four words in the local language, slowly, with the flat patience of somebody who has had a long time to get the phrasing right.

Tamsin went white.

"That is the oath," she said. "That is the *old* form of the maintenance oath. The form we stopped using sixty years ago because it is the form that binds the gate *and the man who is standing in it*." She put her hand out and found the arch and held it. "Vale. That is not an echo. An echo repeats a thing. Somebody is saying the old words *tonight*."

The lantern over the chapel door went out.

Not guttered. Went out, in the middle of a wind that had not changed, with the wick still up.

In the dark, on the wet flagstones, in the chapel where a black line lay in the threshold stone that would be there for the rest of his life, the voice said the four words again, and then, in a voice Adrian would recognise for the rest of his life as the voice of an old friend being kind to him at the wrong moment, it said his name.
