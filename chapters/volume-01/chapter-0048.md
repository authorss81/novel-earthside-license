# Chapter 0048 — The Day Before

On the twenty-third day of the new month, which was the forty-eighth day of the flood and a Sunday, four people from this city stood on a walk-round in the wrong weather at a falling tide and looked at a stick with one mark cut on it, and a woman who has been arguing for nine days that one number is not a number a river keeps was right and was not heard. A page was written in this city's book whose whole purpose is to be read by somebody who is angry. Nine names were filled in over most of a day by a room that discovered that nine names is not a short list. A form was written for watching a hole in a floor that cannot mend it. A nineteen-year-old docker's name was found at the top of a page it was not allowed to be at the top of. A number was prepared in a gallery and not taken. And a mason's objection to the following day was put in front of about two hundred and forty people, not answered for the second time, answered by a docker in nine words, found unsatisfactory by the room, and not cared about at all by the crew.

And at about the eleventh hour of that night a man stood in a gallery two hundred steps below a chapel with a lamp between his boots and did not fill in a box, and a boy of nineteen came down the steps to ask him for a rule for the following day, and did not get one, and was asked for something else in the morning.

---

The four went down at about the second hour of the afternoon, early for a tide nobody is going to work, and the weather was the weather of the twenty-third day of this flood: not rain, not not-rain, the same for nine days.

Tamsin Quill went first because she has gone down first for seven years. Hettis Roke went second, because a Hearthguard going to a wall goes to it before the people who own the wall. Nell Bray went third, in a felt hat, because she is about sixty and does not hurry and has never once pretended to. The fourth was Peth Lomas, fifty, a mason, who said on the twenty-first day of the new month, in a room of forty-one people under a gate, that masons are not a crew and cannot be counted either way, and said so before anybody asked.

They went down the two hundred steps with a half-rotten rail and a low parapet, and the tide was on the ebb in the wrong weather, and what they went down for was a stick.

The gauge is a four-square staff of oak in the outer wall of the lock chamber at the second bay, cut and re-cut by masons for about two hundred years, with eleven marks on it that anybody alive can name and nine that nobody can. The mark that matters this week is the twenty-eighth from the bottom, cut on the twenty-seventh day of this flood, which was a Sunday, by a man who did not say why, and a notary entered that a mark was cut and did not enter why because nobody offered.

**It is the only number in this city that can stop a tide.**

Renna Ord's argument, which she has been making since the fortieth day in markets and doorways and once in a yard, is nine words long and she says it the same way every time:

"**That is one number. A river does not keep one number.**"

She was not on the walk-round. She was in the yard under the chapel and said it there at about the ninth hour, twice, to a mason and to a woman with a slate. She is fifty-eight, she wears a boat-coat, she cannot read, she has said for five weeks that the owing is the mechanism and the crew is the wall, and she has now said for nine days that a lock's whole future is decided four times a day by a groove cut into a piece of oak by a man who did not say why, and that in nine years there will be a Tuesday when that mark is cut by somebody in a hurry, and the sentence at the end of that will be *the mark was cut* and not *the tide was not worked*.

The notary entered that she was not answered, and the reason: the mark is not a rule, not a term and not a promise, and a thing which is none of those three can only be argued about, and arguing about it in this city happens in markets and not in books.

---

The terms were read for the last time in the yard under the lock chapel at about the tenth hour of that Sunday morning, and they took nineteen minutes, and Sera Vail read them in the language of the room and Tamsin Quill read them in English for the ones who needed them. The six conditions took a page and a half, and about two hundred and forty people had heard them twice before in three days and about nine of them had read them in advance.

Adrian Vale stood four feet back through all of it with a school exercise book and did not read them, and gave the reason he gave on the twenty-second day of the new month: **that they are not his and he was not going to stand in a room and make them sound like his.**

And then the notary read the seventh item off the page, which is not one of the six and is a rule about arithmetic:

**The cost is entered before the tide turns and not after.**

"There is a reason for that in the law I keep and it is the only one," Vail said, in the flat voice. "A cost entered after a thing has happened is a description of the thing. A cost entered before is a price, and a price is a thing somebody can refuse, and a man who is refused a price and turns up anyway has given something."

"So tomorrow's column is entered before this evening's tide turns, at the size it is now, and if it changes before six o'clock tomorrow it is entered again, and if it changes after six o'clock tomorrow then tomorrow's work happens at the old size and the difference is entered against the city and not against a man. **That is the whole difference between a price and a bill, and I have never in twenty-six years had occasion to say it in a yard.**"

And then the cost column was read aloud in full at its present size with the date on it — the column that has been entered six times in one month and has no final figure on the end of it — and its reading took four minutes on the fortieth day of the flood and eleven on the twenty-second day of the new month, and the length of a cost column in this city is a measure of how much trouble the city is in.

---

**And the nine names took most of the day, and the room discovered that nine names is not a short list.**

The ninth item of the instrument is not a condition and is in the ninth page anyway: the only way anybody in this city can stop the working is nine named persons saying one word, one of whom is named by herself, on the page since the twentieth day of the new month.

The room that was to fill it was the yard, and it filled in pieces over about nine hours, and the reason it took nine hours is the thing it learned in the first hour and did not know it had learned.

The first four people anybody thought of were all in the gallery or under the near gate: Hesk, who is the foreman of the day crew; Nell Bray, who is a docker; Renna Ord, who has said the crew is the wall; and Adrian Vale, the man in the seat, who was refused — not by himself, and that is entered — by a woman of forty-one from Silt Row in about forty people's hearing:

"**You cannot put him on the list of people who can stop the thing he agreed to on Saturday. That is not modesty. That is a list with the man in it at both ends, and a man who can stop a thing and a thing that was made by that man is one man with two hats, and I have been angry with him for nine days and I am not going to hand him a hat.**"

And then Wenna Cray said the thing that turned the room, and it is in the book in her own words and it is nineteen words long:

"**The crew cannot stop the crew. The crew is the hand. So the nine have to be people the working costs, and every one of them is owed something by it, and that is why it is a short list and not a long one.**"

And after that the room found the nine in about nine hours, and about four times in those nine hours somebody stood in front of a person and asked them a question they had not been asked before, and three times the person said no, and all three refusals are in the book with the reason.

> **Renna Ord, boat-coat, of this city, fifty-eight, who has named herself, and who says the ninth name is the only one anybody may take back.**
>
> **Hesk, foreman of the day crew, who said the four words under that gate for the first time in his life on the twenty-first day of the new month, voted against the same thing fourteen hours later, and asked to be asked.**
>
> **Nell Bray, docker, about sixty, who says a record is not a fence, and who is one of the four that go down with a gauge.**
>
> **Elsa Skell, of Corrow, forty-four, who works the bottom field, who has not spoken to a man from another world since the eighteenth day of this flood, and who is on this list because the working spends her field and not because she was asked nicely.**
>
> **Ilda Marsh, of Nine Elms, seventy, who refused the standing charge, wrote the fourth line, used it, and will not be the woman who makes it two.**
>
> **Hessa Ruun, water-count of the Reed Reach, about fifty, not a notary and the closest thing the wetland has to one, who said on the twenty-third day that a concession is a document a Crown scholar reads.**
>
> **Hesper Saye, bone-setter, fifty-eight, who has told four hundred men a thing about a shoulder or a scalp and has been wrong about ninety of them, and who is on this list because the cost of this is a wrist and she is told.**
>
> **Peth Lomas, mason, fifty, not a crew, who said so before anybody asked, and who is the fourth that goes down with a gauge, because a mason cannot be counted either way.**
>
> **Nyla Ferrow, keeper of the lock chapel, about seventy, whose ground the scar is under, the oldest name on the list, and the only one on it who is there because of a floor.**
Three people said no and all three are in the book. A flood-creel woman of about fifty with a hood up said no in nine words: **that she is the ninth name of a widening, and a widening does not get a name in it.** A salt factor of about forty-eight said no, and took four minutes, and said that a man who is owed a season cannot also be the man who stops the thing that spends it, because then the only way to stop it is to have already lost — **and that is the Brass Measure's fourth term in a different coat, and he said it before anybody said it.**

And **Marek Osley asked to be put back on after the nine were filled, and was refused by the room in four minutes, and the reason is Wenna Cray's: a man who owes a thing and stops the thing that spends it has found the cheapest door in this city, and the cheapest door opens when the person who owes has already lost.**

---

**And the form for watching the scar was written on the twenty-third day of the new month in the chapel, by a keeper with a piece of chalk, and by a woman of fifty-eight with tweezers, and by a mason, and it is in the book, and it is four items long, and it is the answer to a question that the whole of the last ten days has been asking, which is what anybody is actually supposed to do about a black mark in a place.**

The ground under the lock chapel was opened at the hairline in the flagstones on the nineteenth day of this flood and closed and re-boarded on the fortieth day of this flood, with the date chalked on the underside, and the boards have not been lifted since and nobody lifts them. What is under them is entered as **SCARRED** in the ledger, described and not named, in the words of a mason and an anchorwright — and the description is also a description of a seam in a wall, and a description is not a place.

Nyla Ferrow sat down on the step of her own arch at about the first hour of the afternoon, with a slate on her knees, and dictated four items to a girl of seventeen, and the width of the column is entered as a fact for the second time in the same year.

> **One. A line of salt in a shallow cut in the boarding itself, between the fourth and fifth boards from the north end, changed by whoever is nearest when it goes damp. The state of it is a reading and not a mood. Salt that has been damp and dried and dampened again breaks differently from salt that has only been wet, and a woman of fifty-eight with tweezers taught me that in nine minutes.**
>
> **Two. Four marks in chalk on the boards at four fixed points, from which any person with a straight edge may take a depth. The marks are not wiped for anything. If a boot is on a mark the depth is taken when the boot is not on it, and the date of the taking goes on the slate and not on the floor.**
>
> **Three. Four people, turning up, twice a day, in the open, at the arch, and one of them is me because it is my ground and I am not going to be a person who visits it. No list, no name at the top of anything, and a person may say in this yard that they have been and have not been, and both are entered.**
>
> **Four. The state of the salt, the four depths, the hour, the date, and whether anything came up, on a slate on a nail inside the arch where anybody coming in can read it without being asked to come further in. And the price of the salt, which is about a penny the quarter, written under the four items, because in nine months somebody will ask what a form costs, and a form with no price on it is a favour, and a favour is a thing that stops.**

Then the notary asked her the question this city's whole book exists to ask, and the answer is entered with it, neither above the other:

> **What this form cannot do, in the words of the keeper of the ground: it cannot close anything. It cannot mend a dressed joint under a floor. It cannot be done from a floor that has not been lifted, and the floor has not been lifted since the fortieth day of this flood and is not going to be lifted again in this flood. And it cannot say where the residue of tomorrow's working will go, because nobody in this city knows that, and neither does the man from another world, and he has said so in a hall.**
>
> **And it is a form and not a promise, and I would like the word form next to my name. A promise is what a keeper says on Sunday. A form is what a keeper does on Monday. I have kept that ground for thirty years and I am seventy, and I would like it entered that I noticed that about four people in this city confuse the two.**

Ottoline Vask was there, and she read the four items in the local language for eleven masons and a docker and did not add a fifth.


---

**And then the fourth page of the instrument was written, and it is the one nobody wanted, and it is the page about the day the crew does not turn up, and Sera Vail wrote it herself at about the sixth hour of the evening of the twenty-third day of the new month in a yard with about two hundred and forty people in it, and she read it out twice, and about nine people in that yard have said since that they did not know it was going to be there and were glad.**

> **What happens on the day the crew does not turn up.**
>
> **The join in the main breach is where it is and is not worked, and this instrument is not suspended by anybody, because there is nobody in it to suspend. It is not withdrawn. It is not ended. It stands.**
>
> **The record of that day says that the crew did not turn up. It does not say a fault. It does not name a man. It does not say a name was sent for, and there is no entry of anybody going to anybody's door, and that last is a term of this instrument and not a practice of this city.**
>
> **The cost column stands as it was last entered, and the difference between that figure and the truth is a gap and is not an error, and a gap is left open and is not closed by anybody writing over it.**
>
> **The next tide is not worked until four people have been down the walk-round in the wrong weather with a gauge. That is the whole of what a stopped thing is and of what it costs.**
>
> **A cost that stops is a cost that has stopped, and is not a thing that has been paid, and is never to be entered as paid, by anybody, on any day, for any reason.**

And the notary said, in her own words, in the yard, after the second reading:

"**I have been a notary in this city for twenty-six years. I have entered four instruments in that time I wanted to strike out on the day I entered them, and one of those four was mine. I have never entered a page whose purpose is to be read by somebody who is angry, and I have never wanted to enter one. This is the first. And my not wanting it is not an argument against it, because the only page I ever wanted to enter that nobody wanted was a page about a man I was fond of, and I entered it, and there is a grave behind an ordinary wall.**"

And a man from another world said, in English, in front of about two hundred and forty people, and it is in the book in his own words and it is four words long:

"**Then that is the point.**"

About four people in that yard understood what he meant and about two hundred and thirty did not, and Vask has entered that she was one of the four.

**And Renna Ord wanted one line changed, and it was changed, and the change is entered, and what she wanted was the word *refused* and what is on the page is the word *not owing*.**

"That page says the crew did not turn up," she said. "I want it to say the crew refused."

"No," said Vail.

"Then say they were not owing. **Say the city did not pay.** Because if the page says refused, then the crew refused, and a crew that refused can be asked again and can refuse again, and in nine months it is a thing they do on Tuesdays, and a thing you do on Tuesdays is not an owing, it is a habit, and a habit costs nobody anything, and the wall will have a hole in it the shape of a habit."

"You want the page to say the city failed."

"I want the page to say **who** failed," said Renna Ord, "because it is not the crew's page. It has never been the crew's page. It is the only page in that book that is not the crew's page and it is the one about the crew not being there, and if the only page about forty men not turning up is a page about forty men, then in four years this city will have a book that says the crew stopped and a clerk in a building in Aurel will read it and be right."

And it was changed at about the seventh hour of the evening, in about two minutes, in about two hundred and forty people's hearing, and the change is entered with the request and the requester and the hour, and what the page says is: **the record of that day says that the crew were not owed, and did not come, and that there is no fault and no name and no door.**

And Renna Ord said, "That is worse than mine," and Vask said, "Yes," and Renna Ord said, "Good."

---

**The second page of the roll was found at about the fourth hour of the afternoon, and it had a name at the top of it, and the name was the boy who keeps the roll.**

The roll of forty names has no name at the top of it, and Sera Vail will strike out any name put there, and the reason is the oldest rule in this book: a list with a man's name above it is an order, and a thing nobody has asked for cannot be owed.

The first page is the forty names. The second page is the wages: forty rows, the ordinary shift rate, the ordinary flood-creel wage, and a column for what has been paid, which is empty, because the city has no money and the city's credit is a field four miles away. The roll was made on the twenty-first day of the new month.

**At the top of the second page, above the forty rows, in a boy's hand, was a date: the twenty-first day of the new month.** That is correct and Vail said so and nobody argued with her.

**Beside the date was a name, in the same hand: the name of the man who keeps the roll.** Nineteen, out of the day crew, eight watches in three weeks, the boy who wrote *works* in the tide table on the eighteenth day with the *k* pressed too hard, who asked for a rule in a gallery on that Tuesday and was refused the first time and given it the second, and who voted against the distributed working on the twenty-first.

His reason for the name, when Hettis Roke read it out of him in a yard, was eleven words long:

"**A page with no date on it is a page that can be moved to a different month.**"

Nobody in that yard could answer it, and about nine of them thought it was a very good reason, and Ottoline Vask thought so too, and she said the sentence she said on the twenty-second day of the new month in about nine seconds:

"**It is a name at the top of a page. I said in a yard that if a name goes at the top of it I will read it out in a yard, and I have said that about a list before and I have been right about it.**"

And she read it out — the date and the name and the reason and the eleven words — in the local language, in front of about two hundred and forty people, and a boy of nineteen stood in that yard and did not go out. Renna Ord has entered that she was one of about nine who did not go out either, and the reason is that a man who walks out of a yard when a thing is read about him has decided the thing is not true, and she does not know that it is not true.

The name came off the page at about the fifth hour. **The date stayed.** The entry says the name was struck out by the notary and the date left by the person who put it there, and the reason the date stayed is Vask's: **that a page of wages with no date on it is a page that can be moved to a different month; and it was true; and a true reason in a boy's hand is still a name at the top of a page.**


---

**And then the gallery, at about the ninth hour of the evening, with nineteen men in it, and the twenty-fourth reading prepared and not taken.**

The lower gallery is a barrel-vaulted chamber over three arched bays. The pale edge of the join in the main breach is in the second bay's wall. The tide table is on the wall of the first bay. The fourth column of the roll is nowhere near any of it.

Nineteen men of the day crew were in the gallery at about the ninth hour of the evening because it is where they stand twice a day, and Adrian Vale was in there with a folding steel rule and a stub of chalk, four feet back from the wall of the third bay, doing the thing he has done about nine times in four days, which is to set up a reading and not take it.

He checked that the straight edge sits true. He wiped the dust off the fifth chalked mark — the docker's *nine* is on that wall, and one of the five carries a boy's number four inches under it and nine inches to the left, and neither has been moved since the eighteenth day of the new month, and he wiped the boy's and not the *nine* — and then stood there with the rule against the stone and did not read it.

"You are going to take it," said Nell Bray, from the step, in a felt hat.

"No."

"Nobody has ever been told that."

"I am telling you now," he said. "**A reading taken on the ebb of a working tide is a number. A number taken on the wrong water is a lie, and it is worse than a lie, because it is arithmetic.** Every one of those twenty-three was taken on the same water at the same hour with the same edge. If I take the twenty-fourth at the ninth hour of the evening of an ordinary ebb, then in four months somebody in a room will put the two side by side and they will both be right and the difference between them will be nothing. **A ruled line is a fact and a missing line is a fight, and so is a number that is right in the wrong place.**"

"That is the same sentence twice," said Hesk, from the sill.

"One about the page and one about the river. I have been down here nine times in four days and I have taken nothing, and I want that entered tonight in those words, and I want a woman of forty-one to be the one who hears it."

Nell Bray said, from the step of the second bay, in a felt hat, in about nine seconds, and it is in the book in her own words:

"**That is the first true thing anybody in this gallery has said about a number in forty days, and you should have said it on the fortieth night.**"

Wenna Cray was not in the gallery. Hettis Roke entered that she had been asked to be, and that she sent back one line from a room above the market with a slate: *then the twenty-fourth is taken tomorrow at the hour the column is kept at and not before, and somebody tell me what that hour is, because I have been in this book for nine days and I do not know it.* And the hour is the seventh hour of the morning, and it is now on a board in a room above the market, and it is the first time a person from another world has been told a time by a person who does not keep time for him.

---

**And the objection was put in front of the room again, and it was not answered again.**

Ottoline Vask stood in the yard under the chapel at about the seventh hour of the evening, in front of about two hundred and forty people, and put the two sentences in front of the room a second time, and said she was doing it on purpose and was not going to soften one word of them, and that if she does she will read them out in a yard again.

"**One. A floor that has come open once is not a pattern. It is a hole.** And I was outvoted in four minutes on the thirty-seventh day of this flood about a rescue and I was right, and I have not taken it back, and I am not going to use the dead to make my point."

"**Two. The forty words under that near gate are the toll and they are the pattern, and a city cannot spend a thing and be its own foundation at the same time.**"

And the cost of the second sentence, which she will never give a figure for, is not a season. It is a hundred and forty acres four miles down the Reed Reach road that can no longer promise a gate-opening on a date, and the *year of certainty* gone out of a field on an ordinary morning.

She put it in the yard and then stood there, and there was no answer, and **Vask entered that the objection is unanswered, that it will be unanswered the day the working is done, and that a person who has said the same true thing twice in public and been answered zero times has not lost the argument; and that this is now the argument of her life and being right about it is not a comfort to her.**

And then a docker in a boat-coat, fifty-eight, who cannot read, said from about nine feet back, nine words:

"**The toll is the crew. The date is March.**"

And about two hundred and forty people in a yard under a chapel did not find that satisfactory. It was too short, it had no cost in it, and it did not address the fact that the foundation of a city was about to be made more expensive on purpose with a date on it.

And Renna Ord said, before anybody could say anything, "**It isn't satisfactory for me either. I have four men in that crew who are going to be hurt before March and I cannot stop one of them and neither can the notary, and I have said that in a yard and it is in the book. I have also said the crew is the wall and a wall cannot be a price for very long, and I meant it, and I am not going to unsay it because a mason has been right twice in four days and it is easier to be against her than against a field.**"

And the crew did not care. Thirty-seven of forty voted for this thing on the twenty-first day of the new month, three against, one abstention, the three against by name. And about nine people in that yard understood that the objection had been answered by a woman who is not on the roll and cannot be, and the nine were right and the two hundred and thirty were also right.

---

At about the eleventh hour of the night of the twenty-third day of the new month, in the lower gallery, with a lamp between his boots, a man stood in front of a table on a wall.

The tide table has a row for every tide of the salt year and a last column, and the last column has one word in it for a tide that was worked and is blank for a tide that was not. Four words have been written in that column in fifty days and all four are the same word. The word against the thirty-third is in a docker's hand with a barrow and the *k* is pressed too hard. The word against the forty-third is in a boy's hand, small, and the *k* is pressed too hard.

**The row for the forty-ninth day of the flood, which is tomorrow, and which is a Monday, is empty.**


He did not write in it tonight. He entered, at about the eleventh hour of that night, in a note of nine words, that **he has not written in that box once in forty-eight days; that the row for tomorrow is a Monday; that he is barred from the roster of persons who may open the working; and that a man who is barred may also keep his hands off a table in a gallery. Nobody has asked him to write in it and he is going to stop going down to look at it.**

Then he looked at his right palm in the lamplight. Two inches of hair-thin black in the heel of it, the same width it has been since the second morning of this flood, forty-seven days old, not moved one sixteenth of an inch; and a thin pale ghost running toward the wrist, which is all that is left of a mark he put into a wall on the fourth day and could have taken back, and did not.

**Nothing gained.** A page, nine names, a form of watching a hole, and a word struck out of a page. He is a witness and only a witness. He can perceive the nearest join-line and he cannot create a working, he cannot open a threshold, he cannot hold a place, and there is a box in a table on a wall that he is not going to fill in.

The boy came down the two hundred steps at about the half hour past the eleventh hour and stood at the bottom of them in the wet, having come down them in the dark, and he said:

"I want the rule for tomorrow."

"You asked twice. Refused the first time, given it the second, and both are in the book, because you asked for the first one to be left in."

"I asked for the rule for tomorrow."

"**There is no rule for tomorrow.**"

The boy stood on the bottom step in the rain and did not say anything for about four seconds.

"Then what is there?"

"A shift. At the sixth hour, at the ordinary volume, out of the line and not together. About nine of the nineteen of you under that gate and about eleven holding the stage, and nobody will ask you anything, because nobody who opens it is allowed to ask you anything — the condition that stops a single person from doing it is the same condition that stops a single person from stopping you."

"You said I asked for the rule and you gave me the answer."

"I gave you the answer. You asked for the rule."

"What is the difference?"

"**The answer is in a person and the rule is in a page, and the page is what is left when the person is asleep or hurt or sitting on a step at half past eleven at night having asked twice.**" Adrian looked at him. "And I am not the man who gives you the rule, and I want that on the page in those words. I was asked on the twenty-first day of the new month to be struck off the roster of people who may open this, and it took two hours and ten minutes to get accepted, and about nine people in that room thought I was building a martyr, and the sentence that got it accepted was said by a woman of twenty-four who is four foot eleven, and it was about a page. **So the rule is not mine to give you. The only people who can give you a rule for tomorrow are forty men who turn up at six and are not going to be in a room about it.**"

The boy took that in. It took about four minutes, and then he said:

"Then I will be in the water at six."

"Yes."

"Then somebody will have to be in the water at six with me," said Ovin Sarr, "and it will not be a rule, and that is worse, and I would like it entered that I said worse."

"It is entered," said Adrian Vale, "and I am going to write down tonight that you came down two hundred steps in the dark to ask me for a thing I am not allowed to give you; that I have been struck off; and that the only reason I know what tomorrow is and you do not is that I read numbers off a wall for a living and you hold a gate."

He wrote it in the exercise book on a page with somebody else's name on the cover, and went up the two hundred steps, and the black line in the chapel stone said nothing while he was on it, and it has said nothing since the twenty-fifth day of the flood.
