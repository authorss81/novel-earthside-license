# Chapter 0044 — A More Attractive Form

On the nineteenth day of the new month, which was the forty-fourth day of the flood and a Wednesday, a Crown Law scholar explained for about two hours, in a room in a house with a burnt lintel two hundred steps above the salt works, a thing he had read about for nineteen years, and it was almost entirely true, and it required nothing of the city of Saltwake at all, and it would have cost that city no money and no labour and no season and no man, and he did not ask for obedience and he did not ask for a mark, and at about the eighth hour of the evening of the same day, in a yard, in front of about two hundred people, it was refused by a man who has not refused anything in a room for five weeks, with the true reason and not the fair one, and a mason of fifty put his hand on his shoulder in front of about forty people and said that he had thanked him for the other one and that he was sorry about the other one.

---

The room above the market had about nine people in it at about the fourth hour of the afternoon of the nineteenth day of the new month, and the rota on the loft wall has thirty-one names on it in four columns and the fourth column is what the work is for and not what the person is for, on a notary's ruling, and there is a ruled line under one name and a date and nothing in the fourth column, and the fourth column has been empty for ten days and it is going to be empty for eleven and nobody in that room has said so and nobody in that room has filled it in.

"What did they want on Monday?" said Mina Okoye.

"About ninety hands for the Crown's," said Adrian Vale. "Forty hands for the second. Nobody for the first, and four people afterwards who said they had wanted the first."

"And the count?"

"There was no count. Vail would not run one. It is entered as a show of hands, with the number of hands and the number of people in the room, and she has entered that a room that has been asked to choose among three things in twenty minutes and has shown ninety hands for one of them has chosen what it was given and not what it wanted."

"**Then we lost it in a room in twenty minutes**," said Noor Haddad, "and I have been sitting here since the fourth hour trying to work out what the ninety hands were for, and it is not a Crown's, because the Crown's has a lawyer in it and a lawyer in this city is a man from Aurel who has read two hundred and forty words in a hall and a clerk who is not a lawyer and a notary who is neither. It is a stipend. It is quarterly. It is money that arrives from outside and does not have to be got out of a field by nineteen people at four in the morning, and about ninety people in this city have not been paid for anything since the first day of the month and the whole of this city owes one man a season, and a man with a barrow told me on Monday evening in the yard that she had put her hand up and that she would put it up again and that if I asked her why she would say **because I am tired**, and that is not a reason and it is the reason."

"Enter it," said Priya Shah, from the step, with a slate.

"I do not want it in the book that the people of this city are tired."

"Adrian." Priya Shah is twenty-four and she has been writing down what a person said for nineteen days and she stopped a word in public once in a hall and a mason has thanked her for it. "If nobody writes down that ninety hands were a stipend, then in about four months a person in a building is going to read that the people of Saltwake chose a single-author Crown containment and that is what is going to happen, and it will not be entirely false, and that is the sentence I do not want. **Write down that ninety hands for the Crown's was a quarterly payment and that a woman of about fifty-eight said *because I am tired*.**"

"It is written down," said Adrian Vale. "It was in my notebook at the ninth hour on Monday night and I have not shown it to anybody and I am showing it to you now because I have been wrong about one thing for three days and I would like somebody in this room to catch me at it."

---

He said it at about the fifth hour and it took about nine minutes and there are about nine people in the room and all nine entered something about it afterwards, which Vail has noted, because in forty days nobody in that room has entered anything about anything.

"I have been carrying something since Saturday night and I have been wrong about it since Monday night, and the thing I have been wrong about is this.

"On Saturday night a man in a good coat said a hundred and thirty words in a yard about a lock, in about two minutes, in very reasonable language, and a notary entered them in the fourth volume of this city's book in a very neat small hand, exactly as they were said, with the hour, and then entered underneath them that it is not the notice from Aurel and that the two documents that say the same thing do not agree with each other. And I read that page on Sunday morning and I wrote in a notebook that I was going to find out what the sentence is in a room, from somebody who is very good at rooms, and I have not done that, and on Monday night I found out what it is in a room from a woman of twenty-nine who read it out loud twice and said one word, and the word is *standing*, and I have not told anybody what the word is until this afternoon, and I asked a notary on Monday night not to enter it for two days, and she entered the request and did not ask me for a reason, and she has said she will ask me for a reason tomorrow, on the twentieth day of the month, in a room and not in a corridor, and I am going to have to give her one, and I am going to give it to her in a room with about four people in it, and then she can have it too.

"**And here is what I have been wrong about, and it is the only thing I am going to say and then I am going to go up two hundred steps.**

"Tuesday morning, in a gallery under a lock, a man of forty-one from this city asked me in front of nineteen men whether I was going to enter a number as a fact or as a fault, and I said a fact, and I said it in about forty seconds, and about nine people in that gallery did not believe me, and one of the nine was the woman who asked. And she asked me because a fault can be given to somebody and a fact can only be carried, and she was right, and I said the true thing, and the true thing is that the number moved because a mason took a prop out of that gate at the turn of a tide and entered it in her own hand before I asked her to, and a crack does not step, and that is a fact about this city and not about a person.

"And on Monday night I sat in this room and worked out that the reason the ninety hands went up for a Crown's clerk is **that a Crown's clerk is a man who can be sent.** He is not here. He answers to a building in Aurel and he does not stand on a staging and he does not hold a line and he does not come down two hundred steps in the rain, and he cannot be sent to a village, and he cannot be asked in a yard, and he cannot be made to say a number out loud in front of a woman from Silt Row.

"And that is the whole of what I have been wrong about, and it took me three days and a woman with a bowl already had it on Monday, and I want it entered that it took me three days and that a woman with a bowl is fifty-eight years old."

---

**Sabra Vint's house has a burnt lintel and it is four hundred yards above the salt works and two hundred steps above the lock, and the two hundred steps are the walk-round's steps backwards, and a man in a good coat asked to use it, and she said yes, and the reason she said yes is in the book in her own words and is that he came to her house on the sixteenth day of the month and read her paper aloud in a yard in front of about two hundred and ten people and could not read one character of it and then said so, in a yard, and entered that he had said it, and she has been coming down those steps nine times in nine years asking for that paper and this is the eleventh time and it is the first time anybody has come up them.

The room has a table in it, a stove, a chair with a cushion mended four times, and a sheet of paper on a waxed board on the wall with nineteen shapes on it that a mason of thirty-four years has never been able to get her hands on, and the shapes are not writing in any language anybody in that room has, and the woman who keeps it is sixty-one, has lived above the salt works for forty-one years, and cannot read.

Ivenn Marrow sat in the mended chair with a slate under his arm and he was at his best, and being at his best is the most frightening thing about him and there is no instrument in this world that can audit it.

He explained for about two hours. He did not use a document. He did not have one, and he said so in the first ninety seconds — that he had a letter from Aurel and was not going to use it, and that the letter was for somebody else and about a different matter and that a man who brings the wrong paper into a room has already told you what he is.

What he offered was this.

"That the containment of the main breach in the gate be held by the holder of the pull. By you. Not by a clerk. Not by a committee. Not by a quorum of nine that meets when it feels like it and cannot be got for a fortnight in March.

"That it require nothing of Aurel. No workway. No mark. No stipend. No advocate. No seal. No office. **No Crown.** That it be entered in this city's own book, in this city's own words, and that the Registry's demand of the twenty-eighth day of the flood be answered by this and not by anything from that office, and that a man in a good coat will enter in the book, under his own hand, that the Crown has no part in it and no claim on it and no standing in it whatsoever.

"And that your answer on the containment of that join be the answer, and that it not be answerable by any other person in Saltwake. That is the whole of it. It is one clause. It is the fourth clause of the Crown's third instrument with the Crown taken out of it, and there is no more in the Crown's than there is in mine, and I have read both and the Crown's has about four hundred more words in it and every one of the four hundred is about which office is responsible for a year."

He said that in about nine minutes and then he stopped and let the room have it, and there were four people in the room: Adrian Vale, Sabra Vint, and two more who were asked to be there because a refusal in this world has to be entered and an entry needs two witnesses who can say they heard it.

The two more were **Hettis Roke**, Hearthguard of the outer ward, nineteen years in, with a waxed slate, and **Peth Lomas**, a mason of fifty, who cut a slab for a grave on Sunday morning and whose name is under a true sentence on a keeper's board in Adrian's own handwriting.

**He did not ask for obedience and he did not ask for a mark and he asked for a seat.**

That is in the book, in Roke's hand, entered by Roke on the night of the nineteenth day of the new month, and it is the reason Adrian Vale did not have the sentence ready, and it is entered as the reason and it is this:

> **That the thing asked for was a seat, and that a seat is a place to be angry at, and that this is the definition of a seat given in front of a hall of about two hundred and eleven people on the eleventh day of the flood by a docker in a felt hat and entered in the ledger, and that a seat has to be legible to a person who does not like you, and that this is the one argument against a thing in this city that cannot be got round, and that I could not get round it, and that I have been trying to get round it since about the seventh hour of the evening and I am not able to, and that I would like it entered that I could not get round it and did not say so, and that the man asking for it did not point at it once in two hours.**

---

Adrian Vale did not refuse him for two hours. He asked eleven questions and the man in the good coat answered eleven of them, and about nine of the eleven answers were true, and two of them were better than true, and one of them was the reason.

The one that was the reason came at about the eighth hour of the evening, and Adrian Vale did not raise his voice, and Roke wrote all of it down, and the whole of it is in the book and it is about nine hundred words and it is the entry that is going to be read in about four months by somebody in a building.

"You have done this three times since the second night of this flood and you have done it in three different places and I have watched all three and I have not been able to say what they were until Tuesday morning in a gallery under a lock, and I am going to say it now and I am going to be wrong in some part of it and I want to be wrong in front of you rather than in a notebook.

"The first was a chalked offer on a keeper's board that said *expect you to do as I ask*, and you withdrew it yourself in daylight in front of thirty people and wrote under it that the answer he gave was better than the one you would have given, and asked him for the true reason, in his own hand, on that board, in a place where anybody who comes up that yard has to read it. **One true reason. In one hand. In the one place in a city where a keeper keeps things.**

"The second was two hours of translation in a hall in front of about three hundred and fifty people, word for word, and you kept the four disagreements in and added one sentence saying you had not removed them, and then you said, unasked, that a man in a good coat doing the thing he is best at in a room of frightened people is genuinely useful and that this is the dangerous part. **One account. In one hand. And the honest thing about a document in one hand is that you can say what you did not remove, and only you can say it.**

"And the third was Saturday night, a hundred and thirty words about a lock, in about two minutes, in very reasonable language, in a yard, in front of about two hundred and ten people, and a notary wrote it down in a very neat small hand exactly as it was said, and then wrote down underneath it that it is not the notice from Aurel, and that the two documents which say the same thing do not agree with each other. **One reason, in one hand, in one book, entered by one person, and that person is you, and you have been the only person in this city who has ever been able to say what is in that hundred and thirty words, and everybody else in this city can only repeat it.**

"And I want to say the last half of that out loud, because I have been carrying it since Thursday and it is the sentence I have been unable to say to a room, and it is about you and it is not an accusation and I am not going to pretend for one second that it is.

"**A man in a good coat asked me for the true reason and I wrote mine. A man on a table told me the difference between an argument and a record, and it took him four minutes and it took me nineteen days, and you have been in this city eight weeks and you did not have to wait for a man to die to work it out.**"

Roke's slate stopped about a second before the sentence ended and started again, and he has entered that it stopped.

**Ivenn Marrow's face did not do anything.**

That is Roke's entry, in his own hand, in the flat voice he uses when he is entering a thing he does not understand: **that the face of the man in the good coat did not do anything at the end of nine hundred words said to him in a room by a man he has been the most useful man in the room to for eight weeks, and that I have been in this city nineteen years and I have watched about four hundred men be told a true thing in a yard and I have watched what four hundred faces do, and I have never once seen a face do nothing on a true thing, and that is the first time and I am putting it in the book because in nine years somebody is going to want to know when he found out.**

And then the man in the good coat said, in the local language, in about four seconds, and then again in English so that it would be in the book in both:

> **"I did not have a man to die. I have nineteen years of other people's records, and a great many of them are about men who did not die."**

And Roke wrote it down exactly, and there are about eleven words in it, and it is in the book, and Adrian Vale has written in a notebook that the sentence is the whole of the man and that he worked it out in four seconds and that he was not defending himself and was not attacking and was answering a question, and that the question was about arithmetic, and that the answer was arithmetic, and that **a man who has kept the record for nineteen years and is asking to be the only hand that answers is not asking for a seat, and I have been in this city forty-three days and I know exactly what it is and I do not have the name of it and I am not going to invent a name for it in a room tonight, because the notary in this city wrote on the thirty-first day of the month that a quarter of a thing is not a thing and it is the right rule and it is her rule and she borrowed it off a mason, and there is a man in that room at the table with a bare right arm and a sheet of paper on the wall that I cannot read.**

---

**The refusal was given at about the fourth hour of the evening of the nineteenth day of the new month in a yard with about two hundred people in it, and it was entered the same night, and the two witnesses are Hettis Roke and Peth Lomas and the notary was there and read it back at about the ninth hour.**

He said it in about two minutes. It is in the book and it is not one sentence, and the first half of it is about the offer and the second half of it is about himself, and about forty people in that yard have said that the second half was the reason.

"No.

"And I want to be exact about what I am refusing, because I have been in this city forty-three days and I have refused four things and three of them were about me and one of them was about a man who is under a board, and I am not going to be a man who refuses a thing in a yard and lets the yard think it was about something easier.

"**It is the offer, and the whole of the offer is the fourth clause.** Everything else in it is better than the Crown's and cheaper than the Crown's and the notary has said so in a book and I am not going to stand here and say a word against it. No Crown. No workway. No mark. No stipend. My own book, my own words, and a man in a good coat putting under his own hand that the Crown has no part in it. I could not have written a better offer in a month and I have read it four times and I am not going to pretend I have not.

"And the fourth clause is that my answer is the answer, and it cannot be answered by anybody in this city, and it has to be like that because a join wants one pair of hands and a mason of thirty-four years will tell you that and she is right.

"**And the sentence that ends it is a sentence a woman of twenty-nine read out loud on Monday night in a room of two hundred and forty people, and it is in this city's record, and it is a hundred and forty years old, and nine people in this city can still say it and four of them were in that room and nobody asked them.** The old form of the oath binds the gate and the standing of the man in it. It was taken out of the oath in this city sixty years ago. A man in it died, and the reason he died is that the standing was a man and the man could not be got and the gate did not open and about four hundred people were on the wrong side of a river in a flood month, and there is no stone with his name on it anywhere and the list is in a building in Aurel.

"And on Monday a Crown's clerk stood in a hall and read a notice that has the same word in it. And on Saturday a man in a good coat said a hundred and thirty words that are the same word with the man left out of them. And tonight in a room above the salt works a man offered me the same word and took the Crown out of it so that it would cost this city nothing, **and that is the most attractive thing anybody has put in front of me since the second night of this flood and it is the same thing three times.**

"And I cannot give the reason in one sentence and I have tried for two hours. The reason is this and it is about me and it is the true reason and I have never said it in a room.

"**On the sixteenth day of the new month, in a converted salt store, I said in front of about forty people that a rule which makes a man the answer for a floor is the reason a man went into a floor three days earlier, and a notary stopped me in the middle of it and made me say it as what I said, and it is in the book, and I am still saying it, and about nine people in that room disagreed with me and one of them was a woman of seventy from a village that has refused twice and one of them was a man who cut a slab for a grave on Sunday morning.**

"**The person who is going to be the standing is me.** And I do not have a mark of my own and I never will in this world, and I have refused a workway three times, and the third time was in a hall of three hundred and ten people and the reason is chalked on a door in my own handwriting, and I am the only person in this city who cannot be replaced, and a man on a wall in a gallery said that to me on Tuesday morning in one sentence and said nothing else for two hours, and he is right, and I have not been able to put it down for two days.

"So: **no. And the second no is the one that matters, and it is that I am not going to be the reason again, and if a join in a gate wants one pair of hands then I would rather it wanted forty men twice a day and a mason and a woman with a slate and a woman with a bowl than it wanted me, and I would rather that be a piece of work and a cost in a book and something four people have to be angry at, because that has a name and a date and a person who can be asked, and I cannot be asked for it because I am the one it is about.**"

---

**Sabra Vint was asked what she thought.**

It is in the book, and the asking is in the book, and the asking was Adrian Vale's and he asked her because she was the only person in that yard who could not read, and the reason he asked her is in the book and is his, and it is: **because there are about two hundred people in that yard and every one of them has a standing and I wanted one person there who could not be asked for anything by anybody and could not be answered by anybody either, and there is one.**

She is sixty-one, she has lived above the salt works for forty-one years, she has kept a sheet of paper for nine years, and she has come down two hundred steps eleven times, and she has been given a woman with a bowl and a page nine times and has never once been given the sheet.

"I have been asked by a man from another world what I think," she said, in the local language, and Peth Lomas read it out afterwards for about thirty people who would not have had it, "and I am going to say the true thing about my own paper and then I am going to go home, and I am not on your side and I am not on his side and I would like both of those entered.

"The true thing about my paper is this and it has taken me nine years to be able to say it in one breath.

"**I write the shapes. I have never once written what I think the shapes mean.** Nine years, once a year, nineteen shapes, and the page stops at the shapes. I have never told a single living person what I make of them, and I have never been asked, and about nine people in this city have had the paper in their hands and not one of them has asked me that question, including the man who read it out loud in front of two hundred and ten people on Saturday night and could not read one character of it and said so in a yard and entered that he had said it, which was more than anybody else has done.

"And there are about two hundred people standing in this yard arguing about who gets to keep the record of a join in a wall, and the woman who has been trying to give a record of nineteen stones to this city for nine years has not been asked one question about it, and so I am going to say the thing that is in my paper and it is one sentence and it is not about either of you.

"**Somebody has to be the one who writes down what they think it means, and I have not done it for nine years, and that is the only thing I have done wrong in nine years, and I am sixty-one and I would like to stop.**"

And about four people in that yard understood that sentence and about two hundred did not, and she said afterwards that she had expected about four and that nine years is a long time to be the only one who is not writing things down.

---

Peth Lomas put his hand on Adrian Vale's shoulder in that yard at about the fourth hour and a half of the evening of the nineteenth day of the new month, in front of about forty people, and said, in the local language, and it is in the book in his own words:

"On the seventh day of the new month you wrote a sentence about yourself on a keeper's board in your own hand and I read it out in that yard and put my name under it because I agreed with it, and it is a true sentence and it is about you, and about two hundred people have read it since.

"And tonight you said a true sentence about the thing itself, in a yard, in the rain, with a hundred and thirty words about a lock in it, and it is not about you at all, and it is a worse sentence, and it is going to cost you the one thing you have been refusing to say since the thirty-first day of the month, and that is that you do not know whether the other thing works, and there is not going to be a room in this city in which you are permitted to say that twice."

And Adrian Vale said, "I know," and the notary entered that, and Roke entered that two men said a total of two words to each other in about a minute and a half and that the two words were in the local language and that they were *I know*.

---

The refusal is entered. It is the fourth refusal in this city's book and the first one in five weeks in which the sentence given in the room is the true one and not the fair one, and Vail has entered that, and has entered the reason in her own hand, and the reason is: **that the three previous refusals were made in a room in which the man refusing was also the man the refusal was about, and that a refusal about yourself in front of a room is the easiest kind there is and the one this city has had three of, and that I would like it entered that this one was not easy, because the sentence in the yard took two hours to arrive and there are about forty people in this city who have told me in the last two days that they believed he would take it, and I did not believe he would take it either, and I have entered that as well.**

Ivenn Marrow went down two hundred steps at about the ninth hour of the evening of the nineteenth day of the new month and he went down them to be useful to somebody, and about nine people in this city have said since that they saw him go down and did not know what to make of it, and he stopped at the bottom of them at the parapet for about four minutes and looked at the river, and then he went into the low room by the salt wharf, where a woman of fifty-eight with tweezers was working on a man of thirty-one from Leeds, of the second list, who has a bad right hand and has not been able to make a fist since the twenty-fifth day of the month, and he carried a bench in for her, and he did not stay, and nobody asked him to.

And about two hundred steps above him, on the upper road, at about the ninth hour of the evening of the same day, a woman of sixty-one who keeps a sheet of paper said the number **nineteen**, out loud, on a road, to a man on the road.

The man was a docker of the day crew going down to the Lock of Salt for the evening tide and he did not know her and he said goodnight.

And that is in the book, in her own words, entered by her and not by anybody else, and it is the eleventh time she has said that number to anybody in nine years and the first time she has said it to a person, and neither of them knew what it meant, and she wrote in the margin of the page in the waxed case: **said it. Nobody answered. Go home.**
