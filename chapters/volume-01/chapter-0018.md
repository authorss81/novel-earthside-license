# Chapter 0018 — The Cost of the Field

It is a three-hour walk down the Reed Reach road and Adrian made it twice on the Thursday, once with Tamsin and once with a man from Silt Row who had come up to the hall on Friday and had been coming back to the hall every day since, on her own feet, at her own cost, with her own boots.

Wenna Cray was forty-one and had a face like a shut door and had lost a year of a field she had not yet finished losing, and by the third mile she had stopped being a complainant and started being the most competent person Adrian had spoken to since he arrived.

That is what happened to the argument. He had spent fourteen days carrying a debt and a seat and a queue, and the thing he had expected from this walk was that a farmer would want a number and would be angry that he could not give her one, and instead what happened was that a woman of forty-one from Silt Row took his notebook out of his hands at the second stile, looked at four pages of it, gave it back, and said, in the local language, in a voice with no performance in it whatsoever:

"You have drawn the lock. And the ditch. And nine places where the water is going. And that is very good work, Englishman, and it is about a lock, and my mother did not lose a season to a lock."

"What did she lose it to?"

"To a ditch," said Wenna Cray, "and to a gate, and to a boy called Osten who is twenty-two and who holds the second gate at Long Water and who is a better engineer than anybody in Saltwake, and whom I would have married in a normal year." She kept walking. "Come and look at the ground and then tell me what your book is for."

---

The bottom field is a hundred and forty acres and it is not ugly.

That was the first thing Adrian had to unlearn, and it took him about a minute and a half. He had been carrying a picture in his head since Thursday morning of the second tide — a brown ruin, a dead thing, the shape of a loss — and the ground in front of him on the Thursday afternoon was a low flat field of a hundred and forty acres of the best soil he had stood on in any world, drained four days ago, with the ditches cut and clean and the winter grain drilled in it in lines that went to the horizon in both directions, and a woman walking a stick along a headland.

Nothing was dead. That was the thing. He stood at the end of a headland for about four minutes waiting to feel something and felt nothing, and then he made himself walk out into it, knee-deep, in a suit, with the rain coming, and there was a boy of about nine standing in a ditch with a stick, watching him, and Adrian waved at him and the boy did not wave back and that was correct.

The shoots had gone back under. The field would be replanted in the ember month and it would come up. The ditches would hold. The soil was the same soil it had been on Monday.

"This is not a disaster," Adrian said.

"No," said Wenna Cray.

"Then what—"

"Look at the water in the ditch," she said. "Not the water. The *ditch*."

So he looked at the ditch, and the ditch was clean, and cut, and full to two thirds, and he could not see what a woman of forty-one and a man who could perceive the nearest join-line in five hundred feet of masonry could both see.

And then he got it, and it took about four seconds, and it was the single most frightening thing that happened to him in the first month.

"Nothing is wrong with the ditch," Wenna Cray said. "That is the problem. Look at Tuesday. Look at the mud on the third stake. That mud is level all the way across. In a working field, a ditch at half a stone in a wet week is a *mess*. It is uneven. It has got high places and low places and a man has to know which, and the whole of the trade is knowing which, and the whole of a *promise* is that somebody knows which and can tell you.

"That ditch is level. Somebody has taken the guess out of it. Somebody has made the water in this field do exactly what it is told, everywhere, at once, for the length of a mile and a half. And that is worth something. That is worth more than a crop, and every man in the Reach will tell you the same, and none of them will be able to say it to you in a sentence, because it is not a thing that gets *said*, it is a thing that gets *known*."

---

They walked to Silt Row and then four miles further down the Reach road to a hamlet called Corrow, and Elsa Skell came out of a gate in a coat with a stick in her hand, and she was forty-four and had been working the bottom field since she was nine, and she took one look at Adrian and said something in the local language that Tamsin did not translate.

"She says tell him before he starts," Tamsin said.

"Tell me what," Adrian said.

"That he has come to tell her the number, and there is no number, and if he opens the book in this yard I am going to send him up the road." Elsa Skell had come out into the yard and had not offered anybody a chair. "You are the man from the wall. I know what you are because my sister was in that hall. Come into the yard. Do not open anything in the yard."

Elsa Skell put her stick down against a wall.

"Now," she said. "Say what you came to say."

"I came to see what a season of this field is," Adrian said. "I owe it. It is in the book against my name. I have come to see it in the field and I have brought a notebook and I can tell you how the pressure in your ditches has moved every day since the flood came, and I have got a method, and it takes a stick and a post and two hours a day and anybody can do it—"

"Then that is not what I asked for."

Elsa Skell waited.

"I asked the meeting-ground a month ago and nobody has given me an answer and I am going to ask you now and then I am going to go and plant." She put her hand flat on the wall of her own gate. "How many measures of winter grain am I to put in?"

Adrian opened the notebook and shut it again.

"I do not know."

"Then that is the conversation, and it is four words long, and you have had it." She was not angry. That was the awful part; she had the manner of a woman doing a job she has done a hundred times with a bad supplier. "Now. Do you want to know why I cannot plan a season? You do. You want to be useful. So: I have to decide in eight days. On the last day of the salt month, or the next day, or I lose the window and the grain goes in late and the late grain is a thin crop in a year I cannot afford a thin crop. Eight days. And on the eighth day I have to put a number in a book, and the number has to be a number, and it has to be mine, and if I put in too much and it goes under, I eat my own seed and I am out next year as well as this. If I put in too little, my neighbour's gate gets a bad share and my neighbour's household cannot promise a date to the gate at Long Water, and a household in this Reach that cannot promise a date does not keep its share of the water. That is not poverty. That is worse. In this country the poor are hungry for a fortnight. The household that cannot promise is hungry for a year and is not the one anybody sends anything to."

"Who decides that?"

"Nobody. It just is. There is no court. The meeting-ground takes it, and the meeting-ground is nine men and eleven women and they have never once voted about me and they will never once vote about me, because it is not a vote, it is a *count*, and a count is a thing you do to people and a vote is a thing you do with them." She picked her stick up. "So. You have a notebook. Do you have a number for me?"

"No."

"Then you have come a long way for nothing," said Elsa Skell, "and I am sorry, and you should go and stand somewhere else and look at your ditch."

---

They were about a mile back up the Reach road when Adrian asked Tamsin to stop, and stopped, and put his hand on a fence post, and did the thing he could do.

"You can feel the line," Tamsin said, in the local language, and then in English. "Not here. There is nothing here. A fence post is not a join."

"No. But the ditch is." He got his hand flat on the earth at the ditch edge, in the wet, and closed his eyes, and there was nothing there, and then there was one thin cold thread of it about four feet away, weak and diffuse, running off down the reach toward the lock, and he could not choose to have it, and it had cost him nothing at all and it lasted about six seconds, and when it went he got a headache behind his left eye that sat there until Friday.

"Say what you saw."

"Not a join. A gradient. It is not damage, it is not a *break* anywhere, and it is not the man in the join doing the oath, because that is up at the lock and this is four miles away. It is the *effect* of that, still running, still running, fourteen days after it should have stopped when the tear closed." He wiped his face. "Elsa Skell is right that the ditch is level. And she is right that the water is being told what to do. And the water is being told, and there is no man out here at all. It is not being done. It is *habit*. Whatever has been standing in that join has been spending the reach for fourteen days and it did not stop when we shut the door, and it is not going to stop, and the level in her ditches is the *shadow*, not the working."

"And the field?"

"And the field is the first payment." Adrian said it in the flat voice and heard how it sounded and did not soften it. "Vail wrote on Thursday of the second tide that the debt is *a season* and that it is enforceable and that if I cannot pay it the record says so. And a season is a number, and the number is a thing that has been paid. And the number is not a number. It is an *open account*, and it is being written about forty times a day, and nobody in this city can see it, and I did not think of that until a minute ago on a road, and it is the single most important thing I have learned since I arrived."

"The debt is going up," said Tamsin.

"The debt is going up, and Vail wrote down that I owed a season, and in eight days on the last day of the salt month I am going to stand in front of the same two hundred people and answer three questions about a thing I have been told is a fixed amount, and the fixed amount was fixed the morning after the second tide, when it was smallest." He put his hand flat on the post again, uselessly. "So it is not a fine. If it were a fine I would spend it. It is a relationship. I have to go and be a person in a village eight days from now and *continue* to be one, and that is a different thing and I do not have a procedure for it and I have not written one down."

He looked for the pale field. There was nothing. Not in the air by the road, not in the ditch, not in the flat grey light of a wet afternoon four miles from a lock. The last time the thing had said anything at all was on the Saturday night before last, in a room two floors above a market, and since then it had said nothing in the yard on the Sunday, or the Monday, or the Tuesday, or the Wednesday, nothing on the walk-round, nothing on the walk out here, and Adrian had begun to understand that the messages came out of *places* — the yard, the hall, the seam, a walk-round in the rain, the wall of a chandlery on a wet Saturday, and the one place they had never come from was the gate itself, which was the place he most wanted one — and that it had never once followed him into a field, and that he did not know what that meant, and that he was writing it down anyway.

---

The walking back took until dark and it happened about a mile from Saltwake, and it was Wenna Cray who found it.

They came over a rise onto a flat lane above the lock where the road passes a row of allotments and a commons, and there was a woman standing in the lane with a barrow, in the rain, in the dark, and the barrow had four sacks on it, and she was about seventy, and she and Wenna Cray had a long conversation in the local language that Adrian listened to and understood about a third of.

Wenna Cray came back to him looking like a woman who has been handed something heavy.

"That is a miller," she said. "Her name is Orla Senn and she has a mill on the upper channel and she has been walking down this lane twice a day since the water came up, in the dark, before the market opens, with a barrow, and she has been leaving four sacks of flour at the second gate at Silt Row. Not for the village. For the field. She is putting it on the ditch banks. In the rain. At four in the morning."

"For the field."

"For the field," said Wenna Cray. "Because the drowned shoots have been going back under and there is nothing in that field to hold the banks, and the banks are the only thing that stops the whole flat going into the second ditch, and she has been putting flour on the banks for the fifteen days since the water came up over the ditch, and she has not told anybody, and she has told nobody because if she tells the market then the market will bring the field a whole council and ask for a gate."

---

They found three more things in two hours, on foot, in the dark, with Wenna Cray, and Adrian wrote all of them down in the notebook that evening and did not sleep.

A Hearthguard from the outer ward had started carrying salt up the Reach road twice a week, unasked, on a horse that was not his, because the water at Silt Row had gone brackish on Wednesday and the field gates needed setting. The Hearthguard had not reported it. Nobody had sent him.

The gatehouse at Long Water had taken a load of thatch out of its own roof and put it over a section of the second ditch, and Osten, the twenty-two-year-old who holds the gate, had done it on his own time on the Thursday, and had told his father that he had done it, and his father had told Wenna Cray by Friday.

And on the Friday morning, at six, Owen Park had gone down to the bottom field on his own, with no tools and no purpose, and had stood at the end of a ditch for an hour with his hands in his pockets while the rain came off the lock, and had come back and eaten breakfast and said nothing about it, and had gone back on the Saturday and on the Sunday and on the Monday, and had told nobody, and had been seen.

"That one is yours," said Tamsin.

"No," Adrian said.

"He is one of yours."

"That is exactly what I mean," Adrian said. "He did it on his own. He did it four times. He did not tell us, he did not ask, he did not put it in Priya's log, and he did not ask me for anything, and he is the one person in that loft who has a full pair of hands and nothing to do with them and no workway, and I did not assign that. I nearly did. I want you to know I nearly did."

"What would you have done?"

"Made it a rota," Adrian said. "I would have made it a rota. Rota, Wednesday, the field, two hours, bring a barrow. Which is a *completely* reasonable thing to do and is what a man like me does, and if we had done that then in four weeks there would be a sheet on the wall in the loft with a list of names on it and a column for hours, and Orla Senn would have stopped, because Orla Senn is doing it in the dark on purpose and a rota in the dark is a rota with witnesses in it and witnesses are what kills it."

"Mm."

"That is the whole of what I have learned in seventeen days," Adrian said, "and it is the opposite of what I came here to do, and I would like it written down in a place where I cannot lose it, and I would like somebody to be a witness, and I am not going to ask, and Tamsin, you have been doing this since I got here and you are the only person in this world who has ever once written down a prediction made for a reason and then made me live inside it, and if I ever get the chance to take a chair, the first thing I do with the chair is put a column in it that nobody can check, and the first person I put in it is you."

"You can put me in it now," said Tamsin, in English, in the dark, on a road above a lock. "I have been in it since that Thursday morning. Go to bed. The salt month ends in eight days and there is a woman in Corrow who is going to decide on that day how much of her life to put into the ground, and in eight days you are going to stand in front of two hundred people and answer three questions about a number that has been growing every hour since the morning after the second tide, and the only reason you will be able to stand there is that about eleven people in this city, four of them from the loft and the other seven I could not name if you asked me, and several of whom will never find out what you said about them, are walking out onto a flat field in the dark and putting sacks of flour on the banks of a ditch that nobody ordered them to."
