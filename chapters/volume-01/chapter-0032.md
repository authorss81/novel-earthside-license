# Chapter 0032 — The Offer in the Yard

There is a tallow store on the upper road above the high-water line and it is the only warm dry building in Saltwake that a person can rent the whole of, and the man who keeps it has wanted to let the upper floor for a year, and Jonah Pike had been in it twice before anybody else in this city knew he was in the world.

---

It came out in the yard on the Sunday morning, at about the sixth hour, in the rain, in front of about a hundred and forty people, and it came out because Jonah Pike had spent a night writing it and because the yard is the only room in this city where a thing gets said in public by whoever is nearest, and on the Sunday morning the nearest was a man from another world who had put the true reason for refusing a workway on a keeper's door two days before and had slept about two hours of the night between.

The nearest had, at about the fifth hour, carried four buckets of water up two flights of stairs to the disused Salt Weavers' chapel for eleven people who came out of a floor nine days ago, one bucket at a time, in his right hand, up a stair with no rail, and had done it in about nineteen minutes, and one person saw him do it and did not know what she was looking at, which is a girl of sixteen called Kirsty Lund who has been in this world eight days and who wrote it down that afternoon in a school exercise book with the entry *he carried four buckets up and did not put them down and did not say anything about it and his face did not do anything*, and Mina Okoye read that entry on the Sunday evening and did not show it to anybody for four days.

The binding is said twice a day and nobody has to hear it. That is the whole invention and it took thirty years and four women and it is the reason about a hundred and forty people were standing in a yard at six in the morning on a Sunday in a flood month, and it is the reason Jonah Pike chose it, which Adrian worked out about four minutes into the thing and wrote down afterwards and underlined, because Jonah Pike choosing a room is the most frightening information Adrian received that week.

"Forty of us said the words at six," Jonah said, in English, to the six, at volume, so that the yard could hear the shape of it even where it could not follow. "And then I am going to say a thing, and I am going to say it in here rather than in a room, because I have been in a room three times this month and every time I have been in a room I have been the only man in it, and this is not that. Owen, give me your hand. No — give me the other one. Thank you."

He had a sheet of paper in local language, in his own hand, badly, with the words spaced out like a man who has never once written a document in another language and has decided that is not an excuse.

---

> **A PROPOSAL, read in the yard under the lock chapel, on the ninth day of the new month, in the rain, in front of about a hundred and forty persons, by Jonah Pike, of the six, and not entered in the book of the Lantern Concord because he has not asked to have it entered and the notary was not present.**
>
> **One. A building. The upper floor of the tallow store on the upper road, three rooms and a loft, dry, with a stove, above the high-water line. It is held for six weeks at no rent, on the word of Jonah Pike, and after six weeks it is to be discussed.**
>
> **Two. Work. Every person in this proposal who wants a job has one, on the first day they ask for it, and there are eleven people in this city from another world who have asked for nothing and are owed four things by this city, and two of those four things are a place to lie down and dry clothing, and this proposal answers both of them by Friday.**
>
> **Three. Equipment. Shared, held in common, and anybody in the rooms may use it.**
>
> **Four. Work rights at the lock. Every person in these rooms who can work will be on the lock's books, at the ordinary rate, on the ordinary rotation, and will be paid the ordinary wage, and will be owed nothing and will owe nothing.**
>
> **Five. And in exchange: the standing of this arrangement at the Lock of Salt is exclusive, and no other arrangement of any kind with any person from another world is made at this lock, and the arrangement is made with the standing of the Lantern Concord of Saltwake, which has a seat in this matter.**

It took about four minutes to read because Jonah read it slowly and badly and did not stop once to explain anything.

Aldis Kerrow, who had a very good coat and a narrow mouth and had been standing at the edge of the yard since the fifth hour because that is where he stands, said one sentence.

"The Concord has no seat in this matter."

"Read that into it," Jonah said, in English, to Adrian, and did not look at Kerrow again.

"Mr Pike," said Kerrow, in the local language, and his voice did not rise, and it carried anyway, because it had carried across a hall of four hundred on the thirty-first day of the flood. "The Lantern Concord has no seat in this matter, has not been asked, and will not be given one by a document read in a yard on a Sunday morning by a person who has not entered it. I am the assessor of strangers for this city and I am telling you in this yard, and not afterwards, because afterwards you would put it in a market as a thing that was never in the draft."

"I will put it in the book on Monday," Jonah said, "as a thing that was in the draft, and the book will say it was struck."

"Then it will say it was struck, and about a hundred and forty people in this yard will remember the reading and not the striking, and you know that, and I am going to go and stand by the arch, and you may all carry on without me."

He went and stood by the arch.

---

About a hundred and ten of the hundred and forty stayed. That is entered in the roll as a number because Aldis Kerrow entered it before he left and entered it in the book afterward, and the number is one of the more useful numbers in the book, because it means the number who heard the whole thing is a hundred and ten and not a hundred and forty, and the number who left is thirty, and nobody has ever found out who the thirty were and Vail entered that too.

Of the thirty who left, eleven were from the Reed Reach, having come up the road on the Sunday because there was a market on the Sunday, and they left at item five, in a body, without speaking, and Tamsin Quill watched them go and did not go after them and has said since that that was the correct thing to do and that she was not the person to do it.

And what happened after item five is the part that ended the thirty-second day of this flood and cost a man his life nine days later, and Adrian has written it up in nine pages and the nine pages are the worst thing in the notebook.

Jonah Pike is right about nine parts in ten of almost everything. He has been right about nine parts in ten of almost everything since the sixth day of this flood, and the tenth part is always the same part, and the tenth part is always a local woman's word for a social fact, and Renna Ord's word is *order* and Dante Kuo's is *cheap*, and the word for what happened in that yard at about the sixth hour on the ninth day of the new month is neither of theirs.

It is *dry*.

"It is the only proposal on the table," Jonah said, at the seventh hour, to about sixty people who had not gone, "that puts eleven frightened people somewhere dry tonight with a job in the morning. I have been in this city thirty-four days. I have watched a woman carry a bucket of water up two hundred steps for a boy of eleven who was not hurt, and I have watched four people from another world sleep on a floor in a disused chapel with the plaster still on the north wall because a woman gave them a key, and I have watched the six of us not know what we are doing tomorrow. And a man in a good coat has been more useful in this city than any of us and he has a Crown seal on a letter that says a position may be filled by a person who can hold it, and I have read it, and I am not going to pretend I have not, and it is a Crown document and it is four days old and it is the first document anybody from another world has been handed by a government."

"Jonah," Adrian said.

"No. Let me finish, and then you can say whatever it is you have been not saying since the fourth hour, because I have watched you not say it since the fourth hour and I have decided that I would rather be argued with than waited on." Jonah folded the paper. "I am not asking for a door. I am asking for a floor and a wage and a stove. And I am telling this yard that I have a man in a good coat who has told a hundred and forty people in a market in the last two days that the floor is available and the wage is available, and that is *true*, and that it is the only true thing anybody has offered in a month, and that I am not going to let a man with a season of somebody's field in his name be the only man in this city who says no to a dry room."

And the yard made a noise, and about nine people in it looked at Adrian, and about thirty looked at Jonah, and he noticed both numbers, and he wrote them down that night, and the number thirty is the number that the rest of this is made of.

---

He could not say it.

That is the finding and it is in the notebook in four places and in the book in one, and the notary entered it herself, on the Monday, unasked, in a flat clerk's voice, and read it back, and the reading is one of the four sentences Adrian has asked to be read back to him in a year by a person who does not like him.

It is this:

> **That the holder of the pull did not answer an arrangement brought to a yard of a hundred and forty persons on the ninth day of the new month, and did not refuse it, and did not say that he was going to think about it, and said nothing at all for about an hour and forty minutes, and that a silence entered in a yard is not a refusal and is not a consent, and is a third thing, and this city does not have a word for the third thing and has had four hundred years and could not be bothered.**

He tried twice.

The first time was at about half past six, to Jonah, in a doorway, in English, with the rain going sideways, and it took him about eleven seconds.

"Jonah. The seventh clause is not true and the fifth one is not available. The Concord has no seat. And work rights at the lock are not yours to sell, and the instrument in the book is continued and may not be used, and everything you have just promised those people is a promise made by a man who does not hold the thing he is promising it with, and I have been the man in the book for thirty-four days and the first thing that seat taught me is that a promise about a place is made by the people who maintain it, and you have just read out in a yard the words *the standing of the Lantern Concord of Saltwake, which has a seat in this matter*, and it does not, and Kerrow said so in front of a hundred and forty people, and I—"

"And what," said Jonah Pike, in English, in the rain, in a doorway, without any heat in it at all, which was the problem. "What is the sentence, Adrian. Say the sentence. You have had it since the fourth hour and you have not said it and I have watched you not say it four times this afternoon. **Say the sentence.**"

And Adrian Vale stood in a doorway in the rain in a town of eleven hundred people in a flood month and did not say the sentence, and it took him about four seconds to understand that he was not going to, and it took him another eleven to understand why, and the why is the whole of the rest of this book.

It is not that he did not have it. He has had it for nine days and he wrote a version of it on a sheet of flour-merchant's paper at about four in the morning on the Monday and read it over nine times. It is that the sentence is *no thank you, and here is a better thing, and I will put my name on the better thing*, and the better thing is the **statement of error** — a procedure four days old, in a book, in which any person in this city may enter a statement against any entry, in their own words, without a reason and without a cost.

And the sentence he could not say is this:

*Jonah, if that warehouse is offered to eleven frightened people in exchange for the labour of a lock, then in nine months it will be offered to a Crown clerk in exchange for a gate, and the Crown clerk will be better at it than you are, and he will be kind, and he will have a seal, and I have read the letter.*

It is a good sentence. It is the best thing Adrian has said in a month. It is also, and he knew this standing in a wet doorway, **a sentence a man in a good coat would have written about a man in a good coat**, and it puts Jonah Pike on the same shelf as Anwen Kroon, Registrar of Crossings and Tides, and Jonah Pike is a forty-two-year-old warehouse supervisor from Sheffield who came out of a hole in a wall without asking and who has withdrawn three arguments in public this month on his own initiative, and who has a sheet of paper in a good coat's handwriting and a debt of a different kind that is not in a book.

And so Adrian did not say it, and the reason he did not say it, which he wrote down at four in the morning on the Monday in the flat hand, is this:

> *I did not say the sentence to Jonah Pike because it was unfair to him, and because unfair is a thing I would only have said to be right in a room, and I was not going to be right in a room on a Sunday, and I have been right in a room twice in thirty-four days and both times I was doing it to somebody who had not asked.*
>
> *And because I did not want to be the man who killed it. That is the whole of it. I have been the man in the book for thirty-four days and every single thing that has come to a yard has come to the yard to be killed by me, and there is a woman with a bowl in this city who told me in the second week that a rule about people made by a man who had not asked was the worst rule on a wall, and I have not broken that rule in thirty-four days, and standing in a doorway on a Sunday afternoon and saying the sentence would have broken it, and I would have broken it because I was tired, and because a warehouse is dry.*

Renna Ord came and found him in the doorway at about half past seven, which she did not do on purpose, and said, "He has gone up to the tallow store with two of them and the keeper, and the keeper is showing him the upper floor, and there are four people up there at this hour who are not from here, and two of them are of the eleven, and I want to tell you what I have noticed and then I want to go to bed."

"Tell me."

"You have been in three rooms and two doorways today and you have not said one word out loud in any of them," said Renna Ord, "and in thirty-four days you have said more words out loud in worse rooms than any man I have ever worked with. And there is a difference between a man who is thinking and a man who has run out, and I do not think you have run out, and I have watched men run out."

"Then what."

"You have run out of people you are allowed to be rude to." She shouldered the barrow. "That is the one. And it is the worst place to run out of and it is the only one I have never seen anybody get out of."

---

The Monday was the day the Registry's letter was answered, and it was answered locally, and it took four hours in a converted salt store and the answer is in the book and is nine lines long and has caused a certain amount of difficulty in a building in Aurel that will not be visible to anybody in this city for about a month.

Sera Vail put it in the way she puts everything in, which is that she read somebody else's draft first and then made it worse.

Kerrow had drafted the answer. It was nine lines, it was courteous, and it said that the Lantern Concord acknowledges the notice of the Registrar of Crossings and Tides and will consider the alternatives set out in it, and Vail read it out and then put the slate down.

Adrian said, in the local language, to about forty people: "Because there is no chair in this city for the man they are describing. A chair is what they want. A chair is a thing with a back to it."

"It is not a chair," said Tamsin Quill, in English, in the middle of a hall, without waiting to be asked, which she has done four times in a month. "It is a **seat**. A chair is what a person sits in. A seat is a place to be angry at, and it has to be legible to a person who does not like you, and that is Nell Bray's sentence from the second week of this flood and it is in the book and you have been in a hall two hundred times without it occurring to you to say the other word, and you have said *chair* three times in ten days and I have let two of them go."

"I am not talking about a chair in a room."

"You are talking about exactly that, and you are talking about it in a room, and a Crown clerk in Aurel is going to read a word and a word is a thing a clerk can hold up." She did not soften it, which is her standing practice and which she has never once done for him, and Adrian has written down that it is the reason he is not a man who says the true reason in a room. "Say *seat*."


"That is a letter that costs nothing and says nothing and buys eleven weeks, and it is the best draft in this city and I am not entering it."

"Serena—"

"**It is Sera Vail.**" She did not look up and she did not soften it. "You have said it in a room, in front of forty people, on the day you could least afford to, and you have said it to me. In nine months somebody in a market is going to be talking about the name of the woman who holds the page nobody is allowed to see, and it is going to be the one you invented, and it is going to be in a book that goes to Aurel in a wooden case. Write *Sera* and go on."

"Enter this instead, and read it in the yard afterwards and not in here, because in here there are forty people and in a yard there are two hundred." She wrote for about four minutes, which is a long time for her. "Enter: that on the thirty-first day of the flood this Concord answered, at a sitting of four hundred and ten persons, the question whether a first license may exist in Saltwake, and the answer was no; and that the answer was no, and that it is a public answer, and that it was given before the notice from Aurel was read and was not given in answer to it, and both of those facts are to be in the same entry and neither of them is above the other, in the form the book has used since the thirty-first day and about which this city has already had one argument it did not need."

"And then?"

"And then: that this Concord will state, in its own words and not in the form required by the notice, and sealed, and on a date named in advance, **that no crossing will be made from the Lock of Salt by this instrument, by this Concord, or with the standing of this Concord.**" She looked up. "Say it back to me and tell me what is missing."

"That the form the Registry requires says *permanently*, and says *by any person, in any form, by any person*, and says that any person who makes one is answerable to that office, and this is a different sentence in three places, and it is weaker in the first and much stronger in the second and much weaker in the third, and there is no version of it that is both honest and acceptable to a clerk in Aurel, and the reason there is no version is that the honest version is *we are not going to promise that nobody ever will*, because a city that promises that is a city that has taken a thing out of the hands of the next four hundred people who live here."

"Enter that," said Sera Vail, "in those words, and enter that I wrote it and not the assessor of strangers, and enter that the holder of the pull has read it and has not spoken, and enter that he is in the room, and enter the hour."

Adrian was in the room and had not spoken for about two and a half hours, and Vail entered it, and it is the second of the four sentences he has asked to be read back to him in a year by a person who does not like him:

> **That the holder of the pull was in the room at the tenth hour of the morning of the tenth day of the new month, when the answer to the notice of the Registrar of Crossings and Tides was entered, and read out, and sealed with the seal of this Concord; and that he was asked whether he had anything to say and said that he had one sentence and that he was not going to say it, and that he was entered as present and silent, and that this is entered as a fact and not as a fault, and that the reason is entered and the reason is in his own hand and is not this book.**

*Ask the people who maintain the gate. Ask the keeper. And ask the woman who keeps a count, and put her answer in the book in her own words and do not summarise it, because she will not come back and say it a second time.*

*And on Friday, in a yard, in front of two hundred people, about the man from the wall who would not say why.*

---

Ottavia Reek came up the market on the Monday at about the fourth hour of the afternoon with a document in a leather tube, and she did not come to Adrian.

That is the thing to understand about Monday afternoon and it took Adrian until the Tuesday to understand it and about a week to understand it properly, which is that she came to find Jonah Pike, and Jonah Pike was on the upper road, and Jonah Pike had been the only man in a yard for about thirty-six hours.

She is second of the Brass Measure. She is not a villain; she is a factor with a quarter and she has a father-in-law in the guild and she has a warehouse on the low ground that took water in the second week and a bill for it, and she has spent eight days being extremely useful to a city that has not bought anything, and she has not once been unkind to anybody from another world, and the tarpaulins and the stretcher that went into a yard on the twenty-fifth day came out of her own yard without being asked for.

"Mr Pike," she said, in the local language, and she said it to him and not to Adrian, and about a hundred and forty people were in that yard by then because it was Monday and the market shuts at five. "You have read a paper in a yard. Here is the paper."

She put the tube on the trestle and took out four sheets, and there are four sheets of it in the Concord's book with the hour on them, and Adrian has read all four, and they are the reason a docker in a yard said a sentence on the Monday afternoon that has been entered eleven times since by four different people.

The five terms Jonah had read on the Sunday were the five terms Reek had offered on the sixth day of the flood and had been refused in one sentence in a yard. That is not a coincidence and Jonah Pike did not conceal it.

> **The Brass Measure: first. The same five terms, with the addition, in the second term, of the words *and of any person from another world who is housed under this arrangement*.**
>
> **Second. That the persons so housed shall be entered on the books of the Measure for the work of the lock, and shall be paid the ordinary wage, and shall be entered by name, and their names shall be on a list kept by the Measure.**
>
> **Third. That the standing of the Lantern Concord of Saltwake attaches to this arrangement, and that the Concord's seal is required, and that the Concord's seal has been given.**
>
> **Fourth. That the dockers' labour at the Lock of Salt be drawn from the Measure's own list, and that the Measure's list be the list of record for all labour at that lock, from the day of this instrument forward; and that the men so drawn be paid the ordinary wage and shall not be members of any crew; and that this is entered as a standing order and not as an agreement, and does not require the consent of any man so drawn.**
>
> **Fifth. And that any person from another world who is not housed under this arrangement may not be entered on the books of the lock, may not be given work at the lock, and may not be counted among the hands of the lock in any return entered in any book; and that the entry of such a person in the roll of strangers shall be made by the Measure and not by the Concord.**

It is the fourth term. It is always going to be the fourth term, and Adrian has written that down four times in four days in a notebook and underlined it, because a thing a man reads out in a yard does not live in his head, it lives on page four of a leather tube, and page four of a leather tube can be carried to Aurel in a case, and the Crown's second letter says that a position may be filled by a person who can hold it.

Renna Ord read it out herself.

She did not ask. She was in the yard and she has been in this city for fifty-one years and she cannot read, and she took the four sheets off the trestle and stood in front of about a hundred and forty people and had Peth Lomas read the fourth term aloud, in the local language, in a flat voice with a bar in his hand, and then she said the sentence, and it is nine words and it is in the book in her own hand and it has been read back by a notary four times since:

"**That is a lock that a guild can close.**"

And then, because about forty people in that yard did not understand what she had said and had already begun to say the other thing, she said the other half, which is the half that matters, and she said it to Adrian in front of a hundred and forty people because she wanted it in front of a hundred and forty people:

"And a lock that a guild can close is a lock a man can be angry at. So say it again, all of you, in a month, when you have had time to think about it, and not because I told you to this afternoon."

And Jonah Pike stood in that yard and did not contradict her, and did not agree with her, and about forty people looked at him, and about thirty looked at Adrian, and the count is in the book because Renna Ord told a notary it and the notary wrote it down, and she said it out loud first, in the yard, twice, so that nobody could say later that she had said it quietly.

---

He did not answer him that evening either, and the question Jonah Pike asked him in front of people on the Monday at about the seventh hour, in a market, with a tallow store lit up behind him and about sixty people within earshot, was this.

"Adrian. On the thirtieth you said out loud in a hall that you are staying Unbound, and you said why, and it went into a book and into a roll that goes to Aurel in a wooden case, and about four hundred people heard it." Jonah had his hands in his pockets and he was not enjoying it, which Adrian could tell and which did not help. "I have never once in thirty-four days heard you say what you are *for*. Not what you are against. Everybody here knows what you are against because you have been against something in public every day since the ninth day, and there are eleven people in that building who are owed four things by this city and two of the four are a place to lie down and dry clothing, and it is Monday night and the stove in the upper floor of a tallow store is lit, and I have offered them a wage."

"And I have no answer to that," Adrian said, in the local language, badly, and Tamsin did not correct him and did not look at him, which is how he knew she was in the room.

"Say that in English," said Jonah Pike, in a market, to sixty people, "and then say it again tomorrow when you have had all night, because tomorrow I am going to read the fourth term of that paper out loud in that yard again and a docker is going to stand there and say that it is a lock a guild can close, and it will be right, and then about two hundred people in this city are going to be waiting to hear what the man in the book is for."

He walked away up the road. He did it without any theatre at all, which is how Adrian knew he had lost an argument, because Jonah Pike had wanted to win it and had decided to be seen not trying.

And Adrian Vale stood in a market in the rain on the tenth day of the new month and was asked, in front of sixty people, what he was for, and had not one sentence, and did not answer, and wrote it up at two in the morning on the Tuesday in a room above a market with four and a half hours of sleep behind him, and the entry is the shortest thing in that notebook and it is nine words and it is the whole of the next three days:

*Ask them what happens to the eleven on Friday. Then decide. Then stop.*
