# Chapter 0002 — The Wrong Kind of Loading

He woke because his hand hurt, and for a disorienting second he thought it was a paper cut from a printout.

Then the cold came up through the stone under him and made the paper cut into a fact.

He was lying on his side on a stone ledge about four feet wide, in water that was up to the level of his ribs and rising. The sky above the chamber was a dirty green, the color of a river seen through a bottle, and the light in it was the light of a day that has already been going for a while. Dawn, maybe. He had no idea. He had a suit on and no shoes and his right hand was bleeding into the water in a thin line that kept being diluted and coming back, which meant the bleeding had not stopped, which meant it was worse than he wanted.

He got himself up onto one elbow, which took longer than it should have.

The place was a chamber cut out of a river. A big one — a barrel vault of dark dressed stone with a floor of steps going down at one end and a sluice of black water at the other, and above him, at the top of a set of stairs he could not see the top of, a ceiling of heavy beams with light coming between them. There was timber everywhere: baulks, braces, a great wheel-shaped thing on its side, hemp rope the colour of wet rope ought to be. It smelled of iron, of tar, of cold stone, and of fish, and under all of it the flat mineral smell of a lot of water that had recently gone somewhere it did not belong.

Somewhere above and behind him, water was running.

Not the water around his ribs. That was almost still, moving with a slow patient push like something thinking. The other sound was coming down through a hole in the wall, and it was the loudest thing in the world, and it was *falling*.

He turned his head, and there was a light on the wall.

It was not a lamp. It was the same pale grey as the text that had been on his screen, the exact same pale grey, and it ran in a thin irregular seam through the wet black stone of the lock gate at the level of the water, about as long as a car, and it was *bright* in a way that hurt — not like a bulb, like a wire seen through a gap in a fence. Where the light was, the water was doing nothing. Where the light was not, the water was streaming past his ribs.

He looked at it directly.

For a moment the light was unbearable and everything else went out of it, and then the light was only light again, and it stayed exactly where it was, and it gave him nothing. No direction. No sense of *toward*. No arrow, no prompt, no little box asking him to choose. It was light in the way a scar is a colour: a fact about the stone, not an instruction to him. He tried, deliberately, to feel which way it ran, and all he got was a bad taste in his mouth and a sharper awareness of how cold he was, and a woman on a stair shouting at him.

Somebody shouted.

He spun, and there were three people on the steps above him, and they had stopped coming down.

The nearest was a woman with her hair pinned up in a knot that had lost the argument some hours ago, in a soaked coat that had been good once. She had a boat hook in both hands, held low and forward, the way you hold a hook when you are not fishing. Behind her, a young man — nineteen, twenty — with a cooper's adze in one hand and his weight already on the back foot of a stair that had gone slick. Behind *her*, an older woman in a felt hat with a lamp hooked to her wrist, who had stopped to light the lamp properly, because the lamp had gone out.

They were looking at him. Not at the wall. At him.

The young man said something. It was three syllables and it rose at the end, and it was not a question.

The woman said something longer and much more level, and she did not raise the hook.

He understood none of it. Not a word, not a grammatical shape. He had heard languages he did not know before in films and had never had the experience of a whole sentence of one arriving in his head and staying there with no room in it for any other meaning. He had, in fact, a small stupid hope that it was Latin, that it was French, that this was some kind of organised event or a very elaborate performance, and he gave that hope up about halfway through the woman's second sentence, because her face was afraid, and afraid people do not use words for show.

He put his hands where she could see them. Open. Above the water, palms out, the left one first because the right one was doing something interesting with blood. He got his shoulders down and made himself small and he said, slowly, in the flattest voice he had, "I don't understand you. I'm not — I don't belong here. I'm not with anyone."

He had wanted to say *please*. He was aware, distantly, that it was the correct word. He did not say it, because the woman had a hook and he had decided, somewhere in the last four minutes, not to be the thing that made her use it.

The three of them looked at each other. The woman in the felt hat made a noise that was almost a laugh and did not have any humour in it. The young man on the stairs said the three syllables again, and this time the woman hit him in the arm without looking at him, and the young man stopped talking, and the whole exchange told Adrian more than he would have believed: the three-syllable word was a *name* for him. A bad one. Somebody's joke, probably, the way a nickname is a joke until the nickname outlives the man who made it.

Then the young man looked past him, at the wall, and his face changed.

Adrian followed his eyes.

The water at the level of the seam had begun to climb the stone. It was not pouring; it was *leaning*, a thin sheet of it standing on the wall face at an angle no water stands at, and it was climbing toward the seam. The seam's pale light was spreading. It had been the length of a car. It was now longer than the chamber.

A voice, very close, saying nothing. The hair on his forearms lifted. There was no sound. There was a *placement*, the sense of a mouth inches from his left ear on a day with no wind, and the sense was so specific that he turned around and there was nothing there but three feet of wet stone and a coil of rope.

Then the pale field came up, in the same grey, small, in the middle of the air in front of him like a page held at reading distance:

> You may ask one person to stand with you.
> A witness is not a servant.
> Ask them what it will cost them, or do not ask.

It sat there for four seconds and was gone.

It did not tell him how to get out. It did not tell him who had arrived, or why, or when it would stop. It told him one fact about a thing he did not yet understand, and then it left him with a wave coming in and a man on a stair and no idea at all what the cost was.

He looked up at the water that was climbing the wall. The dark under the seam was not a room. He had been hoping, for the length of the flight and the drop and the waking, that when he got here he would find a door — a door has a hinge, a door has a step, a door has somebody on the other side who put it there. What was under the light was a *depth*. It went back and down and it did not end anywhere he could see, and there was no draught coming out of it, and no light coming off the far wall, and when he looked into it for three full seconds absolutely nothing happened, which he had not expected and did not know what to do with. He turned his head away and his stomach came up into his throat, and he was sick over the edge into the water, and it made no difference to the water at all.

It was a doorway in the sense that a hole in a bucket is a doorway. It did not have a building attached to it.

So he stopped looking at it. That was the first genuinely useful decision he made in this world and he made it because he was, underneath everything else, a man who had spent six years building things that only worked if the front door was load-bearing.

The young man on the stairs had gone back to coming down, carefully, one step at a time, testing each with the toe of his boot before he put his weight on it. The stone under the water was polished by a century of boots and by nothing else, and where the pale light fell on it the polish looked wrong. It looked like the shine on wet glass.

"Ovin," the woman said. "Bray, hold the line. Don't get near the light."

"Ovin" — the young man — stopped with his foot on the next step. He was maybe two steps from Adrian. The water was at his knee. He was looking down at the stone and then up at the woman, and Adrian could read the arithmetic on his face because it was the same arithmetic Adrian had been doing for the last four minutes. The man in the water was a stranger. The light on the wall was very far away. Ovin had been sent down the steps, and a boat hook, and the whole weight of the night had been the argument about whether a stranger in the water was something you needed to know about at close range.

Ovin came down one more step.

The stone went.

There was no crack. There was no sound at all. The step simply stopped being a step, and Ovin's boot went into a hole that had not been there a second before, and the hole was where the seam was, and he went down to about his waist with his arms flung out and the adze spinning away from him and his mouth open, and the water closed over his shoulders.

The woman swore. The woman in the felt hat was already on his way down, too slow, four steps behind.

Adrian had a length of line in his hand. He did not remember picking it up. It was tarred hemp, thicker than his wrist, and it came out of the coil on the wall and he had a fistful of it, and it was anchored to nothing he could see, which meant it was anchored to whatever the coil was bolted to, which was a mooring post, which was four feet behind him, which meant that if he went over he would take the post with him.

He also had, in his other hand, an iron tool. A spike — a heavy square-shanked spike the length of his forearm, tar-black, with a loop of rope still rotted round its head, apparently fallen out of the water and come to rest against the step. He had taken it for something to stand on. He was holding it in his fist the way you hold a hammer when you have not decided what you are doing.

He made the decision.

He got his weight over the line, hard, until it came tight and took the sting out of his back, and he leaned out and put the spike down into the floor at Ovin's shoulder — not onto Ovin, into the stone beside him, driven by the weight of his arm and nothing else, a bad piece of work that would have embarrassed him in a shop and worked anyway because the stone was soft and wet. And he got the loop of the spike's head under the boy's armpit, and he hauled.

Ovin came up out of the water like a fish out of a bucket, one arm hooked on the spike and both hands scrabbling at the step, and Adrian got a fistful of the back of his coat and pulled him onto stone. The boy was vomiting river water and swearing in the same language as the three syllables, and Adrian held him with an arm across the back of the neck the way you hold a retching child, and thought, in the middle of all of it: *he is going to be a hundred pounds heavier and I am going to lose my grip.*

The woman's boat hook came down and took Ovin's coat at the shoulder and hauled him the last two feet onto the step above, and the woman in the felt hat got there and got an arm under the boy, and for a moment Adrian was alone in the water again with the line burning his hands.

He let go of the line and sat down hard on the ledge, because his legs had started making arguments and his left wrist had gone to a fine bright line of pain that ran from the heel of his hand to his elbow, and the wrist was the one he had landed on.

His right hand had been bleeding the whole time. He looked at it. The pale mark on the heel of his palm was clean and dry against all that water, as if it had decided to be a different kind of thing.

Above him, the water stopped climbing the wall.

Not gradually. It let go all at once, the sheet thinning and sliding back down the stone in one long peel, and the pale line in the gate narrowed back to about the length it had been when he woke up. The running sound above got quieter. The chamber's water began, slowly, to fall — an inch, maybe — as though the room had been holding a breath and had changed its mind.

Ovin lay on the step and coughed, and then, in his own language, said something to the woman that was clearly *sorry*, and the woman said four words back that were clearly not about forgiveness, and she turned her head and looked at Adrian for the first time since she had stopped him from killing herself with a boat hook.

She did not lower the hook. She said something long and slow, with three words in the middle of it that sounded like a name, and then she stopped and waited, and Adrian understood with total clarity that she had just asked him a question, and that the entire result of his next twenty seconds would depend on his answer.

He had no answer. He had no language, no currency, no proof, and no way of explaining that the man she was talking to had come out of a wall by accident and did not know the first thing about tides or hooks or who was allowed to be on her steps at dawn.

So he did the only generous thing available. He unclipped the copper token that had been sitting loose on the wall beside him, a thick stamped disc the size of a playing card with a number punched into it and a smear of green where the wall had held it for a century, and he held it up where she could see it, and he let go of it so that it sank slowly in front of her instead of dropping it.

Then he put his bleeding hand flat, palm down, on the stone in front of him, and left it there, and waited to be told what he was.

The mark on his palm did not go anywhere. It was warm, and the water was cold, and there was a boy on the step above him being sick into the river, and Adrian Vale sat in a barrel vault under a lock he could not name and understood, with a completeness that was almost restful, that he had not survived anything yet. He had only stopped the next thing from happening, and the next thing was going to be much worse than a boy in a hole, and every single one of the people on those steps had already paid for it.

Then a voice from higher up the stair said a word, and everyone on the steps turned, and Adrian looked up.

There was a woman standing where the light from between the ceiling beams was thickest, so that she was lit from behind and he could not see her face. She was not in a boat-coat. She had a short cloak, a walking staff with a hooked head, and a leather satchel worn soft at the strap. She came down the steps without hurrying, which he later understood had been the single most frightening thing anyone did to him that morning, because everybody else on those steps was afraid and she was not, and she put herself between him and the three people with hooks and stood there looking down at his hand on the stone.

She crouched, took his wrist — the bad one, without asking, which he let her do because there was no version of the morning in which he had the strength to refuse anything — turned his palm up into what light there was, and looked at the mark.

Her fingers stopped. Just for a moment. Then she let go of his hand, stood, and said something to the three on the steps that made all of them look at him again and then, deliberately, away.

Then she said a sentence to him, in the same language as the three syllables, and this time he was not the only one who heard it, and it was short, and it ended in a question.

Behind her, the pale line in the wet black stone stayed exactly the length it was.
