# Chapter 0029 — The Review

The review was set for the last day of the salt month and it was held five days later, and both dates are in the book, and Adrian Vale has come to believe that the five days between them are the most useful thing that happened to him in a month.

It was held at the first light of the thirtieth day of the flood in a converted salt store with about three hundred and ten people in it, which is forty more than the first sitting and about seventy more than the first night anybody came, and the roll of public business had nine sheets on it, and one of the nine was the reason the review had been moved, which Vail read out before she read the questions, in a flat clerk's voice, with the hour on it.

"The field review of the bottom field, entered on the first day of the salt month, set for the twenty-fifth day at the first light, not held, and continued to this day, with the three questions unaltered. And the reason, entered as it happened, and read back: at the eleventh hour and eleven minutes of the twenty-fifth day a ground opened in the yard of the lock chapel, nineteen persons were carried out of it, eleven other persons came out of it, and a man of this city has a shoulder he will be asked to hold again for the rest of his life. The review was not taken because there was no room left in which to take it."

She let that sit for about four seconds, which is four seconds longer than a notary is supposed to let anything sit.

"And I am going to say the other half, because a notary who reads only the reason has written a book about a bad day. **I entered the continuation on the twenty-seventh, before I knew what the arm was going to be, and I moved it because the hall was going to be full of people who had come to be told a number about a field, and they were not going to get one, and I would rather tell them that in a yard in the rain at two o'clock in the afternoon than have them stand in this room for four hours and find out at the end.** And a man asked me at about half past three on the twenty-seventh why I had not simply moved the date, and I said because a date that moves is a date that a person can learn to depend on, and a date that moves five days is a date that can move nine."

---

She read the questions. She read them the way she had read them in a corridor on the twentieth day of the month, and Adrian had heard them once before and had had five days with them and had not got any better.

> **One. *What did the field lose, in measures, and by what work.** Not how sad. In measures.*
>
> **Two. *Who is answerable for it, by name, and what does that answerability cost that person in a month.** Not a general answer. A name and a month.*
>
> **Three. *What happens to the households that have already paid, and how will they know that they have been paid.***

Three hundred and ten people in a salt store at the first light of a Wednesday, and about forty of them could follow the English and Tamsin was reading both versions because she always reads both versions, and about nine of the eleven from another world were at the back on the same two benches they had sat on for three days and had not been moved to the front, because somebody in that hall had understood in about a second that putting them at the front would be a decision and leaving them at the back was also a decision and only one of the two was a good one.

Elsa Skell was in the second row.

She had walked eleven miles. She had not spoken to Adrian since the eighteenth day of the month, when she had told him to his face in a gateway on the Reach road that a man who owes a season has no business putting a number in a village's book, and she had been in that room when he said it and he had not looked at her once since.

She stood up at about twenty past six on the first question, before he had answered it, and Vail let her.

"Two numbers," said Elsa Skell, in the local language, and Tamsin translated. "Not one. Ours and his, and we have both had them since the nineteenth day, and they do not agree, and I have come eleven miles so that they are in the same room for the first time, and I am not going to pretend mine is right and I am not going to pretend his is wrong, and I am going to say what mine is and how it is made, and then he can say what his is, and then three hundred people can see that two women in this city have been counting the same damage for five days and have got two numbers, and that is the actual answer to your first question and it is not a measure and it is not a work."

She had it in her coat. Everybody who has been in this city for a month has learned that the people with the worst news carry it in the inside pocket of a coat, folded once, and have it out before they have taken their hat off.

"Four hundred and eleven measures of winter grain, at the price Corrow is paying this week, is the season. That is the number the whole of this is *about*, and every person in this room has it, and it is the number in the book, and it is a season." She turned the sheet round. "Mine is not a season. Mine is what Four Elms and Silt Row and Black Sluice and Long Water have had to put into a ditch bank in eleven days, in labour, at four in the morning, in the dark, by people who were not asked and who have told nobody, and there are nineteen of them and I have nine of the nineteen names and I am not going to give the other ten out in a room because they did not tell me they were doing it.

"Nineteen people. Four hundred and eleven measures. And a miller on the upper channel with a barrow, twice a day, since the day the water came up, who has put flour on the banks of the second ditch and has told nobody and is going to go on not telling anybody until the field is in, and that is not a number in any currency and I do not know how to price a thing that nobody will admit to."

Marek Osley had the waxed tablet out and was writing, and he said, without looking up, "A barrow of flour at the second gate at Silt Row, on the Wednesday, on the Friday and on the Sunday, at about four. I have had a man count it for me and I did not ask him to and he has since been told he was doing something wrong." He squared the tablet. "That is three barrows and I have it. I want it understood that I have it and that I got it by accident and that I have had it for four days and have not put it in, because it is not a cost of the instrument. It is a kindness. And a kindness is not a cost and I will not have a notary enter a kindness in a cost column, because in about two years somebody will use that column."

---

The second question is the one Adrian could answer and he answered it in nine words and then spent four minutes failing to answer the rest of it.

"Adrian Vale," he said, in the local language, and Tamsin did not have to correct him, and he noticed again that she did not, and it was the second time in five days. "In measures, we do not agree, and I have heard the two numbers, and I am not going to stand here and average them, because a number that is an average of two women's counts is a number that belongs to me, and the only thing I have in this world that is mine is a mark in a wall.

"In the work: what spent the field was this city's ordinary maintenance, forty men saying four words twice a day under a gate nine feet from the join in that gate, and I have said so in a notebook and in a yard and in a room above a market and I will say it here. Not the man in the join. The words. The man found the pattern; he did not make it, and the pattern is this city's, and the toll is this city's, and the only question left is by whom and whether they agreed to it, and they have not, and I have been saying that for five days and no two hundred people in this city have accepted it, and they are right not to, because I have not produced a way to stop it that does not drown eleven villages in the next flood.

"Who is answerable. My name, and it is in a book, and it is entered as a season, and it was entered at its smallest on the morning of the third day of this flood, and it has been growing about forty times a day ever since, and I do not have a way of stopping it and neither does anybody in this room. And what it costs me in a month: it has cost me a post in a yard, a season of one man's attention, the only road out of this world, and on the twenty-fifth day of this month it cost me the ability to say a sentence in a room and be believed, which I did not have before and have not had since."

"You did not have that before," said Wenna Cray, from the back, in English, flatly. "You had it on the fourth day, in a market, in the rain, and you used it to get a child put in a barrow."

"Yes," said Adrian. "I did."

---

The third question is the one that nobody in that room believed anybody was going to answer, and the answer came from the back of the hall, in English, from a woman of forty-one who had not spoken.

It was **Kirsty Lund**, sixteen, who had come out of the ground on the twenty-fifth day, who had cried for a minute and a half in a yard and then stopped entirely, and who had said almost nothing for three days.

"The question is: how will they know they have been paid," she said. "And I have been sitting here for an hour and I have been thinking about the fact that I have been in this room for three days and I have not known until about ten minutes ago what a *household* is."

Nobody in the hall said anything.

"In — where I come from, we don't — " she stopped and started again. "If you do a thing for somebody, they know, because you tell them, and there's a record, and the record has your name on it, and if they don't pay, everybody knows they didn't pay and that's the whole of the enforcement. And here — you *cannot* do that. You cannot stand in a room and name the people who haven't paid, because a village that can't promise a date to a water gate doesn't keep its share of the water, and that's not a fine, and everybody here knows it, and I've worked that out in three days from four sentences Tamsin said out loud on a bench on the first day.

"So if the answer to your third question is *the book says they've paid*, then nobody out there believes it, because everybody out there knows what a book is worth, and it's worth exactly what it was worth last time somebody was hungry.

"So the third question doesn't have an answer yet, and it can't have an answer in a book, and I'm not being clever, I just — " she looked at Vail, and then at the woman in the second row, and then at Adrian, and she said it as though she were the only person in the room who had noticed a piece of information on a piece of paper. "You need a person to knock on their door. That's all. You can't write it. Somebody has to go and stand in a yard and say *we have entered that you are paid, and here is the person to say it to*, and if the person who knocks is the same person who owes it, then nobody's going to believe them. So it can't be him."

The room was quiet.

"That is worth more than everything else said in this hall this morning," said Nell Bray, in English, in a felt hat, at the end of the third bench. "And it is a sixteen-year-old, and she has been here three days."

---

And then the pale field came up in the middle of a converted salt store with about three hundred and ten people in it, in the air, in the colour of a paper form, and Adrian saw six people see it happen and nobody else see anything at all.

> **A DEBT MAY BE CLOSED.**
>
> **A debt that is closed is a debt that is finished. A finished debt is a small debt. A small debt may be paid in money, in salt, in grain, or in opened measures, and the person who pays it may be any person, and the person who is owed it may accept the payment on behalf of the person who owes it, and in that case the debt is closed and the matter is over.**
>
> **There is no further cost.**

It stood there for nineteen seconds.

The fourth line was in the grammatical position where a cost goes, and it was the same construction as *the cost of the stone is not part of the workway*, and *the cost has been paid, there is nothing further to pay*, and *the holder may be shown which one the water is using*, and Adrian had four of them in a notebook now and had learned, over four weeks, to read the shape of a sentence rather than the content of it, and the shape of this one was the shape of the other four, and it was the best one yet, because it was the only one that had ever offered him to *stop*.

The thing it was not saying was that a closed debt can be bought. Not out loud. Not in the field. The field would have said it if the field had wanted it, because the field had wanted him to know about the boundary on the fifteenth morning of this month in a flood month in eleven words.

The field did not need to say it, because everybody in that hall already knew it, and had known it for a fortnight, and had said it in four markets: a debt that can be closed can be closed by *somebody else paying it*, and once it is closed the person who owed it is a person who was *helped*, and a person who has been helped does not have to be angry at anybody.

A debt that can be closed is a debt that can be traded.

---

He let it go for about nine seconds, in front of three hundred and ten people, and then he said:

"No."

Marek Osley put his tablet down, which he had never done in public in a month.

"Mr Vale—"

"You have been the best man in every room in this city for a month," Adrian said, "and you have the only number here that anybody can carry to eleven villages, and you have been carrying a hazard of unknown size since the third week, and I have known that you wanted a number I did not have, and I have thought about you every time I opened that book. So I am going to say the thing out loud and then I am going to ask you to carry me."

He turned round to the whole hall.

"I am not going to settle this. I am going to ask to be **continued as a creditor.**"

The hall made a noise.

"Continued," said Vail. "Say the rest of it, because in a book *continued* is a word with a shape."

"The debt is not closed and it is not paid and it is not reduced. It stays open, it keeps growing, it stays enforceable, and I stay the person who owes it, and it comes back to this room on a date that is on the board. The season of the bottom field, entered against my name on the third day of this flood, at its smallest, continues at its present size, which is bigger, and which will be bigger still, and which does not have a final figure attached to it because there is no such figure and everybody in this room knows there is no such figure."

"And the reason," said Vask, from the wall, in the local language, "is the only interesting reason anybody in this room has had in a month, and I am going to make him say it in his own words or I am going to stop him."

"Because a debt that can be closed is a debt that can be traded," Adrian said. "Not traded between people. Traded *out*. Somebody pays it, and then it is a favour, and then the person who owed it is a person who was helped, and then there is nobody to be angry at, and then the thing that the whole of this city has been doing for a month — which is to be angry at a man in a book, out loud, in a hall, at a first light — stops being possible. And I have spent a month learning that a seat is a place to be angry at, and that four witnesses exist so that four people have to be angry, and I have learned it from a docker in a felt hat in this building, and I am not going to be the man who quietly pays his own account and takes the whole of the credit for it in a market in six weeks."

The room had gone entirely still.

"So: continued. Enlarged. Named. With a new review, and I want the new review to be *named* in advance and not described, and I want the interval to be short enough that the person who owns this gate can see me before March."

Elsa Skell stood up in the second row and said one sentence, and it was in the local language, and Tamsin translated it, and it is the sentence Adrian has on the wall of the loft under everything else.

"You have been carrying a number that goes up," she said, "and I have been carrying a number that goes down, and only one of us is going to be right in March, and I would rather the book said that than have it settled today."

---

It passed at about the seventh hour of the morning, in public, in about nine minutes, and the way it passed is in the ledger, and Marek Osley read out the tally himself because he had asked to, and the tally was two hundred and one for, seventy-eight against, and thirty-one abstentions, and there had never been a vote in that hall in twenty-six years, and Vail entered the first one in the history of the Lantern Concord with the reason and the hour, and Kerrow entered the fact that a notary who does not construe her own terms had permitted a count, and Vail said, in a flat voice, "The notary did not permit anything. The notary wrote down what the room did."

Adrian Vale was readmitted to the seat for a further term, and the seat came with a different cost column, and the column is on the wall of the loft and in the book, and it is this:

> **THE COST OF THE SEAT, FROM THE THIRTIETH DAY OF THE FLOOD.**
> **One. The standing labour of the yard, twice a day, in the open, in any weather, by whoever is nearest, and the holder of the pull is the nearest whenever he is within four hundred yards of that yard, and may not leave the yard on a day on which the words are to be said.**
> **Two. A new page in the ledger, opened for the eleven persons who came out of the ground under the yard of the lock chapel on the twenty-fifth day, and kept in the same book and by the same notary, and the holder of the pull may not sit in that seat if that page is not written up as well as any other page, and may not move it to a different book.**
> **Three. The review, on a date named in advance.**
> **And there is no fourth item, because the holder asked for there to be no fourth item, and a woman with a bowl told him in a room that a rule about six people made by a man who had not asked was the worst rule in the wall, and he heard her the first time and understood her the second.**

---

And then he said the thing about the workway, because the board was four feet above his head with a date on it that had run out five days ago and about two hundred and sixty people in that hall could read it.

"The chalk on that door is expired," he said. "It has been expired since the twenty-fifth. I am going to say the rest of it out loud in a room, once, in front of a notary, and then it is finished and it does not have to be said again.

"A man in a good coat offered me a workway in a chapel on the second night of this month, and wrote it in chalk on a keeper's wall with a date on it, on the second Saturday, in front of six named people, a keeper of that room, and a mason. He read the cost out loud in a room on the twelfth night, unasked, accurately, and told six people in a row what it was worth, and argued that it was worth paying, and told me that I had no standing to refuse it on behalf of anybody but myself. And on the Saturday night after that he put his own hand to it, under the keeper's line, and gave it a shape, because — his words — an offer with no shape in it is a rumour a decent man can walk away from.

"I refused it twice in rooms with witnesses. The first time I said it was too dangerous and that was the wrong reason. The second time I said it was because I am too occupied, because I have a duty with no shape, and if I take a duty with a shape then the shape is the one that pays, and mine is not, and I would have decided something in advance in a room in a chapel for free. And both refusals are in the roll of strangers that goes to Aurel in a wooden case, in my own words, with the two dates on them, and the two dates agree exactly with the two dates on the board.

"I am staying Unbound. I have no workway, I am not going to have a workway, and the reason is not that it is dangerous. It is that a man in this city who has taken a workway is a man who can be asked where he is going, and I am a man nobody in this city is owed anything by, and I have spent a month finding that out, and the whole of what a workway would have given me is the ability to be asked, and I have got that in another way, and I got it from a woman who will not name me, in a yard, at two o'clock in the afternoon, and I would rather have that in a yard where anybody can stand in it and watch it happen than have it in me where nobody can check.

"That is the whole of it. I am not going to say it again. If a man in a good coat comes to me about it in a market, I am going to read him the entry on the roll of strangers, in the market, in the local language, and it will have my name and two dates on it, and that is all I have and it is enough."

He sat down.

Hettis Roke stood up and said, "Enter that. All of it, in his words, and the date on the board, and the fact that the date ran out on the twenty-fifth and that it stayed up for five days after it ran out, and that a man saw it every morning for five days and did not take it down."

---

And at about the ninth hour of the evening of the thirtieth day, Ivenn Marrow came up into the yard under the lock chapel with a cloth and a bucket, in daylight, in front of about thirty people, and took the chalk off the keeper's board himself.

Nyla Ferrow stood in the archway and watched him do it and did not stop him and did not help him, and afterward she said that this had been the correct thing and that she had been waiting five days to see whether he would do it in daylight or at night, and that a man who does it at night is a man who knows what he has done.

He wrote something underneath it, in the local language and then in English, in a small, very neat hand, and it took him about a minute, and Adrian read it upside down from the other side of the trestle, standing four feet back, and then had it read out loud by Tamsin Quill for the yard, because the yard had asked.

> **Offer withdrawn. Not refused: refused was the eleventh day, twice, in a room, and the refusals are in the roll and they stand.**
>
> **Withdrawn on the thirtieth day of the flood, five days after the date on this board, by me, in daylight, in a yard, because I set the date and the date is mine.**
>
> **I am not asking him to take it and I am not withdrawing it because he refused it.**
>
> **I withdraw it because the thing it was offered for has stopped being true in a way that a workway could not have told him, and because he has now said out loud in a hall, with a date on it, in front of three hundred people, why he will not take it, and the answer he gave is better than the answer I would have given him, and I have been in this city seven weeks selling a thing and I have been beaten at it by a man with no rights in it.**
>
> **And I would like the reason written in his own hand under this, on this board, in this yard, where the keeper's five words are, and I would like it to be the true reason and not the one in the book. I have noticed that he writes the true reason on the page and the fair one in the room. I would like the other one.**
>
> **— I. Marrow, of Aurel. No Crown office. No warrant. My own hand.**
>
> **And I will say, since nobody in this yard will write it: he will do it. He has never yet not written down the true reason.**

Adrian Vale stood four feet back from a trestle in an archway and read the last line of a document written by a man he had decided was editing the wall, and he could not answer it, and he did not go and get the notebook, and he stood there for about a minute and a half, and then he said, in the local language, badly, and Tamsin did not correct him:

"You have known me nine weeks and you have sold me four things and I have refused two and I have not been able to prove the other two, and you have just written down in a yard in front of thirty people that you think I will do what you want, and it is the most accurate thing anybody has ever written about me and it is in chalk on a keeper's door, and I would like you to understand that I have noticed, and that I have a notebook, and that in a month you and I are going to have a conversation that neither of us is going to enjoy."

"Yes," said Ivenn Marrow. "I am counting on it. I have not got anywhere else to be."

He put the bucket down, and washed the board, and Nyla Ferrow took the cloth and washed the other side, and the four words and the fifth came up out of the lime and the clay and the black of it came away, and the keeper's words were on their own board again, in her hand, four inches high, and the place under them was clean, and a man in a good coat went down the two hundred steps of the walk-round in the dark to be useful to somebody else.
