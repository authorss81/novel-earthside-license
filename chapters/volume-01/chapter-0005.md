# Chapter 0005 — Seven Names, One Door

The split in the outer wall was eight feet long and four inches wide at its widest, and it had a bright edge on it like a blade that had been left in the sun.

That was the first thing Adrian noticed, and he noticed it in the voice of a man filing an observation, which is how he noticed everything. The edge was hard and white and entirely ordinary. Anyone standing on that walk-round in broad daylight could have told you it was the cut of new stone against old, or light coming through a gap, and they would have been right. The wall of the outer channel had opened along a join that had been scabbed over three times in living memory, and where new stone meets old stone the sun gets in and makes a line you could cut yourself on.

The second thing he noticed was that the air coming out of it was warm.

Not hot. Warm the way a car is warm. It came in a thin flat sheet at his shins, and it smelled of engine oil and hot dust and something faintly sweet underneath, and Adrian's whole body knew that smell before his mind did, and his mind arrived a half second later with the only conclusion available: that was a car park. That was a car park on a day like any other day, in a city he had never visited, four hundred miles or four thousand miles from a fourth-floor workroom in a building where nothing had ever happened.

The third thing was a man shouting in English about his legs.

He was on the walk-round. He was twenty feet along, past the half-rotten rail, lying on his side with one leg up and both hands on a section of wall that had no business being a door, and the wall was letting him go in one shoulder at a time.

Adrian got there. He got the man's collar with both hands and hauled and the man came out of the stone as completely as if the stone had been a coat, and went down on the wet walk-round in a heap, and Adrian knelt on him and found he was breathing and had blood in his hair and no idea where he was.

"—where," the man said. "Where the hell. Where am I. What was that, what was that thing—"

"I don't know," Adrian said. "Neither do I. Don't move your neck."

---

By the time Adrian had got back to the corner, the wall was doing the noise again.

It was a low grinding, structural, the sound of a thing that has decided to become a different size. The men on the walk-round backed off. Tamsin came along at a dead run, which was the first time Adrian had seen her move fast, and she went straight to the wall and put her hand flat on the stone on either side of the split and said something short and formal, the way you speak to a dog you are about to have to hold.

Then she turned around and said, "It is opening."

"How wide can it get?"

"I do not know. Nobody knows. It is not a gate. A gate has a frame."

The grinding stopped. The sheet of warm air went out of the air like a breath being drawn in, and the pressure dropped so hard in Adrian's ears that the world rang, and then the split made a noise like a door being shouldered and six people came out of a wall in the space of four seconds.

They did not arrive. There was no opening, no threshold, no sense of a passage. One second the wall was a wall, and the next second there were six people on a wet stone walk-round in a language they did not speak, and the wall behind them was already closing, and the light on the cut edge of the join was already going out.

---

The first sound was a woman's voice saying, in English, in the flat professional calm of a person who has decided to be the calm one: "Okay. Okay. Who — is anybody hurt? Don't move yet. Don't move, just — Owen, stay down. Owen, stay *down*, I need to look at your head—"

The second sound was a man's voice, older, with a clipped, professional edge: "Back against the wall. All of you, back against the wall, hands where I can see them. I don't know where we are, I don't know who's out there, but you don't crowd a doorway. Ma'am, I need you to move two feet left."

The third was a man laughing, once, in a way that had nothing to do with humour, and then a woman retching into her own hand.

The fourth was nothing at all.

The fifth was a man saying, with a strange, patient, horrified politeness: "I want everyone to stay exactly where they are. Nobody touch the wall. Nobody touch *anything*. I need a minute. I need one minute to think and I am asking you for it."

That was Owen Park, and Adrian was going to spend a great deal of the next weeks finding out that Owen had been saying *let me get oriented* in that voice since he was nineteen years old.

---

They came at Adrian separately, which he was grateful for later, because it gave him time to see them one at a time.

There was a tall Black woman in hospital scrubs sitting on the stone with her back against the wall, one thigh held out at an angle, doing something to another woman's hands. The wound was a graze four inches long running from the outside of her knee up her thigh, deep enough to show something underneath that was not a colour she liked. She had already torn a strip off somebody's shirt for a pad. She was breathing through her mouth.

There was a man in a warehouse vest with a bloodied face, holding his own forearm against his chest. The forearm was wrong. He knew it was wrong. He was holding it exactly the way you hold something you have decided to cope with later.

There was a young woman with long dark hair sitting in the wet with a gash running up one forearm, holding a phone up in front of her face, screen toward herself, thumb moving.

There was a heavyset man of about forty-five in a good coat, entirely dry except for the hem, who was counting. Adrian could see his lips going. He counted twice, and got a different number both times.

There was a small woman in a flour-dusted apron folded over and being sick, with a paper bakery bag crushed in one fist.

And there was Owen, flat on the stones, bleeding from a cut in his scalp, absolutely calm.

"Mina," the woman in scrubs said, without looking up. "Mina Okoye. I need boiled water and I need a clean cloth and I need somebody to hold this leg still. I'm a nurse. I have been a nurse for six years." She looked up, and her eyes went along the walk-round, along the wall, along the men with the hooks, and came back. "I'm going to need somebody to tell me where I am in about ninety seconds, and right now I've got six of us, and I've got one bleeding scalp, one forearm, one gash, one aspirating, and a graze I can't see the bottom of. Has anybody got water?"

---

It was Renna Ord who answered, and she answered by handing her waterskin to Mina Okoye without a word, which told Adrian more about how the morning was going than the previous four hours of argument had.

Then Mina said something in English to the man in the warehouse vest, and he said, "Kuo, sir. Dante Kuo," and she said "Tamsin's going to love this" to nobody in particular, and he said, "I don't know who that is, ma'am, but I'll take it."

The rest of it happened in about four minutes and Adrian did not enjoy any of it, and understood every part of it.

Dante Kuo got the six of them — the five who could stand and the one who could not — into the angle of the wall where the walkway widened, back off the open run, and made himself the thing that a frightened crowd pushes against instead of the thing it scatters from. He did it by being six foot two and by not asking permission.

Jonah Pike, the man in the good coat, established himself at the edge of the group with his hands visible and his voice at a volume that made three Veyrans step back, and said, in a register Adrian recognised instantly as *having done this in a room somewhere before*: "My name is Jonah Pike. I run distribution for a living. I need to know three things and I need to know them in this order. Is this Earth. Is that water drinkable. And is anybody else here."

"It isn't," said Mina, without looking up. "And I don't know. And there's us."

"It *isn't*?"

"The light's wrong and there's no grid and the sky's the wrong colour. Sit down, please. You're going to fall over and I'll have to catch you."

The woman with the phone said, "I'm Priya," and then, to nobody, still moving her thumb: "Okay. Hi. So — I'm Priya, this is Priya, I'm going to say what's happening, because if I don't say it then nobody has it, and there's six of us and no, sorry — seven of us, sorry, seven, I keep — seven. Seven of us, nobody knows where we are, there's a wall that did that, and I'm recording because if I don't then the only copy is going to be in somebody's head." She looked at the phone, and the screen showed her own face and a bar with no signal and a red dot and a crossed-out line, and she swore, and then, unexpectedly, laughed. "No bars. I have no bars. I've got a hundred and forty percent battery and no bars. That's very on-brand for the worst day of my life."

The small woman said, "Noor," and then had to stop, and got a little way down the walkway on her hands, and Jonah Pike moved and caught her by the shoulders without asking and without making a thing of it, and she let him.

"Adrian," Owen said, from the stones. "I'm Owen. Owen Park. I've got a thing I need to say and I want to say it while I'm calm, so." He got up onto one elbow. His face was grey and bloodied and absolutely serious. "There's light. Behind the wall, where it opened. Not daylight, not a lamp. It's shaped. It's like a — it's like a coin, or a seal, standing up on its edge, about the size of a man's head, and it's *there* and it isn't a thing any of you can see, and I know how that sounds, and I watched the last four seconds of your stone go past my shoulder so I'm not a *crank*—"

"Ivenn," Mina said.

Owen stopped.

"Sorry, ma'am?"

"Sorry. Name. Say it again for the record." Mina's hand had not stopped moving. "Owen, you're not a crank. I don't know what you saw. Nobody here saw it. But you said *Ivenn* just now under your breath, before you said it out loud, and that's a detail, and details are the only thing I've got."

Adrian had been watching the wall. The pale edge had gone out. The split had closed to a hair and gone dull and wet, and the join around it was visibly, ugly hot — a band of stone about the width of two hands that was steaming faintly in the cold air, which should not have been possible, which meant it had just had something enormous pushed through it.

Then he looked for the thing he had been looking for since the wall, without admitting to himself that he was looking for it.

He looked for a box in the corner of his vision. He looked for a line of text with an objective in it. He looked for the small pale field that had appeared in front of him once, over dark water, with three sentences on it, and he looked for a frame, a header, a way to look at what he had been given and what it had cost him, and he put his attention out in every direction he could think of and there was nothing there. No panel. No line. No status, no marker over anybody's head, no way to tell that Mina's thigh was worse than the rest or that Jonah had already decided to be in charge.

There was a man bleeding on a stone walkway, and six of them, and no display anywhere in the world telling him what any of it was for.

He felt the panic come up, and it was the specific panic of a man who has spent nine years building the screen people look at, discovering that the screen was never the thing.

---

Tamsin arrived with four dockers and Roke and a woman with a wide board of lime and clay, and she did the only thing anyone could have done with the situation, which was to make it small.

She put herself between the walk-round and the yard, she said about forty words in a language Adrian does not have, and she pointed at Mina's leg and at two of her own people, and two of her own people went and knelt on the stones and did what local people evidently did with an open wound, which was to say nothing clever and everything slow. Then she said, to Adrian, in English, with an exhaustion that matched his own exactly:

"Now. Tell them. Not the version you would tell a room. Tell them the true one, which is that you do not know what the wall is and neither do I, and that nobody here has agreed to anything, and that they have injuries and a wall and no road, and that you are about to be asked by a man with a slate to swear to a thing you do not believe." She looked at his palm. "They are going to ask you how to get home. Decide now what you are going to say, because you will not get to think about it, and whatever you say will be the first thing they use to decide whether you are a person or a system."

She was right. He had about ninety seconds and he used about forty of them deciding that he was not going to stand here and talk about a route, because he did not have one, and every hour he spent trying to produce one was an hour that Mina Okoye spent with somebody's thigh.

So he crouched down in front of them, on the wet stones, in a suit that had not been dry since a Tuesday in another world, and he said:

"I'm Adrian. I came out of that wall about eight hours before you did, and I don't know where we are. That's the true version. There's a place called Saltwake, and there's a lock, and the wall is built into it, and there's a flood going on, and I have been wrong about at least one important thing today already." He looked at the mark on his palm. "I have this. I don't know what it is. I've been here one day and everything I thought I knew about how any of this works has already been wrong, and I would rather tell you that now than sell you a plan I've made up."

"Do you have a way back?" said Jonah Pike.

"No."

"Do you have a *way*—"

"No. Not one that works. Not one that I can find. And I'm not going to pretend I've got one this afternoon, because if I tell you we've got a route and I can't deliver it, you'll spend three days betting on me instead of on each other, and I can't afford to do that to you." He heard how it sounded. "I can tell you what the wall did, and I can tell you there are eleven people in this city who maintain a thing the way I used to maintain a thing, and that they have rules, and the rules are not stupid. And I can tell you that a woman called Tamsin just sent two of her own people to hold that woman's leg because we asked, and that nobody asked us for anything in exchange."

Nobody said anything for a moment. The rain came back. It had been doing that on and off all day, the small kind that does not fall so much as arrive.

Then Mina Okoye said, without looking up, "Okay. Somebody get me boiled water. I don't care whose it is, I want it *boiled*." And Dante Kuo said, "I need to see this wall," and Priya said, "Everyone give me your name and say it slow, I'm writing all of it down, and then I want somebody to tell me who's missing from this city because there is going to be a second one of me in about three hours and I'm not doing it from memory."

And Adrian Vale crouched on the wet stones of a lock in a world that was not a game, and understood that he had just been given a job by five strangers, which was the first honest thing that had happened to him since 1:14 a.m., and the worst.

---

They gave him their names and their fears, and he wrote both down.

He did not know why he thought to ask for the fears. It was not an onboarding question. It was the thing you did at the start of a long flight when somebody was frightened of flying: you asked what they were frightened of so that later, when they were shouting at altitude, you knew what the shouting was about and could tell them it was normal, and not something wrong with them.

*He asked for a name and one thing you are afraid of, and he wrote both down, in his notebook, in the order they said it, with the spelling they gave him.*

Mina Okoye: *That I find out I can help and it isn't enough. That I make a call and somebody dies and I know it would have gone the other way and I keep it anyway.*

Dante Kuo: *That I was the one holding the door. I'm always the one holding the door. Somebody hands me a door and then it's my fault when the door doesn't hold.* He said it fast and flat and then said, "Don't write that part."

Priya Shah: *That it turns out I did this for content. That the whole thing was a bit and I made people laugh and now somebody's dead and they didn't.* She stopped. "That one sounds stupid when I say it out loud."

Jonah Pike: *That there's no order here. That we get to a place with no list and no plan and nothing to hold, and everyone slowly stops asking who's in charge.* He looked at Adrian. "You're going to find out whether you can do that too, by the way. Everybody finds out."

Noor Haddad: *My mother's house. That's it. That's the one. I'm sorry — everyone says a person. For me it's my mother's house, and my mother's in it, and I'm the only one who's ever left.*

Owen Park said, "That's cute, but nobody in a whole mess of a situation like this is named Owen Park, and anyway mine's real." He waited for a laugh. He got a sort of one, from Priya, at the wrong moment. "Somebody walks in and I don't get to do the thing. I've been doing the thing for twenty years, and I get to be the one who stands between the crowd and the thing, and instead I'm lying on a *rock*—"

"Do you see it?" Adrian said.

"What?"

"Still there. The light." Adrian had not asked that because he believed him. He had asked it because a man who said I am not a crank and then could not answer the simplest follow-up question was a man in trouble, and Adrian had watched a great many people in trouble at work. "You said it's shaped like a coin. Is it still shaped like a coin?"

Owen looked at the wall, and something happened in his face that Adrian had not expected to see, and did not have a name for.

"It's not near the wall," Owen said. "That's the thing. It walked off."

He stopped. He put his hand over his eyes, and he said, in a completely different voice, the voice of a man apologising to somebody who was not there: "I'm sorry. I'm sorry, I'm not — I'm not doing this, I'm not doing the crazy thing, I'm just—"

And behind them, up in the yard, above the rooflines, the window of the old lock chapel lit up.

They all turned. It was not a fire. There was no fire. The little arched window of a chapel four hundred years old, which had been dark since the flood warnings went up, was filled with a low, even, white light of the exact colour of the mark on Adrian's hand, and it was not flickering, and it did not go out when the voice of the man who maintained the lock called out into the yard, *Nyla, what is that, Nyla—*

"Nyla," said Tamsin, from somewhere behind Adrian, in a voice with nothing in it at all, "close the room."
