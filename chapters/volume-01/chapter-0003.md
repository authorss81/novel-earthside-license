# Chapter 0003 — Names Are Prices

The maintenance yard was a rectangle of packed gravel between the lock's outer wall and a row of sheds with tin roofs, and by midmorning it held eleven people, two carts, a great deal of shouting that had stopped when Adrian came through the arch, and the smell of wet wool drying on a line.

Two of them were wearing the same grey-green coats with a hearth emblem sewn at the shoulder, and one of those was sitting on an upturned crate with a waxed slate on his knees and a stick of chalk, writing the whole time. He was broad, going grey at the beard, and he had the particular stillness of a man who intends to be there long after everyone else has gone.

It was Tamsin who walked him in, one hand between his shoulder blades and not touching him, and the woman with the boat hook was already there, standing in the middle of the yard with the hook grounded like a fence post.

"Does he understand you," Tamsin said to her.

The woman said two words and pointed at her own chest, and then at the yard, and then said the three syllables again.

Tamsin said, "Her name is Renna Ord. She would like you to say it and she would like you to be afraid of saying it wrong. Do not tell her I said that."

He said it. It came out badly. She said it correctly on purpose, so he would know she had done it on purpose, and then said something longer, three words of it, with a hand that gestured at the wall of the lock behind him.

"She says the tide brought him and the tide can take him back and she would like to know which you think is more likely," Tamsin said.

"You came out of the wall," Adrian said.

"Yes," said Tamsin.

That was the only word she gave him all morning that he could not have guessed, and she gave it like a concession.

The grey-coated man on the crate said a question. Tamsin did not answer it. She turned to the younger of the two dock workers — Ovin, with a blanket around his shoulders and one arm held carefully against his ribs — and asked him something short, and Ovin's face did something complicated and embarrassed, and he said something, and the older dock worker with the felt hat said something sharply, and Tamsin said something back that made all three of them quiet.

Then she looked at Adrian and pointed at the mark on his palm and said four syllables, and the yard listened.

He did not get the whole of it. Not close. But he got the shape of it, over the next ten minutes, in the way you get the shape of a language by standing in it — pieces arriving without grammar, each one carrying its own little weight.

The woman in the boat-coat had been at the wall and had pulled a boy out of a hole. Yes, that was in there. Three syllables for the boy: skyborn. And then a longer phrase around it that had the shape of a joke, and the older dock worker made that almost-laugh noise again, and Tamsin said, flatly, in English, "It was a joke. They call anyone who comes out of the air that, because it used to be funny." She turned her head. "It stopped being funny in about the year my mother was born. Do not thank them for the word."

The grey-coated man held up the slate so Adrian could see it. He had drawn the lock, roughly, and a line for the water, and a mark in the wall like a crack, and a stick figure in the water with its arms out.

He was called Hettis Roke, and he was the Hearthguard of the outer ward, and he had spent the first hour of this sitting in a wet yard making himself into a person who could be asked questions, which meant he had learned the eight or nine words of English he would need and was using every one of them.

"Who came out of the wall," Roke said. "Write it. Give the hour. Give your name as you give it here, and the name of the one you were sent for."

"I wasn't sent," Adrian said. "I don't know how I got here. I don't know what this place is. I don't know what that is." He held up his palm.

"Write."

Adrian had spent nine years designing forms. He knew exactly what this was, and he knew that a form which cannot be refused is not a record, it is a receipt, and he had a joke about receipts that nobody else was ever going to hear.

He knelt down in the wet gravel and put his finger in it and drew, in the dirt, the shape of the mark on his palm. He drew it twice. The first time the steady part was wrong and the branching part was right, and the grey-coated man leaned in and said something to Tamsin, and Tamsin said, "He has it exactly. Look at the forks. I have watched four men try to draw that mark off the inside of a sleeve and none of them get the two short spurs. He has drawn it from memory on the ground in a minute, and he cannot write a single one of our letters."

They still could not have his name in their hand. Nobody here wrote Latin. He had been reaching for a moment when he could have felt clever about this and found that he did not have it in him; the truth was that he was kneeling in wet gravel in a suit in front of eleven people who had almost drowned a boy this morning, and the only thing he could offer was a line drawing.

Tamsin solved it the way she solved everything else, by doing rather than arguing. She walked him out of the arch, across a plank bridge, up a stair that had been worn into a curve on the inside of the turn, and pushed open the door of the lock chapel, which was small, and low, and full of wet stone and cold tallow and about four hundred years of a working trade.

The chapel keeper was a woman of perhaps sixty with a face like folded cloth. She had a stick of chalk, a bowl of water, and a board of lime and clay, and she was doing something with all three at the base of an arch where three different jurisdictions met inside the same doorway.

Adrian watched her for about ninety seconds.

She did it in a sequence. Chalk, three marks, a pause where she said something short. Clay, pressed flat, smoothed with the heel of her hand until it was the same colour as the stone. Water, taken from a brass cup and touched to the middle of the patch, and then the words again, a little longer, and her right hand laid flat on the stone for the length of it.

He said, "Is this a tutorial?"

The chapel went quiet in a way that had texture to it.

The chapel keeper turned her head. She had not been turning it fast. She looked at him for four full seconds with an expression Adrian would spend the rest of the day trying to describe and would never get right, and then she said four words, very evenly, and put her hand back on the stone and finished the last line of the recitation with her palm down.

"Tamsin," the woman said. "What did he say."

"Ask him."

"Ask him what he said." Tamsin crouched down until she was below his eye level, which took a while, because she was taller than she looked. "Say it again."

"It's not a tutorial," Adrian said. "I don't know what it is. I said it because I don't have a word for it and I'm—" he stopped. "Sorry. I'm sorry. That was a bad word to use. I don't know why it's bad. Tell me why it's bad and I won't use it."

That was not a thing he had planned to say. He heard himself say it and could not afterwards remember deciding to.

Tamsin looked at him for a moment. Then she stood and said something to the chapel keeper, three sentences, and the woman answered with four and turned back to her board and did not look at him again.

"She is the keeper of this room," Tamsin said. "Her mother kept it. Her mother's mother. That is not a performance and it is not a lesson. In about two hundred years there will be a woman here doing it in a room with different stones in it, and the point of her doing it is that this room is a place where three places agree to keep a gate. When she says the words, she is making sure that agreement still exists. You called it a tutorial. Do you understand why that was the wrong word?"

"No," Adrian said honestly. "Not really."

"Good. It should not come easily. Wrong words that come easily are how people end up being wrong in ways they cannot take back." She looked at the arch, at the wet chalk and the patch of clay under the old woman's palm. "You want the real word for what she just did, and I will give you three, and you may not use them unless you have earned them, and I will decide when."

Then she gave them to him anyway, in the next four minutes, standing in the middle of the floor with her arms folded, and he wrote them down in his notebook with the wrong spellings and she corrected two of them.

A *working* is not a spell and not a prayer. It is something done to a relationship that is already there, and it is only done to things that already exist between people or between stones. A *witness* is not an audience. A witness is the person who sees the working happen and accepts it, which means somebody who could have stopped it and chose not to. A *residue* is what a working leaves in the place afterward, and the residue is never nothing.

"And the three things you need," she said, "before any of it counts as one. A pattern — something real that is already there between people or stones. A hand on it. And a witness who agrees." She looked at the chapel arch. "And under all of it, a cost. If there is no cost, then nobody did anything and the stone is only stone."

"What's the mark on my hand, then?"

"I do not know yet." She said it as a fact, not a deflection. "You carry a *bond*. That is the word for a promise that has been given a shape, and a shape is not a small thing. It means somebody is holding the other end, and I do not know who, and I do not know whether they mean to." Her mouth did something that was not quite a smile. "When a man comes out of a wall carrying a bond, and he has no idea what it is for, and there is a flood in the yard, and the gate is failing — Tamsin Quill, what does your family say happens?"

"That the sky has taken an interest," said Adrian, who had been listening to the other end of that.

"That the *river* has taken an interest," said Tamsin, "and that a god would at least have the manners to say so first."

The grey-coated man appeared in the chapel doorway with his slate. He said four sentences. Tamsin listened with her head slightly down and then translated them without softening anything, which Adrian appreciated in a way he could not have said out loud.

"He wants a confession. He wants it in his hand, he wants the hour, and he wants it sworn. He is not unkind and he is not clever, and he is going to ask you again in an hour, and if you cannot swear he will have to write *unwitnessed* across your whole name, which in this city is worse than being hanged, because a hanged man's family gets a box and an unwitnessed man's family gets a question." She paused. "He is also right to ask. I have four men on a working rotation because of what you did this morning. Two of them will lose a day of pay. I would like that on the record and I would like you to be the one who says it out loud."

Adrian said it out loud. He said it slowly, the way you read out a number that somebody is going to have to live inside, standing in front of a woman with a stick of chalk and a man with a slate, and it was the first time in four years that he had said a sentence in a room whose consequences he could not have predicted, and the room did not reward him for it, which he was beginning to expect.

Tamsin said, "Good. That is a bond you can pay. You will hold a debt to the lock until it is cleared, and it will be written where I can see it, and I will be one of the people who decides whether it has been cleared."

"Do I get a say?"

"You get a say in about a third of the questions. That is what a bond is. If you wanted all of them, you should have been born here."

Somewhere behind the chapel wall, water moved. Not much. A shift, a settle, the kind of noise a building makes when it is deciding something about itself.

---

The maintenance yard again, an hour later, in flat grey light. Ovin's arm had been bound with strips off a flour sack. Nell Bray, the older dock worker with the felt hat, had taken the copper token out of the boat hook's crook and was carrying it around on her palm, and Adrian stopped her in the middle of the yard and held out his hand for it.

"Where does it belong," he said, when she did not move.

She looked at Tamsin. Tamsin said, "Put it on the board."

There was a board in the shed: a slate the size of a door, ruled into rows in chalk, each row a number. Nell put the token on the number that was cut into it, and the row was empty, because the token had come loose out of the wall and gone into the river, and it had been off the count since before any of them were born. She rubbed the row out. She wrote the number back in and put the token in the gap in the board where a token lived when it was in its place, and Tamsin said, "Say it."

He said it. He stood in a muddy yard and said, in a voice he had borrowed from a man who reads things out in public, that a stamped copper disc belonging to a numbered part of this lock had been in his hand and was now back on the count, and that the number was on the board and the time was written beside it and the man who had carried it was the man out of the wall. Nell Bray wrote it down in a big square hand and did not look at him while she did it.

Then Tamsin took a dry coat off a peg and made him take off the wet one, and told him three things.

"You will not touch a working. Not to help, not to hold, not to steady it, not even if it is falling apart and you are standing in it. It is not yours and it is not mine and it belongs to the people who set it, and your hands in it will do what hands in a stranger's loom do."

"You will not use a name here. Not mine, not the keeper's, not the name on the board. If you call a woman by a name that is not yours to call her by, you have made a claim on her in front of witnesses, and she cannot answer it. If you have to make someone understand you, you point. That is worse and it is slower and that is the price."

"And you will ask before you take a thing. Every thing. Including water, including a rope, including a door out of a fire. Including a piece of somebody's day." She looked at the wall of the lock. "Especially a piece of somebody's day. Downstream there are eleven villages that use this water. Nine of them have never heard of you. All of them will be affected by whatever you turn out to be."

"And if I break one of those?"

"Then we will have to write down what you broke and who paid for it, in front of the people who paid." She said it without heat. "That is all. It is not a threat. It is the shape of the thing. You are going to want a promise. I am telling you that the promise is not what makes it safe. The record is what makes it survivable."

---

They went back into the chapel because the old woman had sent for Tamsin, and halfway across the floor Adrian stopped.

He did not decide to stop. His boot went down onto a stone that felt wrong, the way a stair goes wrong at the turn of a worn tread, and he looked down.

The chapel floor was made of long slabs. Between two of them, near the foot of the arch, there was a line of black. It was hair-thin and it ran dead straight for most of its length, which no crack in a four-hundred-year-old floor does, and which no mortar line does either, because the mortar lines here were lime and pale.

He crouched. He put his hand out flat above it, the way you put your hand out over a radiator to see if the heat is on, and there was cold coming off it. Not cold as in temperature. Cold as in the absence of a thing that should have been there.

"Nyla Ferrow," he said. He had heard her name in the yard and he used it, and then heard himself use it, and it should have cost him — Tamsin's second rule was about names and he had just walked into it. But she was already looking at the floor.

Tamsin crossed the chapel in three strides and went down on one knee beside the line. She put two fingers into it. She said one word, twice, and the second time it came out of her like something she had been told as a child and had not expected to use.

Then she sat back on her heels and looked up at Adrian, and for the first time since the stairs he could see plainly that she was afraid, and that it was not fear of him.

"How much water have you been standing in," she said.

"Since dawn. The chamber, mostly."

"That was the chamber." She stood. "Nyla. Water."

The old woman was already moving. She crossed to the far wall, lifted a board, and pulled a chain, and somewhere below and behind the chapel a gate of some kind began to grind on its bearing, slowly, the way heavy things move when they have been still for years.

Tamsin pointed at the black line in the floor. Then she held her hand out flat, palm up, tilted.

The water came off the stone.

It rose out of the crack and stood up on the floor, and it stood up about a hand's width, and it did not run. It leaned, the way it had leaned on the wall of the chamber, and it went slowly uphill across the chapel floor toward the arch where the old woman had chalked three marks, and where the three jurisdictions met, and where the maintenance oath was said out loud so that they would all keep a gate.

Nobody in the room moved.

Adrian's palm was hot. He looked down at it, and the pale branching mark on the heel of his hand was the exact width of the standing water.
