# Chapter 0026 — The List

On the morning after the eleven came out of the ground, a man in a chandlery on the upper road asked a serious question in a market, and Adrian Vale has the answer to that question written down in four books, in three languages, and it is the worst thing anybody has ever said to him.

The question was this. He heard it from Noor Haddad, who heard it from a stallholder, who heard it from a man who had been in the hall on the previous evening:

"They're players, though. Right? That's what they call themselves. So there's going to be more, and they're going to want things, and what do we tell the next lot?"

And what Adrian said, in the market, at half past eight in the morning on the twenty-sixth day of the flood, to about nine people who had stopped what they were doing, was: "Nothing has been decided about that, and nobody is anybody's anything, and I am not going to have that sentence in a market this morning." Which is true, and which was in no book anywhere, and which left a market of forty stalls with a hole in it the shape of the answer, and by the fourth hour of the afternoon there were four men from four different places in Saltwake who had each told a different version of the hole, and one of them had added a number.

The number was that there were going to be eleven more every time the thing wanted something, and it was on a market board by the ninth hour, and it was wrong, and Adrian could not get it off, and the reason he could not get it off is that the only person in Saltwake who could have corrected it in public was asleep for four hours in the middle of the day, in a room above a market, on the fourth night of a month, because he had stood at a counter in a hall for eleven hours yesterday and had then sat up half the night with a woman in a doorway and a boy of nineteen.

---

The hall was full at the first hour and the roll of public business had eleven names on it before Sera Vail got there, written in nine different hands by nine people who had been up all night, and she read them, and then she made the woman with the slate read them again, and then she said the thing she has said in this hall four times in twenty-six years and has never once enjoyed.

"Take them down."

"They've come in good faith—"

"Take them off the roll and put them on the back of it in a different hand, because the roll of public business is the front page of this city and I am not having eleven names in nine hands on the front page of this city on the morning after they came out of a hole, and a name in nine hands is not nine witnesses, it is one rumour with a page." She squared the ledger. "Anybody who has written one of those names may come and read it to me from the back and I will enter it as it is given, and I will enter what the person said about themselves and not what a neighbour said about them, and if a person is not here then they are not entered until they are, and no name of the eleven goes on the front page of this roll until every one of the eleven has stood in this room."

"Can that be arranged," said Adrian, from the back.

"It cannot be arranged. It is the opposite of arranged. It is *un*arranged. Five of the eleven were asleep at the fourth hour and two of them are in a room with a broken arm and one of them is eleven years old and has not said a word to anybody, and I am not going to have their names on a page in this city before they have been in this room." She dipped the chalk. "Now. What is the business of this hall today?"

---

The business of the hall today, in the end, took six hours and it was not what anybody had come for, and the reason it took six hours is that Adrian Vale tried four times to get to the thing he had come to say and was stopped four times, and the four stops are the whole of it.

The first stop was at the third hour, when Jonah Pike stood up on the end of the third bench and said the thing that was ninety per cent right, and it was ninety per cent right, and Adrian knew it was ninety per cent right at the time, which is the part he has written down most often.

"There are eleven people in this city who arrived yesterday with no clothes that fit, no language, no idea where the water is, no idea what a gate is for, and no idea that the ground they came out of is four feet from a hole in the floor of a yard where three hundred people were standing," Jonah said. "And there are eleven people in this city who have already had one terrible day and are about to have a worse one, and I am telling this hall that the single most dangerous thing you can do with eleven frightened people is be kind to them slowly.

"A frightened group with no structure is not a group. It is a *mob*, and a mob is not a bad word, it is a shape, and shapes are what crowds become. In three days one of them will be starving and will take something, and not because he is a thief, and there will be eleven hundred people in this city who have decided in advance what he is. In five days one of them will be ill, and there is a woman in this city with a room by the wharf who is going to have to decide whether to look after him, and she has not been asked and she does not speak a word of his language. In nine days somebody who is not from this city is going to come down that stair with a wagon, and I have seen the other side of that nine times, and on nine of the nine there was a list." He put his thumb on the bench. "So: give them a rota, tonight. Give them a place to sleep that is not a floor in a market. Give them a person to complain to who is not the man in the book, because that man is a queue and everybody in this room knows it. Give somebody the job of finding out which of them has family, and give somebody the job of finding out which of them cannot eat what we eat, and give somebody the job of standing in this hall every morning and saying out loud what is going to happen today. That is a structure. And a structure is a kindness, and I am telling this hall that kindness without a structure is not a kindness, it is a delay, and the delay is where the mob happens."

The room made a noise that was about two-thirds agreement.

"It is ninety per cent right," Adrian said, "and I am going to say the other ten per cent out loud because Jonah has just done me the enormous favour of saying the ninety per cent in a hall in front of two hundred people, and if I only argue with the ten per cent then I look like a man protecting a private scheme."

"Go on, then," said Jonah.

"Ninety of that is right and I would have written it if he had not. Here is the ten per cent. Everything you have just described is a *list*, and there is a woman in this city called Renna Ord who has told me that a crew is a man who has owed somebody a shift, and that you ask a man, and if he comes you owe him, and if you do not pay you do not get to ask him again for nine days, and that the order in which people are asked is *known* and is older than the writing. That is a rota. It is not a list. And a list of eleven people with no shift attached to it is not a rota. It is an *order*." He looked at the hall. "It is the difference between a wall with a door in it and a queue at a door. And the difference is about four hundred yards of bank and eleven households in Corrow, and I have spent a month learning it, and I have been getting it wrong, and the woman who taught me is in this hall and will correct me if I have got it wrong."

Renna Ord was in this hall, at the back, in a boat-coat, and she said, in the local language, "He has got it right."

"Then give them the job," Adrian said. "All of it. Tonight. But give them a *job* and not a *place*, and give it to a person and not to a name on a board, and the second half of that means that no name of the eleven goes in any column of mine or Jonah's or anybody's, and if somebody turns up at this market on Tuesday and asks to see the list, they do not get a list, they get eleven people and a person to ask."

---

The second stop was at the fourth hour, and it was Priya Shah, and it was the worst thing that happened to Adrian all week, including the floor.

He had been getting up in the hall, in the gap in the floor, twice, and both times he had begun by explaining what the eleven were, and both times he had got a sentence and a half in and stopped, because there is no sentence available to him. He is not a Veyran speaker; he has four words of the maintenance oath and a bad sixth of the local language and he has been living in this city twenty-six days. And the eleven speak English. And that is the entire problem and it took him until the fourth hour to understand that the problem was not the language at all.

"You keep saying *the second cohort*," said Priya Shah, from the fourth bench, in English, and there were about ninety people in the hall who spoke enough English to know that two of the seven were talking and not the man in the seat, and it is the moment that everybody who was in that hall still has.

"Second what," said Adrian.

"Second cohort. That's the fourth time today. And you have used it nine times in three days and I have counted, because I count things, it is what I do, and I have been the one who counts things about us for a month." She stood up. "You have got a *first* cohort, which is a word for a group of people that came out of a hole in a numbered order, and you have a *second* one now, and in about a fortnight there will be a third, and Adrian — the first six came out of a hole in a numbered order too. That is the only reason they are the first six. There is nothing in them that was there before the hole. And every person in this hall knows that, and the six knew it, and it took a woman with tweezers and a man in a yard to make us say it out loud, and now you have walked in here on the second morning and given them a *number*."

The hall had gone quiet in a way Adrian had heard three times in twenty-six days.

"I said the word myself," Priya said. "On the first morning, in a corridor, and I have been ashamed of it for twenty-six days, and I am not going to be ashamed of it for a second sentence. *Cohort* is a word for a thing that was *selected*, and I do not know how it got into your mouth, because you do not talk like a man who has read a manual, you talk like a man who has been in meetings, and meetings are where you get that word. I want it stopped. In this hall, in the room above the market, and on the board. And if it goes into a book anywhere, I want to know, because I am the one who writes the books about us and I do not want to be the one who wrote it."

"Nobody is going to be selected out of a hole," said Adrian, in the local language, badly, and Tamsin corrected him twice.

"Then why have you got a first and a second," said a man on the ninth bench whom Adrian did not know.

And Adrian said, "Because it is quicker," and about ninety people in a hall laughed, and it was the fourth time in twenty-six days that the true thing had been said out loud in a room in this city and been the funniest thing in it.

---

The third stop was at the fifth hour, and it was the eleven.

They had been at the back of the hall since the second hour, on the two benches at the end, all of them, because nobody had told them to move and nobody had told them they could leave, and they had been there for three hours, and at some point in the fourth hour a woman from the Concord's own bench had put a cup of water in front of each of them without asking, and at some point in the fifth hour two of them had started talking to each other in English at the volume people use in a waiting room, and Adrian heard eleven consecutive minutes of it, standing four feet away, and understood every word, and understood nothing.

It was not a language problem.

They were talking about **stats**. Not abilities — *stats*. They were trying to describe, in five different vocabularies, the same set of numbers they had all seen, and they could not agree on what any of them meant, and two of them were insisting that a number had gone *down* and four of them insisting it had gone up, and the argument was not confused and was not dishonest, it was a disagreement between people who had been given the same shape in a different order and had each built a different house out of it.

"They cannot be shown a thing and be told what it is," Adrian said, to Tamsin, in English, under her breath, in the aisle. "That is what it is. They cannot be shown a thing and be told what it is, and we have all been doing that for four weeks and I have been doing it the hardest, and I did it to them and they have spent a month in a world where nothing in it would tell them anything."

"And what would you have them do instead," said Tamsin, in English, with her eyes on the two benches.

"I do not know. I would have them ask a person."

"That is the fourth time this month you have said a sentence that is the answer to your problem and you have said it to me in a whisper in an aisle." She did not raise her voice. "Ask them. Out loud. In this hall. Not us — *you*, and in their language, which you have, and which you have more of than I do, and which is the only language in this building that all nine of us and all eleven of them have ever spoken, and you have not once used it in public in this city in twenty-six days, because you have been afraid of how you will sound, and I have watched you, and I have not said anything, and I am saying it now."

---

So he asked them.

He got up in the gap in the floor at about the sixth hour of the morning, in front of about two hundred people, and he said, in English, which about ninety of them understood badly and which the eleven understood completely, and which Tamsin translated into the local language sentence by sentence without being asked and without being told to and without a note:

"I am going to ask you a question and I am going to do it badly and in the wrong order, and I want you to correct me while I am doing it, because you have all been here two days and I have been here twenty-six days and I have got more wrong in twenty-six days than any of you can imagine.

"Here is the question. There is a wall in this city, on a stair, two hundred steps down, with a shape in it eighteen inches long, and four people signed a book in the first week saying it is not a door and costs a day's labour a month. On the twenty-third night a woman in a raft gave me four lines of paper and said the cost of a thing must be paid by the thing that benefits and not by a person, and I have brought those four lines to this hall and they are going to have to be answered and I have not got an answer, and I am not going to pretend to you that I have.

"And here is the second thing and it is the one I actually want. **There is no mark in this world that would take anybody home.** Not on me, not on the six, not on you. In the first week I put a mark into that wall in front of nine people and I could have taken it back and I did not, and it is in a stone, and nobody can get it out of a stone, and I have written on the wall of a loft that I do not know whether that mark could have brought anybody home and that nobody will ever be able to tell me. So: nobody is going. There is no arrangement by which one of you is going home in the next month, and I am saying that in a room in front of two hundred people because I have been saying it quietly to six people for a month and quiet is how it becomes a rumour with a number in it.

"And the third thing, and this is the one I am actually asking. **I am refusing the work.** A man in a good coat offered it to me in a chapel on the second night of this month and wrote it in chalk on a keeper's wall with a date on it, and the date was the last day of the salt month, which was yesterday. It is expired. I have taken nothing. I have no workway, I am *unbound*, I cannot open that shape and I cannot read a route and I cannot perform a working of any kind in this city, and I have said so twice in rooms in front of witnesses and it is in a roll that goes to Aurel in a wooden case, and I would like to say out loud, in this hall, why, and I have never said it in a hall, and I am not going to say it in a whisper to six people in a loft.

"I am saying it because I have been the only one able to see anything for a month, and it is the best feeling I have ever had in my life, and about a week ago a man in this city put a sentence in a book in chalk where everybody who comes up that yard has to read it, and the sentence is that I do not know where a sentence came from that came out of a stone in his own words, and I do not know whether that is a thing that is being done to me or a thing I am doing to eleven villages, and I have put it in a place I cannot get at alone. And I am not going to be the man who has a thing that nobody in the room can check. That is the whole of it. There is no more reason than that and I have been looking for a better one for a month."

Nobody in that hall said anything for a while.

Then a man on the fourth bench said, in English, in a bad accent, loud enough for the room: "You are saying you cannot do anything, mate."

"I am saying I cannot do anything," Adrian said. "That is exactly what I am saying. In this world there is nothing I can do. I can see a line sometimes and I cannot choose it and I cannot point anybody at it and it gives me a headache and once it gave me a nosebleed, and every time I have ever been in a position to do anything in this world, somebody local has done it and I have stood four feet back and been told I was not needed, and they were right every time, and the one time I was asked to do a thing, in a yard, in front of thirty people, a woman refused me, and she was right as well."

The man on the fourth bench turned round and looked at the two benches at the end.

"Bro," he said. "That's the worst possible answer."

"Yes," said Adrian. "It is. And it is the true one, and I would rather say it in a hall than sell you something in a whisper. And I have got a form on a wall upstairs that anybody in this city can use to say *I saw a thing and you cannot see it*, with a refusal clause on it, and a clause a woman by the wharf wrote in it that says nobody is to be harmed, refused work, refused water, or moved for saying so, and I would like to know whether any of you want to use it, and I would like to know before I answer anything else."

---

The fourth stop was at the seventh hour, and it was the one that mattered, and it was not a stop at all.

Vail had spent three hours waiting for it and had said nothing, and at about the seventh hour she got up with the ledger and the chain and did the thing she does, which is read out in a flat clerk's voice with no softening anywhere in it, and Adrian — who had spent a month building toward a version of this entry and had it almost right — discovered that every word of his version was wrong and that the notary had been building toward the right one since the eleventh hour of the previous day, in a yard, in the rain, with a slate.

> **Entered the twenty-sixth day of the flood, in the hall of the Lantern Concord of Saltwake, at the seventh hour, with about two hundred persons present, and read out twice, and entered against the separate list and not against the list of the six.**
>
> **Eleven persons came out of the ground under the yard of the lock chapel on the twenty-fifth day at the eleventh hour and eleven minutes, with water, without consent, without witness, and without any working of anybody's. They did not come through the instrument called the Narrow Mark. They did not come through the main breach at the Lock of Salt. They came through a dressed joint beneath a floor of a building, described and not named, in a place above the outer channel and on the bank of the river.**
>
> **They are entered as persons who came through without asking. That is the whole of the description and it is the same description that was entered against the six on the first day of the flood, in the same words, and the reason it is the same words is that the only thing that made the six different from anybody in this city was the order in which they arrived.**
>
> **Their names are entered as each person gives them, and not as a neighbour gives them, and not as a person of this city writes them, and any person on this list may strike their own name from it at any time, without giving a reason, and if a person asks for their name to be struck the entry and the hour are struck and the striking is kept.**
>
> **They are not entered as an order, a company, a party, a class, a cohort, a settlement, a faction, or a rescue. They are not entered as anything except persons, and any of those words appearing in any document concerning them after this day is entered as an error and read back.**
>
> **No person on this list has asked this city for anything. No person on this list is owed this city anything at the date of this entry, except the eleven of the second list, who are owed food, a place to lie down, dry clothing, and an answer to the question what the ground they came out of was, and those four things are entered as debts of this city and are not debts of any person.**
>
> **And it is entered, in the words of the holder of the pull, that the answer to that fourth question is: there is no mark in this world that would take any of them home, that he put the only one that existed into a wall on the fourth day of the flood in front of nine witnesses, that he could have taken it back and did not, and that he does not know whether it could have brought anybody home and that nobody will ever be able to tell him.**

It took Vail four minutes to read and the room did not make a sound in any of them.

And then she said, "And I want it noted that I have entered that last paragraph on the word of a man with no workway, an unrecognised instrument and an open debt, and that it is the only paragraph in twenty-one years of this book that I have entered entirely on the word of a person with nothing behind him, and I have done it because it is the only paragraph in twenty-one years of this book that a person could not possibly have entered in order to get anything."

---

It is on that entry, and not on anything Adrian did, that the second group became a list. And what it cost is the thing that is on the record too, at the eighth hour, from the end of the last bench, in a flat voice, in English, in front of about two hundred people who could not follow it and were all listening.

**Eli Mercer**, nineteen, stood up.

"Right," he said. "Two things and then I'm going to sit down, and I want the second one written down properly, because it's about me and I'd rather be the one who says it than have somebody else say it about me later.

"First: I don't want to be on that list with a refusal clause, because a refusal clause is a good thing and I want to be on the list, and I'm not going to be precious about it. That's mine to want. Nobody's to ask me twice.

"Second." He looked at the six. Six rows back, on the left, in a row that had been there for an hour, sat six people from another world who had come out of a hole in a different wall about five hours after he had come out of his, on the same morning, in a city of a thousand people who had gone on with the day.

"I'm not joining them. I know what everybody's doing — you're all doing it, the man in the seat is doing it, the woman with the slate's doing it. You're all looking at those six and going: *those are the ones who know how to do this.* And I get it. I do. I did the same thing on the walk-round yesterday, I saw them and I thought, right, they've got a woman who speaks the local language and they've got a man who's got a *thing in his hand*, and I should get next to them, because this is a game and that's the progression.

"And I want to say out loud, in this room, in front of these people, that I don't want to be attached to those six and I don't want to speak for them, and I'd like it written down in whatever book that goes in.

"Not because I don't need them. Because they've got a month and I've got two, and in a month they learned how this place works and I haven't learned how anything works, and the second I stand next to them I'm a guy with a party, and in nine days I'm the guy who got a party. And then when something goes wrong — and something is going to go wrong, because something went wrong *yesterday*, on top of you, in a hole, in a floor — it's going to be *their* thing that went wrong, and they'll have a month of it in a book, and I'll have two.

"So no. Separate list, separate witnesses, separate everything, and I'll say all of it in my own handwriting and I'll be the first one to say something that gets me in trouble with the other ten, which is the only way I know to be sure they can trust the rest of it."

Somebody at the back laughed, and it was not an unkind laugh, and about eleven people from another world did not laugh at all.

Nell Bray, in her felt hat, said from the third row, in English, in a voice that carried perfectly: "That is the first thing said in this hall in a month that I am going to remember when I am sixty."

And then Priya Shah, who is twenty-four, and who had her hand flat on the ledger, said the sentence that closed the day and started a week.

"That goes in," she said. "All of it. In his words. Including the part where he worked out that he wanted to attach himself to us and attach himself to us anyway, because that is the part that matters and it is the part that is going to be in a market on Friday if I do not put it in a book on Saturday." She looked at Vail. "And I want the six asked. In the record. All six. Asked whether they came through without asking, and whether being the first has been made to mean that they should speak for anybody else, and whether they want to be named as the people who know how this works."

"That is a foolish thing to ask," said Dante Kuo, in English, from the fourth row.

"It is the only foolish thing anybody has said today," said Mina Okoye.

---

Owen Park's shoulder was in a sling in a corner of the hall for four hours while he did not use it, and Hesper Saye looked at it twice and said nothing, and at about the fifth hour he stood up and went out, and nobody stopped him, and Adrian watched him go and did not follow him, and it was the first time in twenty-five days that he had watched Owen Park leave a room and understood that he was not permitted to come along.

At the ninth hour the room emptied and the eleven were walked to the Salt Weavers' chapel two floors above the market, which has four benches, a floor, a window behind the plaster that nobody has opened in six years, and a key held by a market woman, and no arrangement of any kind beyond that.

And at about the eleventh hour at night Adrian went down and sat in the dark on the second step outside the back door, in the cold, with a cup of something hot that Sesh Brack had put in his hands without saying anything, and worked out what the day had done to him.

For three weeks he had been privately, privately embarrassed.

Not by the word. He had stopped flinching at the word in about the fourth day, because it was accurate in a way nothing else in his life had ever been accurate: there was a seam, and there was a rule, and there was a man in a join, and there was a cost, and the whole arrangement had the shape of the thing he had spent nine years building and had spent nine years being quietly ashamed of, in rooms in another hemisphere, with people who did not know what they were sitting inside.

He had been embarrassed by it privately, and he had told nobody, and he had got on with a month of work. And on the morning of the twenty-sixth day of the flood a stallholder asked a serious question in a market, and by the fourth hour a number was on a board, and by the ninth hour four men in Saltwake had each told a different version of it, and one of them had added the number eleven, and by the second day after that there was a market in this city that believed there were going to be more of them every time the thing wanted something.

It was not a lie. That was the part that put him on the second step in the cold at the eleventh hour. *Player* is not a lie about the wall. It is a lie about the people, and the people are real, and a lie about real people, in a city that keeps a count, is a thing with consequences that arrive on foot.

And it was now an **asset**. Somebody could use it. Somebody was already using it — a stallholder with a board, a man from a chandlery, a woman of forty-one who had put herself first in a room on purpose and got exactly what she wanted. And in nine days a man in a good coat would put it in a letter, because a letter to Aurel that begins *an unrecognised instrument is admitting persons who describe themselves as players* is a letter that arrives. Adrian had spent three weeks refusing to say the word in a room. He had refused it, and it had gone into four mouths and onto a board, and his refusal had not slowed it down by one hour.

He had spent a month learning that names move because somebody reads them out loud, and then a number had moved faster than a river boat, and neither of those lessons was any use at all against a word.

He got out the notebook, in the cold, on a step, by the light of a market shutter, and turned to the page where Hesper Saye's question was copied out in the local language with Tamsin's hand and Mina's capitals under it, and read it again, because it was the only sentence he had that was about a person and not about a wall:

*Who gave you the right to make that rule for six people, and what happens when one of them does not want to tell?*

And then, underneath, Owen Park's answer, in a hand that pressed too hard: *then she finds out anyway, and he does not get to be the reason she didn't.*

And underneath that, in flat capitals: *And if I am the one who finds out, then I am also the person who decides whether it was survivable. Ask somebody else.*

And then, in his own hand, on the twenty-sixth day of the flood, at the eleventh hour, on a step in the cold, with a cup going cold in his other hand:

*Then it is the eleventh person, and then it is the hundredth, and the question is not about the wall. I have a form on a wall with a refusal clause in it and a clause a woman by the wharf wrote into it, and the first person to use the refusal clause is the man who wrote the form, and the first person to say the word out loud in a room on purpose was a boy of nineteen who wanted to be on a list and not be attached to a party, and neither of those things was me.*

*Ask the people who maintain the gate. Ask the keeper. Ask the woman who keeps a count. I have not asked one of them about a single thing they care about.*

*And the only sentence I have got is a question somebody asked me in a room, and it is: what happens when one of them does not want to tell? And the answer is that I have to build the thing that does not need them to tell me, and I have four days to do it in, and I do not know how.*
