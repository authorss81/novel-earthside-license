# Chapter 0023 — The Unverified Man

The first thing Hesper Saye said to him was not about his head.

She had come up to the second stair at about nine at night on the twenty-third day because Owen Park had sent a boy with a note, and the note said, in Owen's flat careful hand in the local language, which he could now write badly and correctly and had practised, *I would like to be seen. I know you do not do private work. Come up.*

She came up, and she put her bowl on the table, and she did not touch him.

"Sit down," she said. "Now. Last time I asked you a question in this room in front of nine people and you did not have an answer, and you wrote it on a wall, which was the correct thing to do, and two people answered it and one of them was you."

"That was nine days ago."

"It was nine days ago and I have thought about it every day since, which is not a thing I do." She turned her sleeves back. "So I am going to ask you the same question in a different room, and I am going to ask it in front of nobody, because that is the version of me that is worse, and you are going to find that out tonight, and I would rather you found it out from me."

"Ask."

"Whose head is it." She said it flatly. "Not whose wound. Whose head. Because a man from another world has come out of a wall and something happened to him on the way, and I have had nine people in this room tell me in three weeks that the wall did it. The wall did not do it. I do not know what did it and neither do you, and there is a difference between a thing that happened to a man and a thing that a man is *carrying*, and I have spent thirty-four years telling the two apart, and I want to know which one you are bringing up that stair to me tonight."

Owen Park looked at the table.

"I do not know," he said. "That is the honest answer and I have had nine days to improve on it. I went through a wall that was not a door and I came out the other side with a wound on my head that has had clean skin over it for ten days and has not begun, and you have a word for that, and the word is *unclosed*, and I have not told anybody in this city which part of me is the unclosed part."

"Which part is it."

"The part where I am the only one who can see it." He said it without drama at all. "I put a statement in your city's book on the eighth day and it says *unverified* in a man's hand and the man was right, and I have never asked for it to be taken down, and I would like it to stay exactly where it is for as long as I can stand having it there."

---

Hesper Saye did not look at his head that night either, and Adrian understood about half of why and Tamsin understood all of it, and the half was the following.

Somewhere in the second week Owen Park had begun making other people go.

It had started small and it had not felt like anything. He had asked Ottoline Vask, in a market, in the middle of a conversation about lime, whether the outer wall of the outer channel had ever been sounded along below the cap. And Vask, who is a woman who has been asked about walls for thirty-four years and who cannot leave the subject alone, had gone down on her own time on the Thursday and had sounded eight feet of it with a hammer and had come back and said, to Owen, in the local language, in a yard, in front of six people, *there is a hollow under the cap and I will not say what is in it and you are not going to make me say what is in it.*

And Owen Park had said, thank you, and had written it down, and had not told anybody that he had asked.

Then he had asked Ovin Sarr.

That one had been on the Monday, at four in the morning, on the walk-round, and it had been done so well that it took Adrian two days to see it. Owen had not asked Ovin to go and look for anything. He had come out to the walk-round at half past three to bring Ovin a flask of tea because Ovin was on a solo watch and nobody brings a boy tea at half past three unless they are glad to see him, and then he had said, *I have not been down there at night at all, it is on my list to go, and I cannot find anybody to hold the line while I go*, and Ovin Sarr, who is nineteen and has been alone on that wall six times and is extremely proud of it, had said, *I will hold it. Do not tell Roke I said I would hold it.*

And Adrian found it out on the Tuesday morning, from Renna Ord, who found it out from Ovin Sarr's father, who found it out because Ovin Sarr told his father he was going to be on the wall at four in the morning on the Tuesday, and a boy's father notices a change of hour.

"He has asked three men," said Renna Ord, in the yard, at about eleven on the morning of the twenty-fourth day, with her arms folded and a barrow in the yard behind her. "Vask, the boy, and a man from Silt Row I do not know. And I want to tell you exactly what he has done, because I do not think he knows and because I have watched a man do this before and it is always the same and it always looks like *taking an interest*."

"Tell me."

"He has spent." She said it flatly. "That is the whole of it and it is the whole of what a crew is. You ask a man to come. If he comes, you owe him. And if you do not pay, you do not get to ask him again for nine days, and everybody knows you did not pay, and that is not written down anywhere and it is older than the writing. He has asked the boy. He has asked a mason who is thirty-four years in this city and who was in the middle of a job. And he has done it *pleasantly* — that is the word for what he is doing, he has done it *pleasantly*, in a market, over lime, and the pleasantness is the method, and I want to hear you tell me a pleasant man with a good coat is not a man building a crew out of people who do not know they are in it."

"I am not going to tell you he is," Adrian said. "I have watched him do it for four days and I did not stop him and I want to say that out loud before anybody else does it."

Renna Ord looked at him for slightly too long.

"Right," she said. "Then here is the rest and then I am carrying a barrow. He has not paid them. Not because he is mean. Because there is nothing to pay them *with*, and the reason there is nothing to pay them with is that a thing a man did at four in the morning on his own is not a shift and is not in a crew and is not owed, and it goes into the little pile of work in this city that men do and tell nobody about, and in about two months the pile is going to be the biggest pile anybody has ever seen, because the lock is stepping, and the lock is going to keep stepping, and men are going to keep going out at four in the morning on their own to look at it, because there is nothing else to do and no instrument to do it in and nobody with a page." She shouldered the barrow. "And a thing nobody has a page for is a thing that can be taken off a man without anybody noticing it has been taken."

---

Adrian found Owen Park at the top of the walk-round at about two o'clock on the afternoon of the twenty-fourth day, which was a Thursday, which was the day of the year that the tide table puts the lowest water of the month at about seven in the morning, and which was therefore the day the excavation was to be done, and which Owen knew perfectly well.

He had asked Vask about the outer wall. He had asked Ovin Sarr to hold a line. And on the morning of the day of an excavation he had told Adrian, in a yard, in front of Nyla Ferrow, that he would be on the walk-round from six.

"Why six."

"Because it is the hour of the thing." Owen was standing in the rain at the top of two hundred steps with a lamp off. "I have been on that wall at six every morning for ten days, before the rain, before anybody, and I have never once seen the light when I was anywhere but here or in the field. And I have never once seen it in daylight, and it has never once been there when I brought the lamp lit, and the first time I saw it I had been in a hole with five other people in it and I had been — " he stopped. "It is not a thing I have been able to make smaller. That is the thing I would like somebody to hear. I have tried to make it smaller for ten days and I cannot make it smaller, and every time I have tried I have got a little worse at things I *was* good at, which is walking into rooms."

Adrian stood in the rain on a stair and worked out, in about nine seconds, that he was about to say the thing the mason had said to him in a yard, and that it was going to be the third time he had said it and that the third time would be the one that stuck.

"You cannot go alone," he said.

"I know."

"Not because of the water. Because of what you did to a nineteen-year-old with a flask of tea."

Owen Park's head came up.

"Renna Ord told you."

"Renna Ord told me and then I told Mina, because of the fourth rule, and Mina told me that the rule was mine and that the reporting is hers, and that if I break the rule I have to tell her I broke it, and I have now broken the rule twice in a week and told her both times inside an hour, which she says is a system and not an accident." Adrian put his hands in his pockets. "So here is the whole of it and I am not going to dress it up. You cannot go alone, because if you go alone and you see something then there is one man and a lamp and a wet field and no way at all to make it a thing anybody else has to answer. And you cannot get another man, because the only way you have found to get another man is to be pleasant to him over lime, and that is not a crew and it is not a shift and it is not a page, and it is *taking men*. And I have watched you do it three times in four days and I did not stop you, and if a mason in this city with thirty-four years in it gets hurt on a wall at four in the morning on your account then the Concord's book has got my name in it and the only thing anybody is going to remember is that I was the holder of the pull."

"Then what," said Owen Park.

That was the question that cost the next four days, and Adrian did not have an answer to it until about eleven o'clock that night, and what he had at eleven o'clock that night was not an answer to the question, it was an admission that the question had a shape and that the shape was the problem.

Because the honest reason Owen could not take a man with him was not that the men were being spent. It was that **the only person in Saltwake who could corroborate a light was a man who perceived join-lines**, and there was one of those, and he was standing in the rain, and he had no workway, and he had been told in a yard in front of thirty people that he could not read anything under the chapel floor, and had not been wrong.

He said that. All of it, in about forty words, in the rain, on two hundred steps.

Owen Park listened to all of it and then said, "Then that is a rule and not a wall," and went down the stair to do the six o'clock walk-round on his own, and Adrian stood in the rain and felt the specific, repeatedly-arrived-at nausea of a man who has just been told that the correct thing to do with his limitations is to build a procedure on top of them and then go and be useful inside it.

---

The four rules were on the wall of the loft, and under them was Hesper Saye's question, written in the local language in Tamsin's hand, and under that were two answers, one from Owen Park in a hand that pressed too hard and one from Mina Okoye in flat capitals.

Adrian sat down at the table at about eleven that night with the notebook and Priya's slate and a sheet of flour-merchant's paper, and did not write anything for fifty minutes, which was the longest he had gone without writing in twenty-four days, and it was the fifty minutes in which he finally understood the shape of the thing he was making.

The problem, exactly, was this.

A light seen by one man cannot be put in a record, because a record is a thing other people can act on, and a record of a thing one man saw is a record of a *symptom*. Owen's statement was in the Concord's book and it was entered as *unverified* and it was correct to enter it that way, and it was, in its present form, worth exactly nothing, because the only person who could bring it up again was the man who had seen it, and every time he brought it up he got a little more like a man who needs to be watched, which is what had happened, twice, in three weeks, and both times to a mason.

And the second problem was worse, and it was the one that made him put the pencil down at six minutes past eleven.

The two marks on his right hand were the same kind of fact.

A branching pale ghost running out toward the wrist, which he had pressed into a seam in a wall on the fourth morning of the flood, and underneath it, separate, two inches of hair-thin black that had opened on the second night and had not moved since the second morning. He could see the first about as well as anybody could see it. He could see the second perfectly well, and nobody else in this world could see it at all, because nobody steps over a hand.

He had written it down. He had it in a notebook, and Priya had it in her log, and Saye had seen the first and had not been able to see the second, and Tamsin had looked at both of them for a full minute in the light of a lamp on the first week and had said, in English, with total flatness, *I can see the pale one. I cannot see the black one. I have looked.*

He was, on the evidence of a stone, a man in a join, and a man in a join had been spending a hundred and forty acres an hour at a time since the first morning of the flood. And he could not prove it, and could not disprove it, and had a debt for a season, and a post in a yard, and was going to be asked in a hall in four days whether he was right.

*What we said, and not what it was.*

He wrote the two words at the top of a clean page, and then, underneath them, the first line of the form, and it took him until about one in the morning, and it is the single most useful thing Adrian Vale produces in Volume One, and it is on a wall in a loft above a market in Saltwake, in two languages, with five witness lines, and about eleven people in this city know it exists and two hundred have read the notice that describes it.

> **A STATEMENT OF WHAT A PERSON SAID THEY SAW, WHERE THE THING SAID CANNOT BE LOOKED AT AGAIN BY ANOTHER PERSON.**
>
> **Entered on the twenty-fourth day of the flood, in the loft above the river market, in front of five named people, by Adrian Vale, holder of the pull of an instrument entered in this city's book and not recognised, who has no authority to make this and is making it anyway, and says so here.**

The rest of it took until half past two. He wrote it in English and Tamsin translated it into the local language sentence by sentence and refused three phrases as *tidy*, which was her standing objection to a document, and which on this occasion removed about a fifth of the best sentences in it.

**One.** A person who has seen or heard or felt a thing that another person cannot see, hear, or feel, may enter a statement of it, in their own hand, with their own name, and nothing in this form requires anybody to believe them.

**Two.** The form carries a **witness chain** of not fewer than three names, and a name in the chain is a person who can be asked, separately and without being told what the other said, what they saw; and a name in the chain may be struck out by the person who put it there, at any time, without giving a reason, and the form stands with fewer names or with none.

**Three.** The **standing** of the form is this, and it is entered rather than claimed: *this is not evidence that the thing was seen. It is a record that a person said it was seen, and the record obliges somebody to answer it, and it obliges nobody to believe it, and it is not a finding and it cannot be used as one.*

**Four.** **A refusal clause.** Any person named in a statement may decline to have it entered, may have it struck out afterward, and may ask for the entry to be destroyed, in which case the entry is destroyed and the fact of the entry, and the hour, and the person who asked, remain.

**Five.** No statement of this kind may be the ground of a search, a seizure, an entry onto anybody's ground or building, a change in a price, a change in a wage, a change in a price of passage, or any other act against any person or place; and a person who has entered one may not be required by any office, guild, court, or instrument to act on it.

**Six.** A statement carries a **date of review**, and on that date it is either affirmed, withdrawn, or left to stand unanswered, and a statement that has stood unanswered for two reviews is not thereby made true.

**Seven.** Numbers and quantities in a statement are entered as *what the person said*, and never as *what it was*, and this is the rule of Mina Okoye, of this loft, and it is applied to this form because this form is a document and documents are where numbers go to get believed.

**Eight.** The form does not make anybody a holder of anything, a witness to anything, or a work of anything, and it does not put a mark on anybody, and it is not a working, and **it may not be performed by anybody who cannot speak the language of the room it is entered in.**

And then, at the bottom, in his own hand, under a rule, the sentence that made it true:

> **This form is not in the Concord's book. It has no seal. It is not a precedent. It was written by a man with a seat on an instrument that is not recognised, for a mason and a docker and a woman from another world, and it is offered to the Lantern Concord and it is not entered until somebody with a seal agrees to keep it, and if nobody with a seal ever agrees then it is a sheet of paper on a wall in a room above a market and I have said so here in a document that is going in a box.**

---

He filled in two of them before it was light.

The first was Owen Park's light at the third stake of the bottom field, in Owen's own hand, with the spelling of the local language, and with the witness chain containing three names of people Owen had not told, and Adrian wrote in the blank under the chain, before Owen read it, in the form's own seventh clause:

*That the three names in the chain have not been asked. That the person making this statement has not told them what it is about. That on the morning this is used, each of the three is asked what they saw, separately, and is told only that they have been asked, and may decline, and the refusal is entered and is not held against them.*

Owen read all of it twice, in the cold, standing up, and then said, "This is a form for a haunting."

"It is a form for a haunting," said Adrian, "and a haunting is a claim that cannot be tested, and this is the first one anybody in this city has had, and there is going to be another one in about four days, and I am not being funny, and I am not going to say the other one out loud tonight because I do not have to and because I would like there to be one night this month where I do not say it."

The second one was his own.

> **The twenty-fourth day of the flood, in the loft above the river market, entered by Adrian Vale against himself, without a witness chain, which is entered and not excused.**
>
> **A. I perceive a join-line in places I have no training to read, and cannot control, and it is not reliable, and it has cost me blood on three occasions.**
>
> **B. I hold a thin black line in the heel of my right hand, two inches long, opened on the second night of the flood. It has not moved, has not faded, and has not widened. No other person has seen it. A man in this city has told me it is a stone telling me what I am standing on. I have no way to test that and no way to disprove it.**
>
> **C. I have entered, in this city's book, a debt for a season of a field, and I have told this room that I have not taken a workway, twice, in front of witnesses, and the reason given has been the debt and a post. I want it read back to me in a year by a person who does not like me, and I want it read back *exactly* as I have written it here, and not as a fair summary of what I felt about it in the twenty-fourth day of the flood.**
>
> **D. If it is me, then everything said in a hall in this city about a season of a field is true, and I am the man who took it, and nobody in this world can prove that and the reason nobody can prove it is me.**

He read that one twice himself and then did not want to look at it, which he had expected, and which he had written down, because he was the sort of man who would want it later.

---

The form went on the wall of the loft at ten o'clock on the morning of the twenty-fourth day, above the four rules, and under Saye's question and the two answers, in the local language first and in English underneath, and there is a notice beside it that is the reason two hundred people in this city have read about it without knowing what it is.

> **Anyone in this city may enter one of these. You do not need a name of a person who saw it. You do not need anybody to believe you. You do not need permission and there is no office to ask. Bring it to the loft above the market, or to the market board, or to the market, and somebody will write it out for you in your own hand or in a dictation, and it will be read on the wall with the hour on it.**

Tamsin wrote the notice and would not let him write it, and then made him read it aloud to her four times, and refused the phrase *if you have seen something strange* on the grounds that *strange* is a thing a room does to a person, and made him write *a thing you saw and another person could not*.

"Noor is going to want a column," Priya said.

"Noor is going to want a column and she is going to be right and it is going to be the best column in this book," Adrian said, "and it is going to say *who gave it to us and what they expect*, and the answer is going to be *nobody, and nothing, and it is a sheet of paper on a wall*."

And Priya Shah, who is twenty-four and who had put six names on a wall over a market in the first week and had a fear on that sheet that she had had to take down in front of people, looked at the form for about two minutes and then said, in the flat voice she had learned from a woman in a room by the wharf:

"Adrian. You have made a form for putting a thing in a book that a person cannot prove, and you have made it so that the form cannot be used against the person, and you have written *may not be performed by anybody who cannot speak the language of the room it is entered in* into a document, which means it cannot be used on anybody who cannot get a witness." She put her slate down. "That is the best thing anybody has done in this city since the four rules, and the four rules are on the same wall, and I want it noted that I said that out loud in front of Tamsin, because I have spent three weeks being the woman who makes a record dangerous and I would like one line in my own log that says I said the opposite."

"You have said it in your own log already," said Adrian.

"I have, haven't I," said Priya.

---

Hesper Saye came up at about one o'clock on the afternoon of the twenty-fourth day, and looked at the form on the wall for a long time, and then said one thing about it, which is the reason it worked.

"You have not put in anything about medicine," she said.

"No."

"You have written a form about a thing a person saw and a person cannot look at again, and you have not put in one line about a person being *hurt*." She turned round. "In nine days, in this city, I have been asked about a head eleven times and about a shoulder twice and about a man who saw a light eight times, and there is nobody in this room or in that hall who has ever once written down a place where a person can be hurt because of what they said they saw. And I have been doing this for thirty-four years and I know exactly what a thing like that is used for in a town, and it is used for two things. It is used to stop people saying things. And it is used, later, by whoever has the bigger book."

And she took the chalk, and she wrote a sixth line underneath, in her own square hand, in the local language, and translated it, and it is on the wall.

> **A person who has entered a statement of this kind is not to be harmed, refused work, refused water, refused a place to stand, or moved from a place they are standing, on account of it, by anybody, for any reason, and a person who does any of those things has done it in the open and in front of witnesses and it is entered as a fact and not as a fault, and it is the fault of whoever allows it to continue.**

"That is a clause against this city," Adrian said.

"It is a clause against *a* city," said Hesper Saye, "and every city there is has a clause against it in somebody's handwriting, and in mine it is going to be in a document that a stranger from another world wrote, and I am going to be sixty in this city and I am going to have to live in it." She put the chalk down. "Now. Go and do your excavation, because it is a low water at seven tomorrow and Vask has been in my room for forty minutes this morning and she has not once asked me for anything and she has told me twice that she is not sleeping."

---

Owen Park came in at about nine that night, and did not say anything for a while, and then asked the thing he had asked on the boat, which was now eleven days old and which he asked again in a different form.

"You did not promise me the field," he said.

"I want to be careful with it and I am not going to be careful with it, because you told me a price on a stair and I have had two days to pay it and I have not," Adrian said. "Sunday. Four in the morning. The end of the third ditch, the stake, no lamp. I will stand on the stake and I will tell you afterwards what I saw and I will tell you the truth even if the truth is that I saw nothing, and I am telling you now in advance that the most likely truth is that I see nothing, because I have never in my life been able to see anything anybody else could not see and I have not got a second sense and I have got a headache and a bleeding nose and a line I cannot switch off."

"Then it is Sunday," said Owen Park, "and it is not the wall, and I want to be clear that I have noticed that I asked you for a field and not for a wall, and I have noticed that I did that on purpose about four hours after I asked, and I have not worked out whether it was on purpose."

"You have worked out exactly why it was on purpose," said Tamsin, from the table, not looking up. "You worked out why at the boat and you have been carrying it since, and it is that you have been in this country three weeks and every single thing you have asked for has been about a place you are stood in front of. And you are going to have to go and stand in a place in the dark at four in the morning once, for no reason, with nobody, in order to find out whether you are a man who can be in a place or a man who is always working out how to get into somewhere." She put her pencil down. "If you can do that once, then everything else anybody has believed about you since the Tuesday is still true. And if you cannot, then there is a great deal I am going to have to think about."

---

He wrote it up at eleven at night on the twenty-fourth day, in the flat hand, and it is four pages and the first line is the one he read back at the hearing in a fortnight.

*The twenty-fourth day. Nothing gained.*

*I have spent two days failing to help a man with a light in a wall, and the reason I failed is not the wall and it is not the light. It is that a claim which cannot be looked at again is a claim about a *person*, and there is no instrument in nine hundred years of this city that puts a person in a book without that person becoming a thing that can be used against them. The Crown has one. It is called a roll and it goes to Aurel in a wooden case and it says Unbound in my own words on a Monday morning.*

*And I have written one. It has no seal and no authority and no precedent and I have said all three things about it in the document, in writing, at the top, in front of five people, because if I do not say it then it becomes a thing that is being passed off as one of theirs, and that is the exact mechanism by which the Six Fears of the Seventh of this month happened, which was me, asking for a name and a fear in the rain because it was a good question.*

*And a healer by the wharf has written the clause that makes it any use at all, and she wrote it about a city she lives in, and she is right, and I would like it noted that I did not think of the part that matters and a woman with a bowl thought of it in nine seconds.*

*There are now two statements on a wall. One of them is a man from another world who can see a thing six of us cannot, and the other one is me, and I entered the second one about myself with no witness chain at all, and I want that read back in a year by a person who does not like me, exactly as I have written it, and I have written the sentence down myself so that nobody can improve on it in a good mood.*

*And on Sunday at four in the morning I am going to stand in a wet field on a stake beside a man whose head has not closed in eleven days, and I am going to see nothing, and I have told him so, and he said Sunday.*

*Ask the people who maintain the gate. Ask the keeper. Ask the woman who keeps a count.*

*And note, because nobody else in this room will: a mason has been told twice this month what to do by a man in a good coat, and a boy of nineteen was given a flask of tea, and I said the boy was careful and I was wrong, and the boy is a boy and the mason is not, and Renna Ord used one sentence and I have written it down four times.*
