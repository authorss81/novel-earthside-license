# Chapter 0046 — Ask the Crew

On the twenty-first day of the new month, which was the forty-sixth day of the flood and a Friday, forty men of this city's own crew were asked in a room they are in whether they would do a thing, in the words they use, and about half of them said no, and the half that said no gave one reason, and the reason was a rule of a docker of about fifty-eight in a boat-coat and it is older than any page in this book, and about four hours later the same forty men said yes to the same thing, in the same room, in the same words, and the difference between the two answers is a rule about a shift, and the whole of a distributed working in this world is the difference between the two answers, and it is not Adrian Vale's, and it is not Tamsin Quill's, and it is not a mason's, and it is Renna Ord's, and she has said so in four markets, and nobody asked her to until the twenty-first day of the new month at about the eleventh hour of the morning.

---

**At about the sixth hour of the morning of the twenty-first day of the new month, under the near gate, the foreman of the day crew said the four words for the first time in his life.**

He is called Hesk. He said on the third day of the flood, in a yard in front of a hundred and fifty people, that a lock is not a hole and a hole is not a door and neither of them is a rescue, and he was shouted down by a hundred and fifty people, and he voted against the working of the twenty-fifth day, and Adrian Vale entered his name in a book in front of about three hundred and ten people, and a man of twenty-four with a slate wrote *the next time a working is proposed somebody will say a gate crew voted against it* and made him be entered with a reason.

He has not said the four words once since the twenty-fifth day of the month. About nine people in this city have worked that out and about four of them have said so in a market, and the reason is in the ledger and it is one line: **a man who voted against a working and whose name is in a book is a man who will be asked, in a market, why he stands under a gate saying words he voted against, and no notary in this city can enter an answer to that question that is worth anything to him.**

He said them at their ordinary volume, out of the line and not together, with nineteen other men of the day crew, at the sixth hour of the morning of the twenty-first day of the new month, and he did not say them better than anybody and he did not say them louder, and about nine people in that gallery noticed and about four of them have said since that they did not notice until the following day, and Renna Ord has entered that she noticed and that she said nothing, and why, and the reason is: **because if I had said anything then it would have been because it mattered to me and it does not matter to me, it matters to him, and a woman's noticing a man's first time saying a thing is the oldest trick there is and I am not doing it.**

Nobody asked him to. Nobody has asked him anything since the twenty-fifth day of the month, and he was asked to vote about a working on the same day, in a yard, in front of three hundred and ten people, and his name is in a book twice, and both of the entries are him.

---

**The ask was made at about the eleventh hour of the morning of the twenty-first day of the new month in the Salt Weavers' chapel above the river market, in front of about two hundred and forty people, and the crew of forty were not in that room**, and about nine people in that room said afterwards that they had known the crew was not in that room while they were being asked, and about four of the nine said they had known it while they were putting the ask together.

The Salt Weavers' chapel is a disused guild chapel two floors above the market in the same building as the loft, and Sesh Brack has the key and gave it to the seven of them on condition that they take the plaster off the north wall at the end of it, and the plaster is still on, and about four people in that room on the twenty-first day of the new month were looking at the north wall while a man from another world read out a price.

He read it out. It took about eleven minutes and it is in the book, and it is the first cost column anybody has read out in this city that was not about a season of a field, and the reading of it is in Tamsin Quill's translation and not in his own words, because the terms were hers and Renna Ord's and not his, and that is entered.

**What the first version was.** That nineteen men of the day crew and twenty-one of the flood crew go under the near gate twice a day, at the ordinary volume, out of the line and not together, on every tide of the flood season and once a month after it, until the join in the main breach is shut; that four named persons inspect the near gate and the pale edge before every tide at a falling tide with a gauge, in whatever weather there is, and if the gauge reads what it read on the twenty-seventh day of the month then the tide is not worked and the entry says the four of them turned round; that the cost be entered before the tide turns and not after; that the crew be paid the ordinary flood-creel wage and the ordinary shift rate; and that this continues while the join stands and does not stop when the join is not used.

**And the crew said no at about the sixth hour of the evening of the twenty-first day of the new month under the near gate**, which is the dockers' inner working gate in the lock tunnels, a black timber and iron slab about nine feet from the join in the gate, where forty men of this city's own crew stand twice a day and say four words at their ordinary volume, out of the line and not together, by a promise a docker in a felt hat made in a ledger on the eighteenth day of the month and had read back four times, and the reason for the promise is that a chant is a thing a crowd did and a thing a crowd did can be quoted by anybody for nine years. It took about nine minutes and nobody argued and Adrian Vale did not answer it, and Vail entered it as a count and the count is entered with the number in the room, which is **forty-one**, because Hesk was on the sill and had come down, and Renna Ord was there and Nell Bray was there and Peth Lomas was there because he is a mason and masons are not a crew and cannot be counted either way and said so before anybody asked.

**The reason, and it is in the book four times over in four people's own words and it is the same eleven words, and it is Renna Ord's rule and not the crew's:**

> "**A thing nobody has asked for cannot be owed, and a thing that cannot be owed cannot be paid, and a thing that cannot be paid is a tax.**
>
> "That is not mine. That is older than me. You ask a man to come, and if he comes you owe him, and if you do not pay you do not get to ask him again for nine days. That is the whole of a crew and it has never once been written down anywhere and it is not going to be written down now.
>
> "And nobody asked us. A man stood in a chapel above a market this morning at the eleventh hour and read out what we do twice a day, and he read it well, and he read the cost, and he read the wage, and not one of us was in that room.
>
> "**So it is not a shift. It is an order. And I have told a man in a good coat and a mason and a boy of nineteen and about nine other people in five weeks that a list of people with no shift attached to it is an order, and I meant it, and this is one.**"

And Hesk came down off the sill at that point and said the other eleven words, and this is the second of the four and it is the reason the batch's smallest good deed is about to be used, and Adrian Vale entered it, and a girl of seventeen took it down, and the girl of seventeen is the clerk who took a surname in full across two lines of a narrow column on the thirty-ninth day of the month and was entered as a fact about a column.

> "I voted against it on the twenty-fifth day and he put my name in a book in front of three hundred and ten people and there are four hundred people in this city who know my name is in that book twice, once as a vote against and once as a man with a reason, and I have never been asked a question since.
>
> "**Nobody in this city has ever asked me anything.** Not him. Not the docker. Not the woman with the bowl. Thirty-eight days I have stood under this gate and been a name in a book and not one person has asked me whether I still hold the second half of what I said on the twenty-fifth.
>
> "So ask me."

---

**And then Renna Ord said what she has been waiting five weeks to say, and she said it under the near gate at about the seventh hour of the evening of the twenty-first day of the new month, in about four minutes, and it is in the book in her own words and it took two attempts.**

"**Then it has to be a shift and not an order, and I will tell you exactly what that means and it is not a small thing and none of you are going to like it.**

"A shift is a thing the city owes. Not him — **not a man, not a private debtor, not a field, not him.** The city. And the city pays it, every tide, to every man, in the ordinary rate, on a roll with the men's names on it so that the men can be counted and a man can say *I have not been paid* and be believed, and a man who has not been paid does not come the next tide and there is no entry and no fault and nobody goes to anybody's door.

"And that is the whole of a crew and it is what the six of you have not understood for forty days: **the owing is not the fairness, the owing is the mechanism.** You do not stand under that gate at six because it is right. You stand under it at six because somebody owes you and you have not been paid yet, and that is the oldest arithmetic in this city and it is the only reason it works.

"And the second half, and this is the part I want in a book in a room and not in a market: **the roll has no name at the top.** Not him, not me, not the foreman, not the keeper. Forty names and nobody's name above them and a column of what it is for, like the yard, like the five words, like the thing a mason said is a widening and not a rota. And anybody in this city can be on it and nobody in this city can be on the top of it, and **if my name goes on the top of it I will read it out in a yard.**

"And the third thing and it is the one that is going to cost him and he knows it before I say it: **the roll is owed by the city, and the city's credit in this fortnight is a field four miles away and a woman in Corrow and a village of nine households that said no.**

"So here is the whole price in one sentence and I have been five weeks getting to it: **the day the bottom field is not paid in March, this stops, and the join stops being worked, and the join will be where it is.** And I am not going to soften that for a room of two hundred and forty people on Saturday and I am not going to be the one who is surprised about it."

And there were about forty people under that gate and the tide was on the ebb and nobody said anything for about eleven seconds, and Vask has entered that the eleven seconds is the number and that she counted it, and that she has counted silences in this city since the third day of the month and has never written one down before.

"**You do not get a door that is not somebody's field**," Renna Ord said. "That is the price of a door in this city. The price is the crew. And the crew is the wall."

---

They said yes at about the eighth hour of the evening of the twenty-first day of the new month, under the near gate, in about eleven minutes, and the count is entered and the count is **thirty-seven for, three against, one abstention**, and the three against are entered by name because the whole of this book is built on the fact that a man who votes against a working in this city gets his name entered, and Vail entered all three without asking them to agree to it, and the foreman's name is not among the three.

**The three against are Hesk, and Ovin Sarr, and a gate-hand of about fifty called Ferris**, and Ferris said his reason out loud and it is the shortest reason in the book: **"I have been asked and I have not been asked before and I do not know the difference yet, and I would rather say no for a week and be asked again than say yes for good."**

And Hesk's reason is: "**I said the four words this morning at six and I have never said them in my life and I said them because I am the foreman of this crew and not because anybody asked me, and eleven hours later a man stood in a chapel and read out what I do twice a day and not one of us was in the room.

"So: no. I am not going to be the man who says the words and then says yes in the same evening to make a count look better. Ask me again next week and I will answer and if the answer is yes then I will have been asked and the difference between that and this is the whole of it and I am not going to pretend it is a small difference, and I want my vote entered as what it was and I want the four words entered as this morning.**"

And Ovin Sarr's reason is the four words he said in a yard on the eighteenth day of the new month, about a field and a day's work, and the crowd in that room at about the seventh hour of the evening did not make a noise, and Vail has entered that it did not make a noise, and that she has entered that about nine people in that room were surprised by a room not making a noise and that she is one of the nine.

Hesk said the four words at about the sixth hour of the morning of the twenty-first day of the new month, before anybody had asked him for anything, and he has never said them once before and he did not say them better than anybody and he did not say them louder, and the count of thirty-seven for and three against was taken fourteen hours after he said them, and Vail has entered that sequence on the same page, and has entered that she has never in twenty-six years entered a sequence and has entered this one because a foreman who says the words and then votes against the same thing in the same day is the only occurrence in the history of this book and somebody is going to need to be able to check it.

**The conditions went in on the night of the twenty-first day of the new month in a room above a market, and there were six of them, and they are the terms of the trial instrument and they are entered in the fourth volume and they are read back at the first light of the twenty-second day of the new month in front of about two hundred and forty people.**

**One.** No single person may open it and no single person may suspend it, and there is no opening in it and no hour and no man in it whose business it is, and forty men turn up or it does not happen.

**Two.** Four named persons inspect the near gate and the pale edge before every tide, at a falling tide, with a gauge, in whatever weather there is, and if the gauge reads what it read on the twenty-seventh day of the month the tide is not worked and the only entry is that the four turned round.

**Three.** The standing is reviewed at the end of the flood season, in daylight, in a room, with a date on the page before the day it is taken.

**Four.** No day's work may be added to it without somebody asking the crew first, in a room, in words, and the asking is entered with the answer.

**Five.** A roll is kept with the men's names on it and **no name at the top of it**, and Vail will strike out any name put at the top, and the wages are owed by the city and not by a man, and a man who has not been paid does not come and there is no entry and no fault and nobody goes to anybody's door.

**Six.** Nobody whose standing comes from the holder of the pull may open it, inspect it, sign it, or suspend it, **and the holder of the pull is not on the roll of people who may open it, and has asked to be struck out of it.**

And the sixth condition took two hours and ten minutes to get accepted and the two hours and ten minutes are in the book with the hour and the reasons, and the reason is in about nine sentences said by about nine different people and every one of the nine reasons is the same reason and it is this: **that a man asking to be taken out of a room is a man building something, and this city has been watching a man build things for forty days, and about nine people in a room above a market have not been able to tell the difference between a man building a thing and a man building a martyr, and the difference is the page.**

He could not say it. He has written that down in a notebook and it is four lines: **I have said the true reason in this room on Wednesday and in a yard on the seventeenth and I have said the true reason in three rooms in five weeks and every time somebody has found the fair reason and used it, and and, and the fair reason is always better than the true one in a room, because a room can use it. I have been the fair reason for five weeks. Somebody else has to find mine.**

Tamsin Quill did not say it. She has entered that she was asked twice and declined twice and that the reason is her own, and the reason is: **that I said the true one in a hall of three hundred and fifty people on the thirty-ninth day of the month and about nine of them wrote it down and none of them used it, and I have spent five days finding out that a sentence in a room is not a wall, and I am not going to spend another five days.**

**Priya Shah said it, and she is twenty-four, and she has a slate, and she has been writing down what a person said for nineteen days.**

"Then write down what happens to it if you are hit by a cart on the Reach road on Thursday," she said, "and stop telling this room what it means.

"**A bar on a man is a thing this city can only obey while the man is standing in the room.** You have all been arguing for two hours about whether he means it, and he means it, and meaning it is not a term. In nine months you will all be gone from this room and one of you will be the one standing in it, and every one of you will be able to find a document that says the standing person cannot open this, and in about four years a man will stand in a room with a document and a good reason and a lawyer from Aurel and the document will be nine pages long and correct.

"What I am asking for is a page that says it, in his handwriting, with a date on it, and the page does not care what he meant. **I do not care what he meant. I am asking for the paper.**"

And it was accepted in about forty seconds, and it is entered, and Priya Shah asked for three things and got all three: that it be read back at the first light in a room of about two hundred and forty people; that it be entered that the sentence that produced the condition was said by a woman of twenty-four and not by the man it is about; and this, in her own words, which Roke read out in a yard on the Saturday morning and which is on the page:

> **And that he did not think of it in two hours and that I have been in a room with him for forty days and he does not think of things that are about his own death, and that is a fault in a man and not a virtue, and I would like it entered as a fault.**

At about the eleventh hour of the night of the twenty-first day of the new month, in a room above a market, about a minute after the sixth condition was entered, a pale field came up in the air in front of Adrian Vale in the colour of a paper form and stood there for about nineteen seconds, and about nine people saw it happen, and about four of the nine were the crew.

> **A PERSON STRUCK OUT OF A ROSTER MAY BE ENTERED AGAIN BY A PERSON WHO REMAINS ON IT.**
>
> **The person who enters him is answerable for him.**

"No," said Adrian Vale, out loud, in English, in a room of about nine people.

"Say it slowly and say what it is," said Tamsin Quill, four feet away.

"It is the fifth clause of this afternoon turned inside out and it arrived about a minute after it was written down. Look at it. **The whole of the sixth condition is that no single person can do it, and this says a single person can do it, and then it says the person is answerable, which is the Crown's construction and not mine.** A position may be filled by a person who can hold it. That is item four of a notice from the twenty-eighth day of the month, read aloud in a hall of four hundred and ten people on the thirty-first, and there is a clerk from Aurel sitting in this building who has entered every one of these in a file at Aurel because it is his office to enter things.

"**Answerable is not the opposite of possible. Answerable is a smaller word and it is the one this world has been using instead of the other one for eight hundred years.** If a thing cannot be done, it cannot be done, and a document saying so is a wall. If a thing can be done by a person who will answer for it, then it can be done, and there is a lawyer in Aurel and a quarterly sitting and a stipend, and I have watched ninety people in a yard put their hands up for that shape twice in two days and be right about the money.

"There is no name in that field. It did not say *the holder of the pull*. It said *a person who remains on it*, and that is the fifteenth thing this thing has said to me and it is the first one that has named a **process** instead of a person, and that is worse, because a person can be refused in a yard and a process cannot be refused in a yard, it can only be filled in."

And Renner Sallow, who was in the room, said, "It is now the eighth," and Adrian Vale said, "I am aware, and thank you, and I would like it entered that a clerk from that office has been counting these in a book at Aurel since the eighth day of the month and has never once been asked to stop, and that this city has not convicted him of anything and is not going to, and that the eighth one has his number on it."

**The loft at about the second hour of the morning of the twenty-second day of the new month.**

There were about nine people in it and the room has a stove that does not draw and thirty-one names on a wall in four columns, and the fourth column is what the work is for and not what the person is for on a notary's ruling from the eleventh day of the month, and there is a ruled line under one name and a date in a woman's hand and nothing at all in the fourth column beside it, and the fourth column has been empty for eleven days and is going to be empty for eleven days and nobody in that room has filled it in and nobody in that room has said so.

**It is the ninth day of eleven.** It comes off on the fiftieth at the earliest and four people agreed to say nothing about it to anybody for eleven days, and eleven is a figure of speech and is entered as a figure of speech.

The wall was being read wrongly by about nine people and not one of the nine said so, and what they were reading, in a room with a stove that does not draw, at about the second hour of the morning of a Saturday in a city of eleven hundred people, is this:

**That the group is finishing.** A man of thirty-one from Leeds, of the second list, said it first, to nobody in particular, and about four people agreed with him within a minute, and the agreement was not said out loud. A woman of sixteen asked whether the second group would be asked to help with the terms, and about nine people in that room heard the question as a question about who is left, and there were five of the seven and eleven of the other list and eight of the eleven have no address and one has a scar.

**That the work of the name with the line under it has gone to the lock crews.** A docker of the day crew said it to a woman of twenty-four at about the half hour, in a sentence about the morning's work, and the woman wrote it down in her log, and the sentence was *and the rest of them are at the lock now anyway*, and it was about the ordinary work of nineteen men and twenty-one and it was true, and it was not what was on the wall.

**That a person is going to be asked to be the man in charge of it.** Two people said that one, separately, and neither of them said it to the other, and the reason is that about nine people in this city have watched a man in a good coat write *I expect you to do as I ask* in chalk on a keeper's door in the fourteenth week of this flood and then take it down himself in daylight, and a number of them have noticed that the two sentences about the thing everybody actually wants are on a door and not in a book.

Nobody in that room pointed at the fourth column. Nobody in that room said the word *column*.

And at about the second hour and a half of the morning of the twenty-second day of the new month Adrian Vale came down the outside stair of that building with a school exercise book under his arm and stood in front of that wall for about four minutes and read thirty-one names and one ruled line and one empty column, and he did not fill it in, and he did not say anything about it, and he wrote in the exercise book, in his own hand, on a page with somebody else's name on the cover:

> *The rota is not a will. It is a list of what a person came here to be asked for, and one of the entries is empty and it is going to be empty until the fiftieth, and there are about nine people in this city reading that empty space as a decision, and the decision they are reading is that we are finishing, and the reason they are reading it is that a rule about a person is the only kind of rule anybody has ever wanted in this city, and we have had one for eleven days and they have got used to it.*
>
> *I have not said so. Four people agreed to say nothing for eleven days and I have been one of the four and I did not think about what the other twenty-seven people were doing with an empty column, and I have written this down because I did not think of it, and because a woman of twenty-four asked me on Friday night to write down what happens to the standing if he is hit by a cart, and this is the same question with a different person in it, and she asked it about a page and I am asking it about a wall, and the answer is the same, and it is that the wall does not care what I meant.*
