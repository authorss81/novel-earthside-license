# Chapter 0009 — Seal or Spend

The second tide came in at four in the morning and the argument about the outer tear had been going since ten the previous night, and by four it had the quality of a thing that has been worn smooth by being carried.

They had all of them on the walk-round, or on the wall above it, or in the water at its foot: nine Veyrans, the Hearthguard out of the outer ward, four of the dockers, Vask and two of her masons, Renna Ord with a lantern, and all six of the people who had come out of the wall on Tuesday, plus Adrian.

The rain had stopped. The river was making the noise it made, which Adrian had learned to read in the way a man reads a badly kept road.

He had not slept. He had done four things with the hours instead.

He had walked the outer channel twice with Vask, both times on the walk-round, both times with a lamp held low and a line he had paid out and measured, because you cannot survey a moving river with an opinion.

He had compared the pressure at nine points in the lock at three different hours, and had it in the notebook as a table, and had found that between the first night and now the loss had gone from a foot an hour to two and a half.

He had listened to the voice four more times.

And he had been asked, personally, by four separate people, whether he could open the way back.

"Owen's been up on that wall twice since midnight," Tamsin said, low, beside him. "Both times I sent him down. Both times he came down. I would like you to notice that he came down."

"I noticed."

"He is not going to keep coming down. I am telling you that now so that later you cannot say nobody told you."

Below them, the water in the channel had started to climb the wall in that thin leaning sheet again, and where it leaned the stone was dark and sweating, and the join under the third bay — the one Adrian had found with a chalk stub on the first morning, the one he had pushed the whole weight of the lock into on purpose — was making a sound like a knuckle being flexed.

"Do it," said Roke.

He was not a man who needed persuading. He had come down the walk-round with a bar over his shoulder and a slate under his arm and the air of a man who has made up his mind and would be delighted to be argued with for a while.

"I am not against it," he said, before anyone had got going. "I want it said that I am not against it. That thing opened a hole in a wall we maintain, and something is standing in it taking the river, and there are eleven villages at the end of this water. I would like it shut. I would like it shut before I have to write a name in my hand on a piece of paper saying that a season of the bottom field is gone because a man from the other side of the world thought it was worth keeping."

"That is the first sensible thing anybody has said for ten hours," said Tamsin.

"It is the *only* sensible thing anybody has said for ten hours. Everything else has been *he might come back* and *it might be a way out* and *somebody in there is calling for help*, and every one of those is a reason to be careful, and none of them is a reason to spend eleven villages on." He shifted the bar. "The Concord's own table has a name on it for a proposal to move the boundary. Whose name is on it, Quill?"

"Mine," said Tamsin.

"I know whose name is on it. I am asking in front of him because in nine years' time somebody will say it was the stranger's fault, and I intend that it was not."

She said nothing to that for a moment. Then she said, "No. It will not be the stranger's fault. It will also not be my fault, and I have not slept either. Those are two different sentences and I would like both of them in whatever gets written."

---

Ivenn Marrow had come down at midnight and had been standing at the top of the walk-round ever since, in a dry coat, saying nothing, which was the most unnerving thing he had ever done.

He had one argument and he made it once, and then he let it sit.

"Do not seal it," he said. "I am aware of how that sounds. I am aware of the eleven villages and I have read the same table Quill has read. But look at what you would actually be doing. You have a working stone in a gate and a man inside it, and you have had four days in which to learn his name, and you have learned nothing, and the reason you have learned nothing is that you have spent four days writing down what is *wrong* with him instead of asking him a question." He turned round to face them. "I spent six years of my life in archives telling frightened men that there was a person on the other side of a document. Some of those men were signatories. Some of them were in prison. I am telling you that this is a man, and that you are about to nail a lid on him."

"Nobody has asked you to nail anything," Roke said.

"I have asked you to consider it, and you have not, and in two days' time you will have, and I would like the record to show that the alternative was said out loud by somebody." He looked at Adrian. "I am not asking you to leave him open, Vale. I have no authority and I know it. I am asking you to accept that closing him and leaving him are both things that will be done to a person, and that at least one of them will be done on your say-so."

"He used my name and I let it happen," Adrian said.

"I know. It is in the slate. I wrote it out for the notary this afternoon because I thought it should be in the record in more than one hand." He said it without any weight at all, which was somehow the most frightening thing about him. "That is the only reason I am still on this wall."

---

The choice, when it finally got stated, was stated by a mason.

Ottoline Vask came up the walk-round from the foot of the wall at ten past four, soaked to the thigh, and got the two of them out of the way and crouched in the lee of the parapet and said it in the local language, and made Tamsin repeat it, and made her repeat it *slowly*, and did not stop until she had it right.

"There is a way to close that join that five of us can hold," she said. "Quorum. Five, standing in it, saying the oath for the gate, hands on the stone. It will take the whole night. It will hold for a season, maybe two. And when it lets go it will let go all at once, and it will be the same tear, and the second time it will be worse, because the first time it will have been a working and the second time it will be a scar, and scars do not close."

"And the other way?"

"Leave the seam." She said the word carefully. "Close the join enough that the water goes back where it belongs, and *do not* close the stone. Leave a socket where the join was, three fingers wide, in the wall of the outer channel, under the cap of the old landing. It is not a door. It is a *shape*. A shape a door could one day be built into, by people with a site and a witness and a community that has agreed to receive somebody."

"Could it be used? Tonight? Tomorrow? By me? By any of us?"

"No."

"Then say it in the yard where the notary can hear it," Roke said. "Say that the shape does not open and does not carry anybody and does not bring anybody home, because I have six people up there who have been asking me a question since Tuesday and I am not carrying it another day."

Vask said it again, to the wall, and then Adrian made her say it in English, and she did, word for word, without any softening, and Owen Park sat down on the wet stone with his back against the parapet and did not say anything for a long time.

"Both of them cost the field," Adrian said.

"Both of them cost the field."

"Because the water has to go somewhere and it has been going down there for four days on somebody's authority, and when we stop that, the bottom field takes the whole of it at once."

"Yes."

"How much?"

"No."

"Give me a number."

"No," said Vask. "That is the first thing I will not do for you tonight. If I give you a number, it is a number a man says in a night like this, and in six weeks a clerk will put it in a book and it will be the number." She stood up. "Ask the farmer. Bring me a farmer."

---

Nobody slept, and it went like a working.

That is the honest word for it and Adrian used it afterwards, when it was over, and he did not care in the slightest that it made him sound like a man who had spent his life designing software.

Five witnesses: Vask, Tamsin, Roke, Ovin Sarr, and Nell Bray. The two dockers had spent two days holding a join in a flood and a man from the wall had not known their names on the first morning; Roke had put them on the slate that afternoon because Adrian had asked him to, and here they were.

They went out along the walk-round at twenty past four, in the dark, with three lanterns, and Renna Ord on the wall with a line round her waist, and the two masons in the channel below with oak bars.

And Adrian found the join.

That was his job, and it was the last time for a long time that the job was his alone.

He had the line — he could not choose to have it, but he could stand in the right place and it would come. For two days it had only come while somebody else was standing in a working of their own, and it had gone the instant they let go. Tonight it came with nobody owing it to anybody: no arch, no oath, no masons on a promise. It came the whole way up the outer wall into his feet like a wire drawn through a heel, and it was *cold*, and it was *thin*, and it had a place in it where the cold stopped being cold and became something else.

He told them where to put their hands.

He said, "Not there. Two feet left. And lower, and no — lower, that is right, that is the stone, do not take the mortar, take the stone, take the part that is older than the mortar, and if your hand is going to be on it in four hours then it goes on *that*."

Ovin said, in the local language, "He can see it."

"He can see it," Vask said. "Hold your hand where he says and do not look at him while you do it, because a man who is being watched while he holds a promise is a man who will let go."

He was the one who made it legible. That is what happened. He did not hold the working and he could not, and he was not pretending, but in the dark, on a wall, with a river going somewhere it should not, the thing that made the five of them able to do it at all was a man who could not do any of it saying out loud, in a language they did not have, *there, and there, and not there.*

For about two hours it was the best he had ever been at anything.

---

It went wrong at ten past six, and it went wrong because he was watching it.

There was a noise from below — Vask's masons, in the channel, one of them shouting — and Adrian turned his head.

He turned his head for about a second and a half, and in that second and a half the join he had been standing in — the thin cold wire of a thing that was being held by five people on a wall — went *blind*.

Not dim. Blind. As though somebody had put a hand over a lamp.

He got a fix on the three nearest hands and he could see where they were and he could not see the pattern, and the cold in his feet came up into his knees and then into his back, and his left ear, which had not stopped ringing once since the chapel, went up to a thin high sound like a wire under tension.

"Vale," said Tamsin, from some distance. "Vale. *Vale.*"

And he did the thing that he would be thinking about, on and off, for the rest of his life.

He said, out loud, in English, in the dark, on a wall above a river: "I am holding it. I have got it. Keep your hands where they are and I have got it—"

And he reached for it.

There is no other way to describe what he did. He did not have any technique. He put his attention on the place where the join had been and he *leaned*, the way you lean on a door you are about to take the weight of, and he made, in the privacy of his own head, a direct promise: *I am holding this and I will hold it until it is closed and nobody will need me again for this.*

Nothing happened.

Then everything happened.

It came back through him from the stone, not into his head — into his *body*, up the outside of his legs, through his back, and the cold in it was not cold, it was the specific absence that his own office had spent six years learning to name in a design review, and it was the exact, absolute, technical null of a thing that has been promised by nobody to nobody, and it landed in the middle of his back and folded him.

He went down on the stones on the walk-round. He was not aware of hitting them. He was aware of the rain starting again on the back of his neck, and of somebody screaming his name in two languages, and of blood coming out of his left ear and running down the side of his jaw and off onto the parapet stone, and of the fact that he could not see the join at all any more and had not been able to for a moment, and that he had no idea whatever whether he ever would be able to again.

And the last thing he was aware of, before he went out, was a man in a dry coat on the wall above him, sitting down slowly on the stones with his hands in his lap, watching, and not being surprised, and not being sorry either, but writing something down in a small book with the other hand.

---

He came back in about ninety seconds, on his back, on the wet stones, with four people over him and a fifth holding his head off the ground.

"Ninety seconds," Mina said, from a long way off. "Ninety seconds is what you were out. Adrian. What did you do?"

"I took it," he said. "I tried to take it off them."

"Which of them?"

"All of them."

There was a silence on that wall long enough for the water to make a whole decision, and Adrian understood later that four separate people would describe it, and that none of them would agree about anything else.

"How," said Roke, very quietly. "How did you try."

"I said a promise. To nobody. I promised it to the *stone*." He got an elbow under himself and got himself onto one side, which took a while, and the world tilted and stayed tilted. "There's no such thing. I know there's no such thing. I knew it while I was doing it. I did it anyway, because I had been the only one who could see it for two hours and I got *used* to that, and by six o'clock in the morning I had started to think of it as mine."

Nell Bray, who had been one of the five witnesses and who had one hand on his shoulder, said something in the local language, and Tamsin translated it in a completely flat voice.

"She says: get up. Not you. *Them.* She says you are in the way of five people doing a job."

He got up.

---

It took another four hours.

He sat on the wall above the walk-round for all four of them, in the rain, with a cloth held to his face, and he did not say one useful word, because there was nothing he had that they needed, and at about half past ten Tamsin came and crouched beside him and said, without any preamble at all:

"It is finished. Come and look at it, because you are going to want to see it and I would rather you saw it with me than found out from the slate."

They went down together. The outer wall of the channel, low down where the water had been leaning for four days, was no longer open. It was shut — not patched, not mortared over, *shut*, in the sense that there was a seam in it now, a real seam, three fingers wide and eighteen inches long and dead straight, with a lip at either end.

The stone either side of it was dry.

It had been sweating for four days and it was dry.

"That is the socket," Vask said. "That is what a socket looks like. It is not a door. It is the place a door would be. In a year, in ten years, if this city ever wants a door here, this is the stone it goes in. And it will need five witnesses and a site and a community that has agreed to receive somebody, and none of those exist, and it will not be used."

"No," Adrian said.

"No. You may touch it. It is not a working. It is a join that has been closed on purpose, and what is left of it is a shape."

He put his hand on it.

The stone was warm. Not hot. The same warm as a fresh stamp, the exact warm of the mark on the heel of his palm, and when he turned his hand over and laid the heel of it against the seam it fitted — not the way a key fits a lock, but the way a coin fits the die it was struck from.

Smaller. A great deal smaller. The seam was three fingers across and eighteen inches long. The mark on his palm was the size of a thumbnail. But every part of the branching was in the same proportion in both of them: the fork at the base, the short spur, the place where the line thinned and stopped. He held his thumbnail against the lip at one end of the seam and it covered the lip exactly, and when he took it away the hairline of the joint was still on the pad of his thumb.

He stood there in the rain with his palm flat on the wall of a lock in a world he had not chosen and understood, with a feeling he could only describe later as *the floor of his stomach going out*, that this was not a scar that had been closed on purpose.

This was a socket that a piece of him had been cut to fit, at one-twentieth, and had been carrying in the place where he could not wash it off since twenty to three on Tuesday morning.

And the mark was not on his hand any more, exactly. He turned the palm over in the rain and the pale branching line was fainter than it had been on Tuesday, and in the middle where the forks met there was now a small clean absence, like a chip out of a tooth.

The thin black line was a separate thing, and it was not part of the mark. It ran along the long axis of the heel under the place where the pale line had opened its cut two nights ago, a hair's width across and about two inches long, and it did not thin or fade or warm up when the pale line faded. It was still there. He put his thumb over it and it was still there, exactly the same width, and when he took the thumb away it had not moved.

Vask had told him on Wednesday night what a black line meant, and he had written the definition down in the back of the notebook like a man writing down a rule about traffic. *The promise was made and nobody took it.* Two days before that, in a market aisle, he had watched an old woman put her hand flat on a perished tie and say four words, and had watched a sensible man in a good coat put his tool away because of them. He had been in the city a little over two days and had already been shown the same lesson twice in two different clothes.

He had made one promise in that room with nobody standing in it, and it had cost him two black lines: one in a slab that every person in this city steps over, and one under his own thumb that nobody will ever step over.

He is not carrying the fragment in a pocket. He is carrying it on his hand. It has been in his hand since 2:40 on Tuesday morning and it is in his hand now, and it is smaller, and the shape of the seam is its exact negative at one-twentieth, and there is nothing in this world he can do with that except eventually stand in a public room and let somebody else put it in a stone while he holds a line they can see.

---

Sera Vail came up at two in the afternoon with a lantern and a slate and a leather case, and she was fifty and she was a Concord notary and she was the most frightening person Adrian had met in Veyra, because she had no interest in him whatsoever.

"Vale," she said. "You are not under arrest, and you are not a guest, and I am not here about you. I am here about the field."

She put the slate on a baulk, and she wrote while she talked, and she made him say it back to her three times, and she made him say it to Tamsin, and she made Tamsin say it back to her.

"State the number."

"One season. The bottom field loses a season of stability. The Reed margin takes the worst of it and the eleven villages downstream lose their spring grazing."

"Not a *year*. Say the word. A year is what people say when they have rounded up so that it hurts less."

"A season," Adrian said. "The field loses a season. The Reed margin takes the worst of it."

"Name the cause."

He had known this was coming since the previous night. He had thought about it for two hours this morning while lying on a wall with blood on his chin.

"The cause is the join in the outer channel, which stood open from the hour it tore until the hour it was closed by five witnesses at about half past ten this morning. The cause is not the man from the wall. The cause is recorded in your own table, on the line with Quill's name on it, which says the same thing has been attempted before and every attempt cost the same."

Vail wrote for a while. Then she said, without looking up, "That is a careful sentence. Quill will want to argue with the middle of it. I will allow her to argue with the middle of it in writing and I will attach her objection to the same page."

"Thank you."

"Do not thank me. I am going to make you a creditor." She turned the slate round. "You are entered as holding a debt to the Lock of Salt, and the debt is a season of the bottom field, and it is owed to eleven villages and to the Reed margin, and it is *enforceable*, and any person in this city may come to the Concord and ask what the state of it is. I have written a review date. It is the last day of the salt month. On that day somebody will ask you three questions and you will answer them in front of the same people you are answering today."

"And if I can't pay it?"

"Then the record says you could not pay it, and the record says why, and the eleven villages still have a season." She dipped the chalk. "Which is the arrangement. That is what this city is. It is not that people are forgiven. It is that the account is kept in public."

She wrote his name. She did not ask him to sign, because he could not, and she said so without any apology: "You will make your mark beside it, with your thumb, in the place I show you, and Quill will witness, and it is not a signature and it is not pretty and it is not going away."

He put his thumb in the wax and put it on the slate and his hand was steady, which he found embarrassing.

Then she said, to nobody in particular, while she squared her case: "And the main breach."

The whole wall went quiet.

"The main breach in the gate stands open and watched," Vail said. "It is not closed. It is not going to be closed this month, and it is not going to be closed by you, and I am entering that in the record as a fact rather than a failure so that nobody in ten years' time can say it was attempted."

"Then what is going to close it?" Roke said.

"The hearing," said Vail. "At first light tomorrow, which is in about sixteen hours, the Concord will hear an answer to one question. Not what do we do about the gate. Whether the lock can be worked by more than one person, so that no one person can open it, hold it, close it, or refuse it alone. That is tomorrow. The breach is not tomorrow." She closed the case. "And it is still the case that nothing on this wall today has made the breach smaller. Do any of you understand that? You have had a very hard morning and a very long day and everybody in this city is going to be grateful to you, and the gate is exactly as open as it was at dawn."

Adrian said, "I know."

"You do not know, because you have not looked at it. Look at it in an hour."

He looked at it in an hour. It was exactly as open as it was at dawn. It was worse than at dawn, in one respect, which was that in daylight he could now see that the pale edge of the join in the gate had widened by perhaps the width of a finger since Tuesday, and that nobody had seen it widen, because nobody had stood still in front of it for two days.

He wrote that down in the notebook that night, on the same page as the other list, and he wrote the date and the hour, and he wrote underneath it in the flat hand:

*Not solved. Held. The main breach is bigger than when I arrived and I have not told anybody yet, and I am telling them in a room at first light, and I will not get to be the one who found out.*

He read it over. Then he wrote, smaller:

*The route is empty. No return mark exists for me or for any of the six. The wall is a shape, not a door. Ask the people who maintain the gate.*

And he did not sleep, and at first light he went down to the Concord hall with Tamsin, and the door of the hall was already open, and there were two hundred people in it, and at the back, on a bench, in a good grey coat, with a slate on his knees, Ivenn Marrow had got there early.
