# Chapter 0016 — The Offer, Again

They had moved the meetings out of the loft itself on Friday, and the reason was Sesh Brack's and nobody else's: it was her building and the two lower rooms were her rooms, and she had said so in front of all six of them, in her own language, over a pot, and then she had gone up the second stair and unlocked a door and handed them the key to the Salt Weavers' chapel on the condition that they would take the plaster off the north wall at the end of it, because there was a window behind the plaster and the room needed air.

So the meeting was in a room two floors up with the wind shut out and the first real cold of the flood season in it, with seven people in it who had not had a conversation that was not about somebody's injury since Tuesday.

Nyla Ferrow had come, which Adrian had not expected and which changed the evening, because a keeper in a room makes it a room. Renna Ord had come, because Renna Ord now came to things. Hesper Saye had come because Owen asked her in English and she was extremely pleased about it and brought a bowl.

Ivenn Marrow came at eight and was the last one in and the best-dressed and he had a slate, and he sat down with it on his knees like a man at a service.

---

"Before we start," Adrian said, "I want to say the thing I am not able to say, and then I want six people to say six other things, and none of them are mine."

"Go on," said Mina.

"Nine days ago a man showed me a shape with three facts in it and no cost. I said no. I have spent every day since then telling people I said no because the offer was cheap, and I have found out in the last two days that I said no for a reason I did not have, which means that for eight days I have been *right by accident*, and I do not want to be right by accident again." He put his hands flat on the table. "So tonight I want to say out loud, in a room, in front of people who are not me, exactly what a workway is and exactly what it costs, and then I want every one of you to be asked, separately, in your own words, whether you would take it, and I am not going to say anything in between."

"That is an unusual meeting," said Marrow, pleasantly.

"That is a meeting where the price is legible," said Adrian. "I have spent a week finding out that is the only thing that makes anything survivable. So: Marrow, read the omitted cost out loud. You have had it in your mouth since Wednesday evening and you have been waiting for a room. This is the room."

Marrow looked at him for about two seconds, and then he took the slate off his knee, and he did it.

---

"First the thing itself, and I will be exact, because you have asked for exact and I am not going to insult you by being approximate." Marrow read it as though it were a passage out of a book, which it was. "A workway is a permission profile. It is not a skill and it teaches nothing and it makes nobody strong. It tells one branch of a working *whose work you will be doing*, and from that moment every person affected by that branch is entitled to expect it of you. In your city you would call it a class. Locals call it a workway and I have come to think that they are right about the order of the words.

"Wayfinder. *To read a road is to carry its name.* A wayfinder may not leave a road unnamed while holding it. Benefit: you will be able to read a route's recent choices, and find a safe way through difficult ground, which in this city is worth more than anything else you will ever be offered, because nothing else in Veyra is difficult for you in a way you can measure.

"Limit and cost, and I am going to say this slowly because you asked and because three people in this room have written down that I have a habit of omitting it. The wayfinder must personally walk or bear witness to a route. It cannot move you anywhere. It cannot open a thing or close a thing or hold a place. And the cost — this is the part nobody has ever written down, in nine years of my reading of this trade — is that repeated route work taxes memory and balance, and it takes the road's name into the place where you keep your name, and a man who has carried a road badly in that place is a man who has made a place he lives in worse by looking at it, and there is no ceremony for that, and no one comes and tells you about it, and it is not a punishment. It is an *erosion*."

"Nobody steps over your hand," said Hesper Saye, to nobody, in English, with enormous satisfaction.

"Correct," said Marrow, and went on, "and there is a second cost to this specific offer, which I have never put in a slate before and which I am putting in one now, at my own expense, without being asked, in a room with a keeper in it.

"The stone below the chapel is out of its place. Vask and Quill and the man in the book have established this week that lifting it and replacing it is not a repair; it is a decision about where the river's edge is written, and the last time that decision was taken it put four hundred people in new houses. If the workway is taken, then a wayfinder is the natural person to be asked to carry that stone back down to the water. And a wayfinder cannot refuse, because a workway's holder cannot leave a road unnamed while holding it. The obligation and the temptation are the same object. That is not an accident of the wording. That is the design." He put the slate on the table. "Which is why it is offered to you and not to the anchorwright, and I would be obliged to explain to you, Mr Quill, that this was deliberate, and you would be right to be angry about it."

"I am not angry at you," said Tamsin, in the local language, and did not translate it, and it took the room a second to work out that she had said it in the local language on purpose.

---

"May I say the other half of it," said Marrow, "and then I will sit down and be quiet for the rest of the evening."

"Please," Adrian said, and knew he should not have.

"The cost is worth paying. I want to say that clearly, because I have just spent four minutes making it sound expensive and I do not want a man in a room to think I have made it expensive so that he will not buy it. It is expensive. It is also the cheapest thing in this building, and I have been a Crown Law scholar for nineteen years and I have watched a great many good decisions made by men who were ruined by waiting for a cheaper one.

"Vale, you have been in this city ten days. In ten days you have a debt, a seat, a queue, a landlord, a doctor who does not speak your language, and a hole in a floor you cannot read, and none of it will still be true in a year. A workway is the one thing on offer in this world that does not expire. It is the one thing that a man of Aurel cannot take off you, because it is not a Crown permission and it is not in a Crown roll, and in four years' time when you are a name in somebody's argument about who owns a bridge, the one thing that will still be standing is the shape you have agreed to carry." He folded his hands. "And the second half, and I am sorry to say it to a man who has not asked me for it: you have no standing to refuse this on behalf of the six."

"They were not in the room when you made the offer," Adrian said.

"No."

"Then I have no standing to refuse it on their behalf, and you have no standing to offer it to them, and by the same rule I have no standing to refuse it for myself, and that is why this meeting exists." Adrian put his thumb on the table. "So it goes to six people and one keeper and one mason and it goes in that order, and I will not answer until they have, and if I answer first then I have made this for them and not for me and we will be where we were on Tuesday."

---

It went in that order, and Adrian had asked for it in that order, and he had not expected the *shape* of it, and he wrote afterwards that the shape of it was the only useful thing he learned that night and that he had not known in advance that there was a thing to be learned.

Noor Haddad went first, because Noor always went first, and she refused, and the refusal was the least interesting thing she said.

"No. And I am not going to give you a reason about roads, because I do not understand roads and I have been sick twice this week and I do not have the attention span for a mystery. No." She put her hands flat on the slate. "The thing I want to say is a question and I would like it written on a wall, and the question is: **who does the job?**

"Because a workway is a duty with a shape. And a duty with a shape is a job. And the moment it is a job somebody has to stop doing whatever he was doing before, or do it on top, and nobody in this room has asked a single person in this city to give that up. In Saltwake that is how it works. That is Renna's whole thing. You do not get to hand somebody a duty that displaces a duty without asking the man it lands on, and the man it lands on is not in this room, and he does not know this offer exists, and he is going to find out in about four months when the person he is currently working next to is sent off to read a road for somebody else." She looked round the table. "Nobody has asked him. That is my no. Ask him."

"Somebody write that down," said Tamsin, "because it is the best thing said in this city in a week, and it was said by a woman who does not speak the language."

Jonah Pike went second, and he refused, and gave the best speech anybody made that evening, and the best thing about it was that it was against himself and was not a performance.

"Yes," he said. "I want it. I want it and I am going to say exactly why, out loud, in front of five people, because I am not going to be a man in this room who says no and feels good about it.

"Reek of the Brass Measure gave me forty elms of bar iron, twenty of bar, a set of shoring oak and six weeks of rope on Friday last, delivered to the lock yard, at the price of a *priority on every working at the lock*, and Priya stopped it and Priya was right. Here is why she was right and here is why I want the thing. Because if I had a workway, I would not be a man from a wall asking a guild for iron. I would be a man with a *duty* in the road trade, and the brass man's carts go on roads, and a duty in a road trade is a *standing*, and a standing is a thing you can open a ledger with." He spread his hands. "And in about eleven months I would walk into the chandlery on the upper road with three duties and a settlement behind me and a sign over the door, and every one of the five of you would be proud of me, and I would not have noticed the day it stopped being for the group. I have built that. Not once. Three times, in three cities, with three different groups of frightened people, and I noticed it *twice* and both times it was a woman who noticed it and not me." He put his thumb on the table. "So: no. And put it on a wall, because I am the one most likely to forget it, and I would rather be stopped by my own handwriting than by Adrian, who is the only man here I can get away anything from."

Priya Shah went third, and she refused in about eleven seconds, which is the fastest refusal of the evening and the only one nobody believed.

"I am going to be honest about this and then I am going to regret it, and I would like the regretting on the record after the honesty and not instead of it. I want it. I have wanted it since the week I arrived. I have my own name on a wall over a market because I asked for it to go up there, and a woman from a wharf came up a stair on Friday and asked me a question about a rule I did not write, and I have not slept properly since, and I would like a shape to stand in." She put the slate down flat, the way she did everything with it. "No. Because a workway is a thing that has to be asked about, forever, by everybody affected, and my whole life has been spent arranging to be looked at, and I have never once had a thing I was obliged to be looked at for. And because of the light."

"There is no light," said Adrian.

"There is a light," said Priya, "and the only person in this room who has seen it has put his name on a question and not on a crew, and I have read that entry in my own log, and I would rather this room had one thing in it that nobody can be got into, and Owen is it. So: no. And I am sorry. And I would like it written down that I wanted it."

Mina Okoye went fourth, and said the thing Adrian had not chosen, and the thing nobody in the room had chosen, and it was that she was in favour.

"I want to say it out loud so that it is not a surprise in a bad week," she said, in English, flatly, looking at Adrian. "I think you should take it. And I want to be careful, because I know what I sound like and I have spent nine days being the woman in this room who says the hard sentence, and I am not saying this because I like you."

"Say it."

"You are the only person alive in this city who can see where a floor stops being a floor, and you said it yourself this morning in a yard, and you said it like a man apologising for a tool, and it is not a small thing and it is the only thing any of us has got, and in six weeks somebody is going to ask you to stand somewhere and say whether it is safe, and there will be no other person in Saltwake who has done this before and knows less about the consequences." She put her hands down. "And the offer is a duty. A duty with a shape. I have spent nine days watching what happens to people with no shape: Priya has six names on a wall, Jonah has a good coat, Adrian has a seat he did not want, and Owen has a light in a wall that only he can see and no authority whatsoever to go and look at it. Every one of you is a man with a piece of nobody's job. And the second somebody asks you to do something this city cannot pay for, the thing that will decide it is not the road. It is that you will have no *obligation* to refuse, because nobody in Saltwake is owed anything by you, and a man with nothing owed to him can always be talked round, and I have watched a woman talk a man round in about eleven minutes in a room in this city by saying his own name." She said, "That is the whole of my case, and it is a bad case, and I would like it in the book in my words and not summarised."

Dante Kuo went fifth and said no for a reason that made Renna Ord, in the corner, laugh out loud for the only time in eight days.

"No," he said. "Because a duty with a shape is a thing everybody can see, and when it goes wrong everybody is going to look for the shape and not for the man. I have been in a warehouse for twenty-two years and I know exactly what that is like and I have watched a guy die because he had a title." He flexed the strapped arm, carefully, the way a man does a thing that is not going to be right for a while. "If I do a job and I do it wrong, then it is me. And I know the man, and so does everybody here. That is the only safety anybody has ever actually had."

Owen Park went last, and he was the one who had to be got up, and he stood up, and did not use any notes.

"No," he said. "And the reason is not that it is dangerous, and I want the room to be clear about that, because I have been the dangerous one all week and I would like one thing in the record that is not that. The reason is the light."

"Nothing in this room has ever seen the light," said Adrian.

"Nothing in this room has ever seen the light, and Hesper Saye has had the back of my head open with tweezers in front of nine people and looked me in the eye, and it is not a trick, and it is not the sun, and I have stopped arguing about it." He turned round and looked at the north wall of the room, which was the wall with the plaster on it, and which was four feet from where he was standing. "If I take a workway that reads roads, then the next time I see a light behind a wall I will not know whether I am *seeing* it or whether I am *doing* it. And that is the only thing I have got that no one else has, and I have been carrying it around for eight days feeling like a crank, and the second I get a permission that makes me an instrument then I will never be able to find out whether I am still the only one who can see it or whether I have simply been given the ability to see it. And I would rather be wrong on my own for the rest of my life than be right because of a machine."

He sat down.

Nobody said anything for about four seconds.

And then the pale field came up in the middle of the room, in the air, in front of the north wall, the colour of a paper form, and Adrian watched six people see it happen, which was the second time in his life and the first time he had watched a panel be received by other people and not have a single one of them see a thing.

> A WORKWAY IS AVAILABLE.
>
> Wayfinder. To read a road is to carry its name. A wayfinder may not leave a road unnamed while holding it.
>
> The stone below the chapel is out of its place. It is in the footing of the yard-side landing. Four steps left of the yard.
>
> The cost of the stone is not part of the workway.
>
> This is offered to you. It is not yet accepted.

It stood for nineteen seconds.

And Adrian, who had refused it in a chapel on Wednesday night with nine people in the room and a notary's slate, looked at it and understood three things at once, and he wrote all three down afterwards because he had learned what happens to a man who notices two.

The first was that it was more precise. On Wednesday it had said *two hundred steps of outer wall, then down*, which had taken him two days and a mason to turn into a landing. This one said the footing of the yard-side landing, four steps left of the yard, word for word what Tamsin and Vask had put in the yard that morning, and Adrian had been standing there when they said it. It was not more accurate. It was *current*, to the hour, and nothing in the world that wanted to help him got better at this rate.

The second was the fourth line, and it took him the longest. *The cost of the stone is not part of the workway.* Marrow had said the cost of the stone was a boundary, and a boundary lands on somebody's field, and Tamsin had said it, and Vask had said it, and the keeper of this room had said it in a yard this morning by naming a season of a man's time. And the field said the cost was not part of this. Not *there is no cost* — that would have been a lie, and the field had lied about a cost once and got caught. This was the *other* construction. This was a true-sounding sentence, put in the place where a cost would go, that made a man stop looking for one. It was the same trick as the sentence about a thing being paid for. It did not need to be a lie. It only needed to be *in the right shape*.

The third thing was the last line.

It said *it is not yet accepted*. Not *it has been refused*. Not *the offer was declined and recorded*. Nine days and the thing had no memory whatever of a refusal, and he had spent nine days building his entire understanding of this world on the assumption that a permission was a thing with a state, and there was no state in it. There was an offer, and there was a man, and there was a place for a yes, and the no did not go anywhere because there was nowhere for it to go.

He wrote that down afterwards and it is the single most frightening line in his notebook for the whole of his first month, and he never found out what it meant.

---

He refused it, and he said the reason out loud, and the reason was the reason.

"Twice now," Adrian said. "And I am not refusing it because it is dangerous, and I am going to say the true reason, and it is the least impressive reason anybody has ever given for anything in this city, including me.

"I am refusing it because of the *six people behind me and the four people in this yard and about nine hundred people who live on this water*. I have a duty. It is in a chained book, with a review date, and it is the only thing I have been given here that I did not ask for. And a workway is a duty with a shape, and I cannot hold a duty with a shape while holding an open one, because the shape is going to be the one that pays and mine is not. When the two disagree there is going to be a Tuesday where I have to choose, and if I have chosen the shape in advance, in a room, in a chapel, for free, in front of people — then I have decided, and I will not have decided anything." He looked round the table. "That is it. That is the whole of it. I am not too dangerous to take a road. I am too *occupied*. I have a job."

Nyla Ferrow, from the wall, said one word in the local language.

"She says *right*," said Tamsin, "and she says it in a tone I have not heard her use, and she says it before you finished and she was not looking at you, and I am not going to interpret it further because she did not ask me to."

---

Then Vask came in, and it was nine o'clock, and she had come for the safe line.

"Right," she said. "Since it is settled and there are people here. On the day, before the bar goes in, I want the line. I want the safest run from the north wall of the void out to the north jamb, and I want it said out loud while three masons are standing on it, because that is the last thing I do before I open a floor, and I would like to do it the way it was done in this yard this morning rather than the way it was done on a wall in the rain eight days ago with a hundred and fifty people watching."

"I cannot do it," Adrian said.

Vask stopped.

"Say that again."

"I can see the edge of it and I can tell you where the edge is," Adrian said, "and I have been right three times in a row now, and I have been wrong once, on Thursday, on the walk-round, in front of five men, and I did the wrong one by trying to take it off them. That was eight days ago and I have not got any better since and I have got worse, because I am now the man in the seat, and the seat is going to be in every conversation I have for the rest of the year. If I do your line for you this week I will do it for you in March, and in March I will be a man who has done it four times and cannot stop, and there is no version of that in which anybody is safer." He held her eye. "Get Renna. Get your masons. Renna Ord reads ground for a living and has done it since she was eleven and she will not have any of this on her at all."

Ottoline Vask looked at him for a long moment, and then she said, "That is the first time in thirty-four years a man has told me no in order to leave me a better man for the job," and went out into the stair, and Adrian found that he had wanted Renna Ord to say something and she was not there, and that this was correct, and that wanting it was the whole of his problem.

---

The refusal was entered in three places. Two of them that night, and the third on the Monday morning, and the third is the one that mattered, because the third one is a page in a roll that goes to Aurel four times a year in a wooden case on a river boat, and the person who reads it will be a clerk whose name Adrian will never know, in a room he will never be in.

Nyla Ferrow wrote it on the keeper's board in chalk, in her own hand, with a witness line, and read it out loud in the room before she turned the board round, and then said, "I will send this to the Concord with the arch's own book, and the clerk will copy it, and the clerk will not have any choice, because it comes in a book from a keeper and a keeper's word does not need a reason." She looked at Adrian. "And I want you to know that a keeper's board is not a public roll, and that is the only way I can give you this, so I am telling you what I am and what I am not."

The Monday entry was the roll of strangers, which goes to Aurel four times a year with everything the Concord knows about anybody in this city who is not from it, and which he had found out about in this hall four days ago, and Adrian had worked all of that out about four days ago, standing in this hall, while a clerk wrote the first line of it:

> **Adrian Vale. Unbound. No workway of any kind. The Wayfinder profile was offered on the second day and again on the eleventh, and refused twice. The refusals were witnessed and recorded, and the second was recorded in his own words and not summarised by a notary.**

"You understand what that is," said Marrow, from a bench at the back, with his hat on his knee.

"Yes."

"It is a sentence about you that will still be true in three years, in a building you have never been to, in the hands of a clerk whose name you will never know, and it is the only document in this city that will say the word *no* about you with a date on it. Not the ledger — the ledger is a working record and the working will change. That is a roll. That is a description of a man, and it is going to be read by a man deciding something, in a room you will never be in, and the thing he is going to read is that you turned down a Crown-adjacent offer of power, in writing, on a date, when a lot of people in your position would have taken it and gone home." Marrow stood up. "I would like you to know that I have thought about whether to say that to you tonight and I have decided to say it, because I would like it on the record that I said it and that you had to hear it."

"Thank you."

"You are going to be a difficult man to argue with in about four months," Marrow said, "and I am going to enjoy it enormously, and I would like you to know now that I have never once in my life enjoyed anything more than being able to argue with a man who has refused something twice in a room with witnesses."

---

Marrow's coat was on and he was at the door when he stopped and came back and did the one thing he had not done for nine days.

He asked the keeper for the chalk.

"May I put my own hand to it," he said, "on the other side, under the keeper's line. It costs me nothing and it is worth a great deal." And when Nyla Ferrow did not answer him at once, he said, "You are going to ask why. I am going to tell you why, and then you can refuse. If a man in a good coat writes an open offer in his own hand, on a keeper's wall, in a room with a witness, then the offer has a *shape*, and a shape is a thing a person can be held to, and an offer with no shape in it is a rumour that a decent man in a wet yard can walk away from. I have been selling this for three weeks in rooms where nobody could write anything down, and I have been doing it, and I have not enjoyed doing it, and I would like it on a wall where it is a promise and not a rumour. And I would like a date on it, because a date is when you decide whether I am a man who means it."

She gave him the chalk.

He wrote it on the board under the keeper's five words, in a small, very neat hand, in the local language and then in English, and it took him about a minute, and Adrian read it upside down because he was standing on the other side of the table.

> **OPEN. A wayfinder workway, offered to Adrian Vale, in this room, on the second Saturday of the flood, in front of six named people, a keeper of this room, and a mason.**
> **It is not accepted. It is not withdrawn.**
> **It stands until the last day of the salt month, when the man in the seat of the pull is to answer three questions in public about a bottom field, and until then or afterwards, at his word.**
> **— I. Marrow, of Aurel. No Crown office. No warrant. My own hand, my own risk, and a date.**

"That is the worst thing you have ever done in a room," Adrian said, when he had finished. "You have just given me until the last day of the salt month to say yes, and you have made it cost you the whole of it, and you have put it on a keeper's wall where I cannot walk past it."

"Yes," said Marrow. "And I would like it noticed that I did it in a room, in front of six people, on a wall, and not in a letter to a clerk. If I had written it in a letter, it would have been a rumour by Thursday, and there is a man in this city who would have made it a rumour on purpose, and he would have been entitled to, because I would have given him something better than a rumour to work with."

"You have had me in a room for three hours and you have made me read out the cost of a thing I have been selling," he said. "So here is the part I have not said to anybody, including the anchorwright, and I am saying it to you at one in the morning in a room with the wind shut out because it cannot be said at noon.

"I do not want the workway so that there is one author of the record. I have wanted that since 1994 and it has never once got me anywhere, and I have been wrong about it for a very long time and I did not know I was wrong until I watched a man with a notebook refuse a thing eight times in eight days for reasons that are all *administrative*."

"Then why," Adrian said.

"Because you are about to leave."

Marrow said it simply, with the flat patience of somebody who has said one thing carefully many times.

"You have a route-shaped feeling in your chest every morning and you have had it since Tuesday and you have not said it out loud to one person in this room, including her, including me, and it is the only thing about you that I am not sure you will do. And I have watched enough men in my life to know that a man with a mark in his palm and a debt in a book and no road is a man who is going to be *offered* a road in about two months by somebody with a warrant, and I would like there to be a version of you in this city that somebody has a duty to ask where he is going. Not to keep you. Not to own you. To *ask* you." He put his hat on. "That is why I want you to take it, and it is the reason I would have taken it myself at your age, and it is a chain and I have dressed it as a duty because in this city a chain sounds like an insult and a duty is a trade. You are the only person in nine years who has been offered something by the wall and has said no in a room. I would like there to be a piece of paper that says a man in this city is *responsible for* something, so that if you go, somebody has to write down that you went."

The wind was shut out of the Salt Weavers' chapel and the plaster was still on the north wall and there was a window behind it that nobody had opened in six years.
