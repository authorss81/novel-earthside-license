# Chapter 0024 — What the Dig Found

The excavation had been argued about for four days and dug in one, and the four days of arguing were, in the end, the reason it worked, because by the Friday of the previous week everybody in that yard had said out loud what they wanted to find.

Adrian had wanted to find the end of the joint. Vask had wanted to find out whether the joint was holding the chapel up or whether the chapel was holding the joint up, which is not the same question and is the only question a mason has ever really needed answered. Tamsin had wanted the second setting's date and had been told by two masons and by Halvard Ketch, separately and without collusion, that no living person could give her a date and that the document she wanted was in a tally-house four streets up the hill in a room with shutters, and she had gone and got it. Renna Ord had wanted nothing and had wanted everybody to stop, and had said so on the Tuesday, and had then spent the Wednesday calculating how many more mornings of rain she was going to have to stand in.

Nyla Ferrow had wanted one thing and had said it once, on the Thursday, in the archway, in the local language, and had then refused to say it again for anybody.

"It is my mother's floor," she said. "Whichever way it goes, it is the floor of the woman who kept this room before me, and I have not been in it, and I am not going to be in it, and you are going to open it without me and I am going to stand at the top of the stair and I am not going to come down. That is the whole of my interest and I would like it treated as a thing that is going on and not as a thing that is being kept out."

Nobody had asked her why, and Adrian had wanted to ask her why, and had asked Tamsin instead, on the Friday night, on a stair, and Tamsin had said four words.

"She is not curious. She is a keeper. Curious people open floors."

---

Low water on the twenty-fourth day of the flood came at about seven in the morning and went out again about two, and the tide table on the wall of the lower gallery has a column for it, and the column says *works*, because the Lock of Salt is worked on the turn, and the turns are the days the table marks, and this month the table marks two of them, the twenty-fifth and the twenty-eighth, and the twenty-eighth is the top and is the flood tide and is six days off and is already a row of numbers with a man standing at the end of it.

That is the whole of what the excavation cost the city. It is the best and worst fact of that week and it is on a chalk board in a yard:

**The dig was on a working day. Working the lock on the turn is the thing that steps the join in the gate. There was no way to dig under a chapel on a Saturday and have it be free.**

Nobody said that in a yard. It was said once, at about eleven o'clock, by Marek Osley, the salt factor with the waxed tablet, who had turned up with a slate and a stick of chalk and had stayed for four hours, and who said it in the tone of a man adding a line to a count.

"You are putting eighteen men on a wall tomorrow at the turn," he said, "because you are opening a floor, and I am the man who has to write down why the wall is manned on a day it is not manned, and I would like somebody to have said the sentence *the wall is manned because a man from a wall wanted to look under a floor*, and I would like it in the ledger, and I would like it in yours." He looked at Adrian. "It is the fourth time. Tuesday's was a crowd. Wednesday's was a reading. Friday's was a hole. I am not making an accusation. I am keeping a count and the count is the same count every time."

"Enter it," Adrian said.

"I intend to," said Marek Osley, and went back to his tablet, and did not say one word about what was under the floor, and did not ask, which Adrian understood by then was the most respectable thing anybody had done in that yard in three weeks.

---

They took up eleven flagstones.

That was the argument, and it was Vask's, and it was won on the Friday night by about four minutes, and it was the reason the whole thing came out.

Four feet of it, north from the cut, is enough to see a nine-foot wall. Twenty-two feet of it, north from the cut, is enough to find out whether a nine-foot wall has an end.

"It is the floor," Vask said. "It is a chapel floor. I have been re-laid four times since it was built and I have laid two of them myself, and I know which stones are mine and I know which are my mother's and I know which are four hundred years old and which are two hundred, and I have been telling this room for three weeks that the price of the floor is the floor, and by the floor I have meant that from the first morning, and I have not been thanked for the distinction and I am going to stop making it." She put a finger on a flagstone. "Eleven. Two courses, north edge, mortared in lime, the new stone bedded on the old bed. I can do eleven in a day with four masons and a plank and no trouble at all. The binding at the arch is in the bed joint of the row that is at the foot of it, so we are up to six days of yard binding, morning and evening, and the flagstones that come up get re-laid the same day in lime and clay and not mortar, because there is no time for mortar, and a re-laid flagstone in lime and clay is a flagstone that will be walked on by forty people in a wet week and it is *not* a repair, it is a patch, and a patch is what this floor already is in four places."

"Six days of the binding."

"Six days of the binding, and every one of those six mornings has a man in it who is not a mason, because a mason is in the hole." She looked round. "And I want that written in the yard in the morning before the bar goes in, in front of the people, and I want Nyla Ferrow to read it out, because it is her ground, and because a man who cannot speak the language of the room he is standing in should not be the one who tells a room what it is paying."

It was written in the yard, and she read it out, and Adrian read it out after her in English and got it wrong twice, and about forty people stood in the rain and listened to both versions, which is the ordinary cost of being the only man in a city who is not allowed to translate by himself.

---

They went down at half past six on the Thursday morning of the twenty-fourth day, and Renna Ord stopped the second cut at eleven o'clock with one sentence, and Vask did not argue, which is how Adrian knew she had wanted the same stoppage.

They had been digging north, along the line, for four hours, and the dressed face had gone from nine feet to twenty-one and had not ended, and at about the eleventh hour of the morning they had taken up the last of the eleventh flagstone and there was the corner.

Not an end. A corner.

The joint turned.

It turned to the east, at a right angle, in a dressed return, and it went under four more stones and stopped, and the shape of it was the shape of a doorway: twenty-one feet of jamb running north, and a return of nine feet running east, and the two together making the L that every threshold in this country is the shape of, because there is no other way to make a hole you can shut.

"There," said Vask. "Put your hand on that, boy."

Ovin Sarr put his hand on the return. He was in the hole, because he was twenty-four and had asked to be in the hole and had been allowed to be in the hole, on the grounds that he was the only man in the yard who could read a string line and a mason could not do both.

"It goes under," he said. "It goes under the north wall of the chapel."

"It goes under the north wall of the chapel and it stops," said Vask, "and I want you to notice *stops*, and I want everybody who can hear me to notice that word, because I have been down in four hundred holes in this city and I have never once found a dressed joint that *stops*. A joint stops where a man stops. A man stops where he has got what he came for, or where he has run out, and running out is a thing you can see." She got up as far as her knees. "There is no run-out in that stone. It is dressed. It is dressed and finished and squared and it stops because somebody laid it that way and then built a wall on top of it."

"Built a wall on top of it," Adrian said, from four feet back, with a notebook, in the rain. "Vask. Is the joint holding the chapel up?"

"No," said Ottoline Vask, and wiped her face with the back of her wrist. "And I have known that since eleven o'clock this morning, and I have been sitting on it for two hours, because the answer is a bad one and I wanted it to be the other one and there is no other one."

She said the rest of it in a yard, standing in the rain, in front of about fifty people, because she had decided in the hole that she would.

"This is not a wall. I said on Saturday it was a wall and I was wrong, and I have been wrong in a yard before and I will do it again. It is a *threshold*. It is a foundation course for a threshold and it goes north and east and it stops, and the chapel is built on top of it, and the chapel's own footings come down on the outside of it, and the threshold is holding nothing." She stamped her hand flat on the wet stone. "Which means the thing that went through here, the thing that was *in* this doorway, was here before the chapel. And the chapel was built on the hole and not across it, and that is not a thing anybody does. You build *across* a hole and you leave the hole alone. You do not build *on* one. Unless you do not know it is a hole."

"Or unless you do know," said Tamsin, from the archway.

---

She had the table. She had had it since the Friday of the previous week, and she had had it out on the trestle in the archway for two hours that morning, and nobody in the yard had been allowed within six feet of it, and Adrian had asked her about that and she had said, "Because I am going to use it to say something and I do not want anybody reading it over my shoulder while I find out whether it says it."

It was a single sheet folded into eight, soft along the folds from seven years of being carried in a coat.

"You have all been looking at a wall," Tamsin said, in the local language, with the sheet in both hands, while the rain came sideways under the archway. "You have all been looking at a nine-foot joint and asking how far it goes, and I have been sitting under this arch for two days being frightened, because I have known for four days what the answer was and I have not wanted to say it in a yard, and I am saying it now, and I am going to be wrong and Vask is going to correct me, and if she corrects me I will stop."

"Go on," said Vask.

"It is a table of every attempt this city has made to move the old river boundary. Eleven lines. And there is one line that has never been in it in eleven days of people looking at walls, and there is one line in it that everybody in this yard has been using for three weeks as an example of what a good answer looks like, and the two lines are the same line." Tamsin turned the sheet round so that the yard could see it, which is a thing that a woman does with a document she has carried for seven years and has never read out in a yard, and her hand was not entirely steady, and Vask came and stood at her shoulder and did not take it. "Line one. The only attempt ever carried out, in living memory, by the three jurisdictions together, at a public act, with witnesses. The old river's edge is written in the stone set in the yard-side landing of this chapel, and the stone is to be lifted and set one hundred yards downstream, and the flood to be allowed to find the new edge itself. The cost column says: the bottom field failed in that year, and four hundred people were rehoused, and the boundary was not moved again."

She put her finger on the sheet.

"That is the stone four steps left of this yard, which is dry, and which a man in a good coat pointed at in a chapel on the second night of this flood, and which I told this yard on the ninth day is not a repair and is a decision about where the river's edge is written." She took her hand off the sheet. "And that is the joint under this floor. Both of them. Set in the same year, by the same three jurisdictions, in the same act, with the same witnesses standing round. One of them was a stone you could lift. One of them is a threshold under a floor with a doorway shape in it, and a man who wanted to move a boundary and had a mason write it twice — once where a man could carry it, and once where a man could not."

"Two halves of one boundary," said Adrian.

"Two pieces of one *line*," Tamsin said, "and they were not the same piece and they were not meant to be the same piece, and the whole of the difficulty is that nobody has ever had to explain the difference, because one of them has been in the open for a hundred years and one of them has been under a floor for a hundred years." She folded the sheet over her arm. "And then somebody lifted the one that was in the open and carried it a hundred yards downstream, in public, with witnesses, at a cost of four hundred people's houses, because the *stone* is the part you can argue with, and the joint is the part that cannot be argued with at all, because to argue with it you have to take a chapel floor up, and nobody in this city has ever once in nine hundred years wanted to do that."

She looked up.

"So the boundary is written in two places a hundred yards apart, and only one of them is the one this city believes, and the other one is under a building that everybody walks over, and it is in the same line, and the water does not care which one the city believes. That is what is under this floor. That is the whole of what is under this floor, and it is not a mystery and it is not a monster and it is not a door. It is a *record*. Somebody wrote the boundary into the ground twice, so that moving one copy would be a lie you could catch, and then somebody moved one copy, and left the other one where the water could find it."

Nobody in the yard said anything for about nine seconds, and in those nine seconds a man at the back said, in the local language, "Then whose is the river?"

"It is not ours," said Tamsin. "It is whichever one the water is standing on this morning. That is the horror of it and it has been the horror of it for a hundred years and I have known for four days and I did not say it because I have been on line seven of that sheet since I was twenty-one and I did not want to be the woman who says things like that in a yard."

---

The repair is the last thing, and it took Vask forty minutes and a lamp held sideways, and it is the reason she would not name the thing.

Under the joint, at the depth of the dressed face, in the middle of the nine feet of it, there is a place where the stone is not stone.

It is a patch, about four feet long, and it is the same grey, close-grained stuff as everything else down there, and it is set slightly proud, and the tool finish on it is different.

"That is a repair," Vask said. "That is a man, about two hundred years ago, opening a joint in a foundation stone, looking at what was in it, and closing it again. And he closed it beautifully. It is a better joint than anything in this city has made since, and I have been looking at it for forty minutes and I have been *admiring* it, and I have not admired a joint in about eleven years."

"And the damage?" said Roke, from the step, with his slate.

"There is no damage in the patch. That is the thing I cannot get past." She put the lamp down. "In two hundred years of mason work I have never once seen a joint repaired *over* nothing. You open it, you find the fault, you take the fault out, and you close it on the good stone. There is no such thing as a man opening four feet of a joint in a foundation under a chapel, in a flood month, with a deadline, and closing it on more of the same, and doing it better than the original." She stood up off her heels, and it took her a while. "Unless the fault was not in the stone. Unless the fault was on the *other side* of it, and this side was fine, and he came in, in the dark, under a floor, with a bar, because he had been told there was a fault, and he looked, and there was nothing he could do about it from this side, and he closed it anyway and went and wrote something down somewhere."

"Where," said Adrian.

"I do not know," said Ottoline Vask. "And I have been in this trade for thirty-four years and I am going to tell you the thing I told this yard on the nineteenth morning, and I would like it read back to me afterwards. **A quarter of a thing is not a thing.** I have got twenty-two feet of dressed stone, a corner, a threshold shape, a repair of about two hundred years, a table with a line on it, and a mason in a wet yard telling me a story. If I say what it is, then the story becomes the identification, and the story is *mine*, and in nine months when the ninth Elms household or a man in a good coat or a clerk in Aurel puts the story in a document, it will be a stone in a wall with a mason's account of it on it, and there will be no way for anybody to get back to the twenty-two feet."

She turned to Vail, who had been there since seven in the morning with a slate, standing in the rain, writing.

"So it goes in as a description," Vask said, "in your words and mine, in front of these people, and it does not go in with a name on it. I will not put *Anchorstone* on that page about a stone I have had one afternoon with. I will put *Anchorstone* on it in a year, when there are three of us who have seen it and two of us are dead."

---

Sera Vail read the identification out in the archway at about four o'clock in the afternoon, in the local language and then in English, in a flat clerk's voice, from a slate, with about sixty people standing in the rain in a yard under a lock chapel, and Adrian stood four feet back and let her read it and did not write anything down for nine minutes afterwards, which was the first time in twenty-four days that he had been in a room with a document in it and had not wanted to take it apart.

> **Entered the twenty-fourth day of the flood, at the low water of the morning, in the yard of the lock chapel of Saltwake, and read out twice and witnessed by about sixty persons, of whom nine are named below.**
>
> **A dressed joint, laid on edge, of a single close-grained grey stone, cleared to a length of twenty-two feet running north from the fourth course beneath the north flagstones, with a dressed return of nine feet running east beneath the north wall, terminating square. There is a doorway shape in it. The stone is sound throughout. Above it, the chapel's own footings are laid clear of it and do not bear upon it.**
>
> **At a place four feet long in the middle of the cleared face there is a patch of the same stone, set slightly proud, tooled to a different finish, closing a joint that has been opened. The patch is of about two hundred years and no more. The work is of a high order.**
>
> **The joint is described, and is not named. The description is entered in the words of O. Vask, mason, thirty-four years in this city, and of T. Quill, anchorwright, and no name is entered against it. — O. Vask, O. Quill**
>
> **The table of the boundary, kept by T. Quill, is produced and read, and line one of it is read, and the notary records that the holder of the pull has observed that the two pieces named in that line and the joint described above lie in one line, and that the holding of that observation is a matter for the meeting-ground of the Reed Reach and for no person in this yard.**
>
> **The ground is re-boarded. The dig is finished for this season. Item two of the mason's list is observed: nothing has been taken out of the hole in any vessel, and no writing has been taken out of the hole, and nothing has come out of the hole that answers, and the list stands on the chapel door.**
>
> **The cost of the day, entered by M. Osley, salt factor: the gate crew is eighteen men and one foreman, and the crew is manned at the turn this day because a floor is open in the yard, and the reason is entered, and the count is the fourth such entry in eleven days.**

Vail read it twice, and then she put the slate down, and said the thing that made about forty people in a wet yard sit down on the boards they had taken up in the morning.

"There is no name on it," she said. "That is the whole of what Vask has done and it is the first time in twenty-six years that a mason has refused me a name and been right, and I want it entered that I was asked for one and refused and that I am not appealing."

---

And then, at about a quarter past five, at the mouth of the yard, with the rain easing and the light going the colour of a wet slate, the pale field came up in the middle of the yard, in the air, in front of about sixty people, in the colour of a paper form.

> **THE STONE UNDER THE FLOOR HAS A NAME.**
>
> **It has been seen by three people and read by one. A man who can read it may name it, and the name will be true, and a name that is true is a thing that can be searched for, and a thing that can be searched for is a thing that can be moved.**
>
> **The holder may give the name. Nobody will take it from him.**

It stood there for twenty-two seconds, and Adrian watched about sixty people have a completely ordinary wet Friday afternoon around a thing that none of them could see.

It was the same offer as the fifteenth morning of the flood, in a different coat. *A holder may be shown which one the water is using.* It had been true then, and it was true now, and it would have been true in the middle of the yard with Vask's own hands on the stone, and the reason it was dangerous had not changed in nine days and had in fact got worse, because nine days ago the answer would have been a boundary and now the answer would be a *name*, and a name is what a man writes on a stone when he wants to be the man who set it.

He wrote it in the notebook, standing in the rain, in the flat hand, without crossing anything out, and read it out afterwards to Tamsin, who had watched his face change and who wanted it in a document rather than in a man's memory.

"It is not lying," he said. "That is what I have got to keep saying. It says three things that are all true, in the right order, in a form that a tired man in a wet yard will read as *an offer of help*. It says three people have seen it, which is true — I have seen it, Vask has seen it, Quill has seen it. It says a man who can read it may name it, which is true; I could name it, in about eleven seconds, and I have had a name for it since Saturday. And it says a name that is true is a thing that can be searched for, and a thing that can be searched for is a thing that can be moved, which is *also true* and which is the most frightening sentence anybody has said to me since a woman on a raft said I had made a bill by accident."

"And the last line," said Tamsin. "*Nobody will take it from him.*"

"The last line is the shape of the offer. It is not there to be true. It is there so that a man reads the top three lines and skips it, and then at four o'clock in the afternoon in ten days' time somebody asks him a question in a room and the last line is the thing he remembers about it." He turned the notebook round so she could see the page. "Which is what I have been doing since the twelfth of this month, in my own head, in a market, and on a wall, and the sentence is the same sentence. It does not need to be a lie. It needs to be *in the right place*."

He did not give the name, and the field went, and the yard emptied, and Vask chalked the day's date on the chapel door under the four words and the fifth, and the binding was said at dusk in the yard by a mason who had been in a hole for eleven hours, and it took the first time.

---

The name he had not given was not a Veyran word, and he had known it since the eighteenth morning, and he had written it in his own notebook in Latin letters on the second page, and had never said it out loud to a Veyran.

He had called it the *outer threshold*, because it is on the bank above the outer channel and because it is a threshold, and because it is outside the chapel, and because it was outside the wall in his head.

And Vask's page said *a dressed joint, described, and not named*, and the page was right, and Adrian stood in a wet yard at the end of the twenty-fourth day and understood that a word he had been using privately since the third week had just been retired by a mason, and that he was going to have to stop using it, and that the reason he was going to have to stop using it was that it was a thing he had named and could therefore be held to.

He wrote that down too.

---

And then, because the record is the record, it travelled.

That was the part of the twentieth-fourth day that he had not been ready for and could not afterwards claim to have been ready for, and it is in his notebook in nine words, at the top of the page, and he wrote it there that night before he wrote anything else:

*Twenty-two feet of dressed stone, described, unnamed, in a public book, read out to sixty people in the open — and by the following morning four people who were not in that yard knew what is under the chapel floor.*

It was not a leak. He went and established that, personally, in about eleven hours, and it is the shortest piece of work he has ever done and the only one he has ever been proud of.

Wenna Cray heard it at Silt Row on the Monday night, in a public reading, because the Concord's roll of public business is nailed under oiled cloth on the north wall of a converted salt store and anybody may stand and read it and about forty people in this city do. Hessa Ruun's count went down the Reach road in a punt on the Tuesday. A mason who had been in the hole and had asked not to be named said the name of the chapel in a chandlery on the upper road, buying nails, to a factor who was Ottavia Reek's brother, and the factor repeated it in a market because it is the kind of thing a person repeats. And a woman with a barrow put flour on the banks of a ditch four miles away in Corrow on the Wednesday at four in the morning, and told a boy, because she was alone, and the boy was the miller's, and the miller's boy told Osten Fenn at the second gate on the Thursday, and Osten Fenn had been in a punt with three of them eleven days earlier.

Four routes. Four ordinary mouths doing what mouths do. And not one of them had been asked to say anything by anybody, and the document had been *designed* to travel — it was a public roll, it was in a room anybody could stand in, it had been read twice in the open, and Adrian had put it there.

He wrote this at the bottom of the page and underlined it once:

*This is what I asked for. A record that other people can act on is a record that other people can repeat, and I have spent twenty-four days learning that names move because somebody reads them out loud, and I have been so frightened of that for three weeks that I did not notice that the fix is not quietness. The fix is that the thing that moves should be true.*

And then, underneath, the sentence he did not cross out:

*The thing that moves is now in nine mouths in a city of eleven hundred people and I cannot get it back and I do not want to, and what I have to decide before the twenty-fifth is whether a woman in a loft above a market is still a woman in a loft above a market in the morning, and I already know the answer and I have known it since a mason put a third line on my form about not being harmed for saying what you saw.*
