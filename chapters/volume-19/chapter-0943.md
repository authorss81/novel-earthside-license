# Chapter 0943 — Two Hands On One Leaf

A rod came up the nine steps of that stair in the hand of a man who stopped at the bottom one and did not come on, and the light off the one window went over the flags inside that room and came to an end four feet inside the door and never reached him where he stood.

It was Saturday, the six hundred and twenty-eighth day of the Bare Month. The sun had been up before the light and had been on the roofs and the trestles since, and the crust that had been in the joints of the flags all that week had broken up and gone soft at the edges of the walking spaces, and the light came in under the awning at that store's end and lay the length of the bench and stopped about two feet short of the near end of it. The bar lay along the top step, outside its two sockets, with the sun standing on one end of it. On the outside of the door at the foot of that stair the day in chalk stood out white and the bare piece of board under that day stood clear of the wood and had nothing on it. The second book lay on the bench in the middle of it with its cover slab drawn back off the leaf under it, and the register lay open beside it at the far end with that dry cloth along the head of its own page.

The woman of about twenty-nine who keeps a public register had her own end of that bench with her own hand flat on the page in front of her. The man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board stood at the foot of that stair inside the door with his own board propped against the end of the bench and the cloth over his own shoulder. In the middle of that bench the man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall two stalls along had his own board flat across his own knees with its bare back turned out. The woman of about fifty-two kept her own far wall and the stool standing against the wood by it. The bucket stood on the stone by the table with the rag lying across its rim and the man of about twenty-seven not far off it. The man of about thirty-one who carries things for a living stood at the near wall with the satchel up against his own ribs and the flap of it away from his own side. Adrian Vale had the near end of the bench with his own hand on the wood and nothing in his other hand. The light lay on all of them and did not reach the bottom step of that stair.

**"I have come up that stair with a thing to say, and I will say what it is going to cost before I say what it is, and what it is going to cost is that after this morning there will be two accounts of one day standing in two books in one room and that nobody who is not in this room will ever be able to say which of the two is the one that happened, and that will be so for good, because a line that has gone into a book cannot be got back up out of it by anybody, and the man who put it in there least of all. That is the whole of what it costs and I know it before I say the rest of it. There is a line in that book on that bench with a day standing against it, and the day it stands against is not my day, and I have never had a line in a book in my life and I am not asking anybody in this room to put one in for me now. I was not in this room on that day and I was not in this city on that day either. I was out on those flats in the open with willow in the ground and a rod in my hand, and what that day did is written in the ground out there, and any man with a rod can go and read it, and it is not what that line says. I am not going to stand at the bottom of this stair and say what that line says, and I am not asking anybody in here to tell me that I am wrong, and I am not asking anybody in here to tell anybody else that they are right."** said the man of about fifty-two who digs, at the bottom step of that stair, with the rod standing in his own hand and the light of the doorway on his own shoulder.

He said it once, and it was not said a second time at any hour of that day, and nobody in that room asked him a second thing about it.

**"I am not going to tell you that you are wrong and I am not going to tell you that you are right, and I am not going to say what that line says, and I have not been asked what a line in that book is for and I am not going to be asked it this morning either. I am going to turn that book round on this bench so that the face of the leaf is looking at the door, and you can stand there and read it, and you can stand there as long as it takes you to read it. And when you have read it there is a book open on this bench with a page in it and nobody standing at this end of it, and I am not going to ask you what you are going to do about it."** said the woman of about twenty-nine who keeps a public register, at the far end of the bench with her own hand flat on the page in front of her.

She said it once. Then she put her own hand under the second book, lifted it off the wood without turning the leaf, carried it round on the wood until the face of that leaf was looking at the open door and the light off the flags, and set it down again where the leaf stood open to the doorway, and her own hand went back onto the page of her own register and stayed there.

He read it from the bottom step of that stair. The light off the doorway came across the flags and up onto the leaf and he read it with his own head bent to it, and he was a long time about it, and nobody in that room looked at what was on the leaf while he was reading it and nobody in that room asked him afterwards what was on it. The man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board wiped the face of his own board from the top of the top column down to the foot of it and did not go over the space. The man of about twenty-seven carried the bucket down the stair past the bottom step and up again with the water standing flat in it. The man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall two stalls along kept his own thumb along the foot of his own board and left it there. The woman of about fifty-two kept her own hands at the sides of her dress. Adrian Vale took his own hand off the near end of that bench and went down to the step below the door at the foot of the stair and came back up again and put his own hand in the same place on the wood, and nothing was said about that either.

Then the man of about fifty-two who digs came up the nine steps and into that room and stood at the far end of the bench on the side the keeper was not on. She took her own hand off the open page in front of her and drew the register round on the wood until the page lay square under his own hand and put her own hand back on the far corner of it and left it there. He put his own hand at the head of that page and she kept hers at the foot of it and neither of them took it off, and the leaf lay between their two hands doing what a leaf does when two hands are on it at opposite ends, and what went into it was his own account of a day that was not his own, in his own hand, with the same day standing against it, and not one word of it was read out of that room by anybody at any hour of that day.

Nobody in that room was thanked for anything that morning and nobody in that room was told that anybody else was right, and the man of about thirty-one who carries things for a living stood against the near wall with the satchel against his ribs and the flap of it hanging away from his own side and said nothing. The two books lay on that bench when the light began to go, the one of them open with a leaf in it that two hands had been on and the other one open with a leaf in it that a man's own hand had gone along and come back up, and both of them were on the same bench and neither of them was shut and no mouth in that room said which of the two accounts was the one that had happened.

The man of about fifty-two who digs went back down the nine steps a little after the light had begun to go and stood at the foot of that stair on the bottom step with his own hands on his own knees. The rod was standing on its end in the corner of that room against the leg of the bench where he had put it down when he came in, with the flat blade on the top of it turned in towards the wall, and the end of it had gone into the flags at that corner about the depth of a nail and had to be turned a little before it would come up, and nobody in that room had asked him what it was for.
