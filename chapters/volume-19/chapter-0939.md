# Chapter 0939 — Where A Book Would Sleep

The woman of about twenty-nine who keeps a public register put her own finger down into the dust on the top of the bench and drew a mark on the wood, and the man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board set his own thumb on the first mark and slid it along the grain of the bench to the end of the wood, and the man who came up that stair stood at the near end of it with his own hand on the lip and did not say where a book would go and did not ask anybody else to say it either.

It was Tuesday, the six hundred and twenty-fourth day of the Bare Month. The sun had been on the roofs since the light and the crust in the joints of the flags had begun to break up again and go soft at the edges of the walking spaces. That light came in under the awning and lay down the whole length of that wood. The bar was where it always was, out of its two sockets on the top step at the back of that store. Chalked on the wood of that door at the foot of the stair was a day, and the bare piece of board under the day had nothing on it and stood clear. The chalk box was on that table inside, carried in off the step the day before, its lid standing off at one corner and the two sticks lying across one another in it. The second book lay on the bench at the far end with the cover slab off and the leaf under it standing up to the light at the edge where the slab had been lying across it, and the register lay open beside it.

The three of them were at that bench and the rest of the room went on round them. In the middle of it the man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall two stalls along sat with his own board across his own knees. The woman of about fifty-two had her own wall and her own hands down the sides of her dress. The bucket stood on the stone by the table. The man of about thirty-one who carries things for a living had the near wall with the satchel up under his own arm. There was room in that room for standing and not much else.

---

They worked it out in the ordinary way that three people work out a thing when they are all standing at the same bench with nothing in their hands but what they brought up the stair with. The keeper went first. The book in this room went on the shelf behind the bench, in the place the second one had stood in for as long as anybody could remember, and she drew the first mark for it on the top of the bench with her own finger in the dust. Then the man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board said that a book could as well go on the bare piece of board under the day chalked on the outside of that door at the foot of the stair, and that nobody had ever once looked at what was under that day and that was the safest place in the building for a thing nobody read, and she drew the second mark. Then the man who came up that stair said the top step, where the bar lay, and she drew the third, and she said nothing about the bar and nobody asked her what a book would be doing beside it.

Then the ground at the foot of that stair, from the bottom step out to where the paving gives out. Then the market row itself with the trestles and the boards in it, and the store's end of that row, and the board on the outside wall at the far end of it that stood on its one nail above its step with the hole of a second nail empty above it. Then the salt wharf at the near end of eleven miles of flats with the yard and the store door and the stack of boards in it. Then out past the last named house with the fence along the top of the shut road, and the house with the length of new rope over the back of a chair in it, and the handcart with the mallet bound in cord in the bed of it, and the shut road itself with the gate standing at the end of it.

That was seven places and there were seven marks in the dust on the top of the bench, and the three of them stopped, because they had got to the end of what they knew and there was no eighth place any of them could put down. Nobody in that room was given a name for anything and nobody's hands were given anything to hold for it. A book in a room is a book.

**"I am going to say where a book would sleep and I am not going to write it down anywhere but on this bench, and a book standing in a room does not make the people standing in that room anything at all, and I am not asking one of you to keep any of them, and I am not telling any of you where yours goes."** said the woman of about twenty-nine who keeps a public register, at the far end of the bench with her own finger still in the dust.

The man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board went down the stair after that with his own board under his arm and the cloth over his own shoulder, and the man who came up that stair went down the stair after him with one sleeve of his coat wet to the shoulder and the other sleeve dry, and the two of them went out along the market row together to look at what they had put down in the dust.

---

On the row the board on the outside wall stood in the height where a man walks with its face out and the smear from a wet hand still lying across the middle of it. About four people a day went along that row on their own business while the light was on the flags, and about half of them took a rate off that face without stopping at it and about half went along without stopping at it at all, and none of them stood in front of it. Neither of the two men said one word about it, and neither of them was stopped at it, and neither of them looked at anybody who went past.

---

At the near end of eleven miles of flats Adrian Vale had his own hands flat on the sill of the store door, which stands a hand's breadth across, and he went along that sill with his own two thumbs and said out loud that it stood a hand's breadth across, and that a board would go through it on its side if anybody put one to it, and that the putting of it to it was not his to decide. The man of about thirty-eight at that salt wharf came to the door with a board on his own shoulder, looked at the sill and at the gap for as long as it took Adrian Vale to finish what he was saying, and then put the board down on the flags inside the edge of the yard and went and took his knife to the stack instead. The light went off the flats while the man of about thirty-eight at that salt wharf was still working at the stack, and that board was lying on the flags inside the yard edge at the light's going and had not gone through the door and nobody had decided anything about it. Nobody thanked Adrian Vale for the two thumbs he had put along that sill and nobody told him he was wrong.

---

The keeper put her own thumb on the last of the seven marks in the dust on the top of that bench at the light's going and rubbed it out and left the other six standing, and she went her own hand down the wood over the place where it had been and said nothing about it, and nobody in that room asked her which of them she had taken off the bench and why.
