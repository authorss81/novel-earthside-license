# Chapter 0901 — Three Things That Do Not Agree

Two hands went the length of the bench and stopped at the book where it lay at the far end of the wood, and the wood was cold under them at the near end of it and colder at the far end where the draught off the open door lay along it, and neither of the two hands opened the book.

It was Saturday, the five hundred and eighty-sixth day of the Bare Month. The rain had come off the flats in the night and had not finished, and it came off the edge of the awning at the store's end of the market in a line thin enough to miss. The flags of the row were dark from end to end and the water stood in every seam between them. A bar lay along the top step of the stair at the back of the store out of its two sockets with the wet standing on the whole length of it, and the two sockets on the far side of the door below were empty. Outside the door a day in chalk on the wood had gone soft at its edges in the weather, and under the day a bare piece of board about as wide as a hand stood clear of the door at its near end by the width of a finger, with nothing on it whatever.

The room over the market was full and most of what was in it was on its feet. The woman of about twenty-nine who keeps a public register had the register open on the far end of the bench with her own hand flat on the near page of it, and five strokes stood in the column holding figures with a day against each of the five, and none of them was struck. The second slate stood on the shelf behind the bench at the height of a person's shoulder. At the middle of the bench the stallholder, the man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall two stalls along, kept his own board flat across his knees with the bare back of it turned out and his thumb resting along the foot of it where a rate wanted its day. The man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board was at the near end with both hands down on the wood. The man of about thirty-one who carries things for a living had the one window with the satchel under his arm. The woman of about fifty-two stood where the light coming off the flags reached her, with her hands at the sides of her dress.

---

The three were read three ways the morning and they did not agree about anything. A page is read down, and this one was read down and only down, because a column of figures goes down and there is no way to read across one and get anything out of it. The board on the outside wall at the far end of the market is read across, because its figures come out at the height where a man walks and anybody going along the flags could read all of it without going up on the step in front of it. The book lying open at the far end of the bench with the rain going on the step outside of it was neither, and there was no way in the room to read it at all.

**"It rained on the shut road yesterday the same as it is raining on this row, and it went on raining after the last of them had gone through, and none of it is on a page,"** said the woman of about twenty-nine who keeps a public register, at the far end of the bench, **"and about nine of them went through the gate, and every one of them has gone out of the reach of every mouth in this room."**

**"They have gone out of the reach of this room,"** said the man of about thirty-four, and put both hands flat on his own knees.

About four people a day went past the foot of the stair on their own business while the light was on the flags, and the door stood open on its latch the whole of it, and the room went on with what it had in its own hands. The man of about twenty-seven carried the bucket down the stair and up it again and stood it by the leg of the table with the water standing flat in it, and the thread coming out of the crack in the near end of the trough went along the stone of the step and off into the low place at the side of the yard.

**"That thread out of the near end of the trough wants a heat before it wants anything else,"** said the man of about twenty-seven, at the leg of the table.

The man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board had not been in the room on Friday and said nothing about the road at any hour of the morning. The woman of about fifty-two said nothing either. The man of about thirty-one turned about from the window and looked at the flags inside it instead, and the satchel stayed under his arm.

About the seventh hour the woman of about fifty-two came along the bench and put her own hand flat on the page, and the top edge of the leaf was wet. The wet had come in under the cover where the cover stood up a finger's width on the binding and had gone along the head of the column and come down at the top of it. The day against the first of the five figures had begun to run. It ran a finger's width down the margin and stopped on the line, and the figure itself stood clear of it and the four days below it stood clear too.

**"The day against the top one is going,"** said the woman of about twenty-nine who keeps a public register, **"and when it has gone it cannot be read off this page again, and I cannot put it back as it was."**

**"It cannot be put back as it was,"** said the man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall two stalls along.

Nobody answered her and nobody thanked her. She took her own hand off the wet place, looked at the wet on the palm of it and wiped it on the side of her dress, and left the page lying open with the top of it wet and the rest of it as it was.

At about the ninth hour the man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board went out to the row and stood in front of his own trestle with his own board still standing face out on it, and he did not turn it and he did not put a cloth on it, and he came back up at about the tenth hour and took his place at the near end again.

---

At about the tenth hour he looked down the column from the first of the five to the last of them, and the day against the first had gone the colour of the wet at the head of the page, and where the wet had stopped there was a pale line across the margin about the width of a thumbnail. He put his own hand flat on the wood of the bench beside the book and did not touch the page. The light went off the row and the wet along the head of the leaf began to go into the paper at its own pace.
