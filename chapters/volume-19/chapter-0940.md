# Chapter 0940 — One Thing Said And Said Back

The stair at the back of the store took a man up it at about the tenth hour with the light on the flags at the bottom step and the date in chalk standing on the outside of the door behind his shoulder, and he came up the last three steps at a walk because he was not in a hurry, and his own hands were empty.

It was Wednesday, the six hundred and twenty-fifth day of the Bare Month. The sun had been up since before the light and the crust in the joints of the flags had gone soft at the edges of the walking spaces and was standing pale where a heel had not been on it, and the daylight came under the awning at the store's end of the market and lay the length of the bench. The sun had come along the top step of that stair and was standing on one end of the bar where it lay out of its two sockets. The date in chalk stood out white on the outside of the door at the foot of the stair and the bare piece of board under that date stood clear and empty, and nothing went under it. The box of chalk stood on the table inside with its lid off at one corner. The second book lay on the bench at the far end with its cover slab off the leaf and the leaf lying flat, and the register lay open beside it with the dry cloth along the head of the page.

Adrian Vale had the near end of that bench and no board of his own and nothing in his hands. The woman of about twenty-nine who keeps a public register had the far end with the second book in front of her. The man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board stood at the near end of the room with his own board propped against the end of the bench and the cloth over his own shoulder. The man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall two stalls along sat in the middle of that bench with his own board held flat across his knees. The woman of about fifty-two kept her own wall, her own hands down the sides of her dress, and the stool against the wood on her own side of it. There was a bucket standing at the leg of that table with the rag over its rim. The man of about thirty-one who carries things for a living had the near wall and the satchel against his ribs. Most of what stood in that room was standing up.

---

**"I will have given up the only thing I am good for."**

He said it once, and he said it into the middle of that floor and not to anybody in it, and he did not say it again at any hour of that day. The woman of about twenty-nine who keeps a public register was about nine feet off him at the far end of the bench and she was doing something else with her own hands at the corner of the register's cover while he said it, and she did not stop, and she did not turn round.

**"Put my name down with the rest of them."**

He said it once. Nobody answered him and nobody argued with him and nobody improved on one word of it.

**"Your name goes in the same book as the others."** said the woman of about twenty-nine who keeps a public register, at the far end without lifting her own hands off the cover.

She said it once, in her own words, on the same morning, and she did not say it again at any hour of that day. Not one person in that room thanked either of them and not one person in that room said that either of them was right, and nobody asked either of them a second thing then, and nobody asked either of them a second thing at any hour of the rest of that day.

---

He wrote it himself, in his own hand, at the foot of the column that holds figures on the second book, with a day against it in the same hand. His own hand went over the page and his own shoulder was over it and the leaf was not turned and the cover slab was not put back until it was done. Not one person in that room looked at what was under his own hand while it was going in, and he did not read it out afterwards and she did not read it out and she did not say whose line it was and nobody in that room asked her. The room went on with what it had in its own hands while it was going in. The man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board wiped the top of his own board and stopped at the foot of the middle column and did not go over the space there. The man of about twenty-seven took the bucket down that stair and brought it back with the water standing flat in it. The man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall two stalls along kept his thumb where it had been standing all morning and left it there. The carrier came off his own wall and looked out at the flags inside the room and then at the window and stood there. The woman of about fifty-two kept her own hands down the sides of her dress and did not come off her own wall.

---

Two wet rings were on that bench when the light had gone, standing where two books had stood all day, and they were about a hand apart, and in the second one of the two the dust had been pushed out of shape where a cover slab had come down on the wood, and neither of those rings had dried.
