# Chapter 0920 — A Line In The Grit Between Two Flags

A hand went down into the grit that stands in the joint between two flags at the store's end of that market and drew the end of a short stick along the joint for the length of a hand, and the hand came away and the line was left standing in the grit where the hand had been.

It was Thursday, the six hundred and fifth day of the Bare Month. The night had gone hard with a wind that came and went, and by the fifth hour the frost had come off the walking spaces of the row and the grit in the joints between the flags had been left standing proud of the stone in a ridge about the depth of a finger, so that a line drawn in it stood clear. The trough at the top of the market step had running water in it and the thread out of the crack in its near end had gone back into the yard again with the water and was going across the stone and away. The board on the outside wall at the far end of the market stood on its one nail above its step with the hole of the second nail empty above it, and the top of the writing on it had gone pale with the frost. About four people a day went along that row on their own business while the light was on the flags.

The woman of about sixty-nine with her tin stood at the store's end of that row with the tin under her left arm and a short stick about as long as her hand in her right hand. The man of about twenty-seven had the trough at the top of the step with the bucket on the stone and the rag folded over the rim of it. At the mouth of the yard the man of about thirty-eight at that salt wharf stood with his back to the light looking down the yard. The stallholder, the man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall two stalls along, was at his own trestle two trestles along with the bare back of his own board turned out into the row behind his shoulder. On the step below the door at the back of the store the box of chalk had its lid standing off it at one corner.

---

She drew the line and she stood looking at it, and then she took the tin from under her left arm and opened it and put her own hand in and brought it out and shut it again and put it under her arm, and nobody in that row said one word to her about any part of it and nobody asked her one word at any hour of that day. About four people a day went along that row, and half of them looked at the line in the grit as they went past it and half of them went past without looking at it, and nobody stopped.

**"There is a line in the grit at the store's end and it has been going out and coming back since before the light,"** said the man of about twenty-seven, at the trough at the top of the step, **"and it is not the first one of them and it will not be the last."**

**"It is not the first one of them,"** said the woman of about sixty-nine with her tin, at the store's end of the row.

**"I have watched you do it and I have not worked out what it is for and I have not come over to look,"** said the man of about twenty-seven, **"and I am not going to come over to look today either."**

She did not say anything to that and she drew the line again at the next joint along, about the length of a hand, and she went on along the row and drew four more of them at four joints going up toward the far end of the market, and she came back down past the stallholder's own trestle and went up the stair at the back of the store with the tin under her arm. Nobody in that row said one word to her about the lines, and nobody asked her what they were for, and she asked nobody anything. At the far end of that market the board on the outside wall went on being read off the flags by the people who stopped in front of it and not by the people who walked past.

At about the ninth hour the water came across the row from the store's end in a sheet about as wide as a board and went along the joints and took the grit out of the first three of the lines and flattened them into the stone. The one she had drawn first stood against the flow and kept its ridge for about the length of a minute and then went flat with the rest of them and the grit went on down the row toward the low place at the side of the yard. Nobody went after it and nobody stood and watched the water take it.

---

By the light's going the grit in every joint along that half of the row had dried into a pale ridge standing about the depth of a finger, and in the places where the lines had been the grit had gone down into the stone and had dried a shade darker than the grit standing either side of it, so that every line she had drawn that morning stood under the ridge and showed through it from underneath like the shadow of a thing that was not there.
