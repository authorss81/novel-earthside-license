# Chapter 0946 — Eleven Miles Of Wet Crust

The handcart went out across eleven miles of flats with a hurdle laid flat in the bed of it and the head of a hazel mallet bound in cord standing up at the near end of that cart, and it came through the standing water at the mouth of the yard with the near wheel dragging and the near end of the hurdle standing up out of the bed of it.

It was Tuesday, the six hundred and thirty-first day of the Bare Month. Rain had come on those flats off the east in the night and had not stopped by the fourth hour. The line of broken crust that a wheel had made out of that ground on an earlier morning stood with water in it from end to end, and was not a line a person could put a heel on at all. The water stood about the depth of a boot in the two long hollows it had made and lay thin on the crust between them. Where the rain had come hard on the flats to the east of that line, the crust had gone to pieces altogether and was not crust any more.

The man of about sixty had his own hands on the shafts of that handcart from the yard at the store's end of the market all the way out, and the handcart had no load on it but the hurdle and the mallet. He went out over the eleven miles at about the fifth hour, while it was still raining and the rain came at him flat off the east. He went along the line where he could and off it where he could not, and where he went off it the ground took his boots to the ankle. He came out of it on the far side with the mud to the knee of his own coat.

At the near end of the flats the yard stood with the store door open on its hinges, and the stack of boards in it had its foot in the water that came up out of the ground under the flags. The nine boards that had been taken off the top of that stack on an earlier Saturday were standing on edge against the wall under the eave with the top of them out of the wet. The tally-board lay face up on the top of that stack with its two lines of grey grit across the face of it, and that stack was nine boards shorter than it had been on an earlier Saturday. The man of about thirty-eight at that salt wharf had his own knife in his belt and his own hand on the near end of that stack.

---

What he did there was to get the foot of that stack out of the water with the hurdle he had made for standing things on. The whole of the job was to get the hurdle flat on the flags, and the near end of that stack up onto it, and to keep it up there while the ground dried under it. He set the hurdle down on its side on the flags and turned it over and laid it flat with the long rails running away from him. He got down on one knee at the near end of the stack and put his own shoulder under the boards and took the weight of them onto his own hip, while the man of about thirty-eight at that salt wharf put the flat of his own hand on the near end of the stack and lifted his end of it. The two of them set the near end of that stack down on the hurdle.

It came out. The near end of that stack came up off the wet flags and stood on the hurdle about the thickness of a finger clear of them. The water that had been standing round the foot of that stack went out from under it in one piece and went across the yard to the low place and stood there, and the stack stood on its own foot and on that hurdle and no longer touched the wet at all. But the ground under the hurdle was not what the man of about sixty had expected it to be. Where the hurdle's two feet went down the flags gave under them, and the whole of it settled about the depth of a finger into the yard. The two hollows it made came to the level of the water, and the water stood in them.

**"A hurdle is no use to anybody laid flat on ground that has gone soft under it. I have got the foot of this stack clear of the water, and I have got two holes in that yard I would not have chosen to put them in, and I am going to leave both of them standing where they are. The stack is on the hurdle and the stack is out of the water. I will come back when this ground has gone hard and see what I have left."** said the man of about sixty, at the near end of the stack with his own hand flat on the boards.

Nobody in that yard said anything to him about the two hollows, and the man of about thirty-eight at that salt wharf went back to his own knife and his own boards. The man of about sixty picked the mallet up out of the bed of the cart and put it in his own belt. He did not measure the fence out past the last named house, and did not move a post on it, and did not go back that way at all.

---

The rain stopped a little after the middle of the day, and the sun came up on the flats from the west behind that line of broken crust and began to take the water out of it. By the time he had the cart turned round and was coming home along it, the rims of that line stood pale and hard again where the sun was on them, in long stretches. They were black and wet in every dip and hollow between them, and the two long hollows it had been made in still had water standing in them from end to end.

The way he came home along that line showed the whole of it. Where he had put his own heels on the rims that morning, the rims had taken his weight without going through and were dry and pale under him. Where he had gone off them into the ground between, the wet of his own boots had put two prints in standing water, and those prints were still standing at the light's going and had not gone into the crust at all. And at the store's end of that market the light was going down the flags of the row, and the trough at the top of the market step stood where it stands with the crack in its near end quiet.

The two hollows under that hurdle, a long way off at the near end of those flats, were ringed round with the rims of the broken crust standing up out of the wet like the edges of shallow cups. The water had come back into both of them to the level of the flags, and stood in them exactly as it had stood in them before he came.
