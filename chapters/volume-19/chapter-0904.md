# Chapter 0904 — The Strip Worn Out Of The Dust

A hand went flat along the front edge of the shelf at the height of a person's shoulder and lay there, and the hand was not on the second slate standing at the back of the shelf and it was not on the register standing in front of it.

It was Tuesday, the five hundred and eighty-ninth day of the Bare Month. The night had gone hard and blue with no cloud in it anywhere, and by the fifth hour the light came in under the awning at the store's end of the market and lay along the bench from end to end, and the frost was still standing in the shade of the row at about the depth of a coin. At the foot of the stair at the back of the store the door stood open on its latch and the day chalked on the outside of it had gone the colour of the stone round it. On the step below the door the box of chalk had its lid standing off it at one corner, and nothing had taken the two sticks inside it down to the dust at the bottom of the box.

The man of about thirty-one who carries things for a living took the one window with the satchel under his arm and the flap of it hanging down away from his side. At the leg of the table the man of about twenty-seven had his bucket on the stone and the rag thrown over the rim. The woman of about twenty-nine who keeps a public register had the book in her own hands and open on the bench in front of her with her own hand flat on the page, and behind the book the second slate stood at the back of the shelf with a cut across the head of it, turned away from the room. The man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall two stalls along had the middle of the bench to himself with his own board across his knees. At about the fifth hour the man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board came up the stair and stood at the far end of the bench.

---

At about the sixth hour the man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board put both his own hands flat on the far edge of the book and left them there, and the woman of about twenty-nine who keeps a public register put both of hers on the near edge of it at the same time and left them there too, and between the two of them the book lay open on the wood of the bench doing what a book does, holding its own page down against the draught off the open door and staying open on the leaf she wanted. Neither of them turned a leaf and neither of them took a hand off. The leaf lifted at its outer corner twice and fell back, and the man of about thirty-nine put his own thumb on the corner of it where the paper was dry and it stayed down.

**"The dust on the top of the shelf has gone pale and hard except where an arm has gone across it,"** said the man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board, at the far end of the bench with both hands on the far edge of the book, **"and there is one strip gone out of it and not two, and the strip is on your side."**

**"It is on my side,"** said the woman of about twenty-nine who keeps a public register, at the near end of it, **"and I have been at that book every morning of this flood and I come at it from the near end."**

**"Then nothing has gone over the shelf behind your book at any hour of it, and I can see all of it from where I am sitting without asking anybody a single thing about it,"** said the man of about thirty-nine.

He looked at the head of the slate from the far end of the bench for the best part of the morning while his own two hands stayed where they were, and the slate was turned away from him at the shoulder of the shelf, and he did not go round the bench to get at it, and he did not ask the woman of about twenty-nine who keeps a public register one thing about what stood at the back of the shelf. The keeper went at the shelf herself twice while her own hands were on the book, and her eyes went to the back of it both times, and the eyes were hers and the hands on the far edge of the book were not. From the one window the box of chalk on the step below could be seen with its lid standing off it, and nobody went down the stair and took anything out of it at any hour of the morning.

About the ninth hour the man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board took his own two hands off the far edge of the book, went down to the row and stood at his own trestle with his own board still standing out in the open on it, and he neither turned it nor laid a cloth on it, and he stayed down there until the light went off the row.

---

He came up the stair at about the tenth hour and went out to his own trestle and put the flat of his own hand on the wet face of his own board near the top of it, and when he took the hand away the wood under it was standing pale and dry about the size of his own palm while the wet was standing dark all round it, and the pale place would be there for as long as the face of the board was out in the row.
