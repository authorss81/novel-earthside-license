# Chapter 0938 — A Crack Filled With Another Stone

The room over the market was empty at about the fourth hour, and the man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall two stalls along came up the stair at the back of the store and sat down at the near end of the bench with his own board on edge against his own knee, and the second book lay open on the wood in front of him, its cover slab off and the leaf under it bare, and he put his own hand down flat on the bench about a hand's width from the edge of it and did not touch it.

It was Monday, the six hundred and twenty-third day of the Bare Month. The frost of the night had not gone off the roofs by the fourth hour and the flags inside the room were grey in the light and the window was still blue. The day in chalk stood out on the outside of that door at the foot of the stair, and the board under the day had nothing on it and stood clear of the wood. The bar lay on the top step out of its two sockets with the frost standing along the whole length of it, and the two sockets on the far side of the door below were empty. The register lay open at the far end of that bench and the dry cloth lay along the head of the leaf, and on the shelf behind the wood there was the register's dust-mark and the clean-edged place where the second book had stood before it came out to the bench.

---

He sat there on his own with it for the better part of a quarter of an hour before any two feet came up that stair, and he went over the whole of it and read nothing and had no line out of it. What went through him while he was looking at it was the morning the book came onto that bench and the way the room went on round it as if it had stood there for years, and the face of his own board lying face in against a trestle two stalls along him with the rate at the foot of it wanting its day. He thought of carrying that book up to his own trestle and setting it on his own board face out among the rest of his rates, and took the thought and set it aside, and took his own hand off the bench and put it back where it had been.

Running the whole length of the column that holds figures there was a crack. It went from the near edge of the leaf to the far one, straight, and about as deep as a thumbnail, and it had been filled. The fill was a different stone from the stone the rest of that leaf was cut out of: paler than it, harder than it, and it stood a hair proud of the surface round it, and there were the marks of something small and hard that had been driven into it and gone over afterwards with a stone until it was flush. Whoever had done it had done it well and had done it a long time ago, because the fill and the stone round it had gone the same colour with each other and the edge of the crack could not be got at with a thumbnail at the near end of it. Nobody said a word about who had filled it or when or why. He looked at it for a while longer and did not put his own hand on it, and he said nothing about it to anybody at any hour of that day.

---

The room filled after that the way it fills every morning. The keeper came up and went to the far end and squared her own leaf against the fold. The man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board came up with his own board and the cloth over his own shoulder and stood at the near end with his own hand on the wood. The man of about twenty-seven came up with the bucket and set it at the leg of the table. The man of about thirty-one who carries things for a living came up and put the satchel against the near wall. The woman of about fifty-two came up and went to her own wall and put her own hands at the sides of her dress, and the stallholder got up off the bench and stood at the far end of it with the room between his own shoulder and the book and did not come back to the near end of that bench for the rest of the day.

**"The damp has got into the bare back of my own board overnight and come out of it in a dark line along the foot, and it will not come off with a dry cloth and I will not have it out with a wet one, and the face of it stays face in against the trestle and the rate at the foot of it goes on wanting its day."** said the man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall two stalls along, at the far end of the bench with his own board against his own hip.

---

By the light's going the damp of that day had got into the pale fill along the edge of the leaf, and the fill had gone the same colour as the stone it sat in, and by then there was nothing whatever you could see in that book to say it had ever been mended.
