# Chapter 0941 — A Question In The Ordinary Course

A hand came off the top of a board that stood against the end of that bench with its face out to the light, went down onto the wood beside it and stayed there while a question went along the room, and the question was asked at the foot of the middle column of that board.

It was Thursday, the six hundred and twenty-sixth day of the Bare Month. There had been rain in off the flats in the night and it had gone off again before the light, and the joints of the flags inside the room stood dark and wet while the walking spaces between them had begun to dry pale about the middle. The daylight came in under the awning at that store's end and lay along the bench from the far end to about the middle of it. The bar lay on the top step, out of its two sockets, with the wet still standing in the length of it, and on the outside of the door at the foot of that stair the day in chalk stood white on the wood and the bare piece of board under that day stood clear of it and empty. The box of chalk stood on the table inside with its lid off at one corner and the two sticks lying in it one across the other.

The woman of about twenty-nine who keeps a public register had the far end of that bench with her own register open in front of her and the dry cloth laid along the head of the leaf. The second book lay on the wood in the middle of it with its cover slab drawn back off the leaf under it and the leaf lying flat. The man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board had the near end with his own board propped against the end of the bench, the face of it out to the room and the cloth folded over his own shoulder. The woman of about fifty-two kept the far wall with the stool standing against the wood about a foot off her own skirt, and her own hands where she keeps them. The man of about twenty-seven had his bucket down on the flags by the leg of the table with the rag folded over its rim. The man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall two stalls along sat in the middle of that bench with his own board flat across his own knees. Against the near wall stood the man of about thirty-one who carries things for a living, with the satchel held up under his own arm. Adrian Vale had the near end of the bench with his own hand flat on the wood and nothing in his other one.

---

Questions went along that bench in the ordinary course of that hour, and about four people in that room answered them and about four people said nothing at all, and nothing in that room stopped for any of it. A question went to the keeper about the dry cloth and was answered in about as many words and the cloth stayed where it was. A question went to the man of about twenty-seven about the step and was not answered at all and he carried the bucket down the stair and brought it back up with the water standing flat in it. The light came further along the wood as the hour went on and reached the middle of the bench and stopped there.

**"I have been at this end of that bench every morning of this week and I cannot see the face of that leaf from where I stand, and there is a line on it, and I have wanted to ask one thing about the hand that put it there and not what it says: whether that hand stops at the end of a line or carries on past the end of it into the next thing. I am asking it in the middle of a morning and not waiting on it, and if you do not know then say that you do not know and I will go back to my own board."** said the man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board, at the near end with his own hand on the wood and his own board propped against the end of the bench beside him.

He answered it.

He answered it in about as long as the question had taken and no more, and about four people in that room went on with what they had in their own hands while he was saying it, and the man of about twenty-seven went down the stair with the bucket and came back up it with the water standing flat in it, and the keeper turned a leaf and squared it against the fold. The man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board waited for the end of it the way he waits for anything, and then he took the cloth off his own shoulder and put the cloth on the face of his own board and went from the top of the top column down to the foot of it, and the three columns on that board were set out along the bottom of it in the order they had been set out in every morning of that flood.

Nobody in that room acted on the answer. Nobody thanked him for giving it and nobody told him he was right and nobody told him it had not been wanted, and nobody asked him a second thing about it at any hour of that day.

---

He stayed at the near end of that bench for the rest of the morning with his own hands on the wood and nothing done with them, and when the tradesman went down the stair with his board and the cloth over his own shoulder and came back up again and stood at the same end of the bench, the arrangement was as it had been. The second book was still lying on the wood where it had been lying all morning with its cover slab off the leaf, and the register lay open beside it with the dry cloth along the head of that leaf, and one page and the other were both open and neither of them was read out of by anybody. The stallholder went along the foot of his own board with his own thumb and stopped where he had stopped every morning that week. The woman of about fifty-two stayed where she was. The man of about thirty-one who carries things for a living turned about off his own wall and looked at the flags inside the room and then turned about again and looked at the stair, and the flap of the satchel stayed hanging away from his own side. Adrian Vale got up off the near end of the bench at about the ninth hour and went down the stair and along the row as far as the end of the flags and came back up again and put his own hand on the same place on the wood, and nobody in that room asked him where he had been or what he had gone for.

By then the two rings the bench had been standing with since the morning before had gone off it. They had been two rings about a hand apart where two books had stood all day with the wet coming up out of the floor into them, and neither of them had dried where they were, and by the light's going on that Thursday the wood of the bench was the same colour across the whole length of the place where they had been.

The damp that had come off the head of the keeper's leaf had gone into the edge of the dry cloth and stopped there and dried, and where it reached the margin the run of it stood up off the paper as a hard lip about the thickness of a sheet, and it came to an end about the width of a thumb short of the foot of that margin and did not go on, and nobody in that room had written the day out again in another hand and nobody had been asked to.
