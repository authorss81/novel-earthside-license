# Chapter 0924 — Two Boards Across A Walking Space

A hand went flat on the top of a trestle at the store's end of that market and stayed on it, and the hand belonged to a man who had not written anything on his own board for about nine years.

It was Monday, the six hundred and ninth day of the Bare Month. The night had gone still and the frost had come off the walking space between the trestles before the light and left the wood damp under the hand. The board of the man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board stood face out in its own row with nineteen rates in three columns and the cloth folded over the top of it. The walking space ran between the trestles from the store's end to the far end, and the trestles stood along it with the boards on them. The cloth was damp along one edge where the frost had got into it, and the wood of the trestle was dark where the hand had been.

The man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall two stalls along stood at his own trestle with his own board face in against it and the bare back of it turned out. The man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board stood at his own trestle on the other side of the walking space with his own board face out. Neither of them let go of his own, and the walking space lay between them doing what a walking space does, letting people through. The light lay along the row from the store's end to about the middle of it, and the flags were still dark where the shade had been.

---

The stallholder kept his own hand on the top of his own trestle and the tradesman kept his own hand along the edge of his own board, and the two boards stayed where they were with the walking space between them. About four people a day went through that walking space on their own business while the light was on the flags, and none of them stopped at either trestle. The tradesman shifted his own weight and wiped his own hand on his own coat and put it back on the board, and the stallholder moved his own thumb along the foot of his own board and left it there. What went through the stallholder while he stood was the week of mornings with his own board face in and the rate at the foot of it wanting its day, and he went on standing because the trestle was his to stand at.

**"The foot of my own board wants wiping and it will get none today,"** said the man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall two stalls along, with his own hand still flat on the wood, **"and the bare back of it will stand face in till the damp goes off."**

**"The face of mine wants the sun and it is getting none under this awning,"** said the man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board, on the other side of the walking space with the cloth in his own hand, **"and I am wiping the top of it and leaving the foot of it where it is."**

**"Then leave the foot of it where it is, and I will leave mine,"** said the man of about thirty-four, and did not take his own hand off the trestle.

Later in the morning the tradesman took the cloth off the top of his own board and wiped the face from the top down and stopped at the foot of the middle column, and then he put a figure into the space about two fingers wide there, in his own hand, and it was a rate and not a name, and he put the cloth back over his own shoulder. Nobody in that row was told what it was and nobody thanked him, and he did not read it out and nobody asked him about it. The stallholder looked at the walking space and not at the face of the other board, and the tradesman looked at his own hand and not at the row.

---

By the light's going the cloth had left a pale mark along the top edge of the board where the hand had gone over it twice, and the damp had come back up out of the wood under the mark by the time the shade reached the trestles, and the stallholder's own board still stood face in with the bare back of it out, and the piece of chalk in his own pocket had not come out of it at any hour of the day.
