# Chapter 0932 — A Question That Goes Round The Bench

Two hands came off the rim of the bucket at the leg of the table and stayed in the air while the man they belonged to decided whether to say what was in them, and then he said it, that the second book had been standing out on that bench since Tuesday last and at the light's going it would have to go back on the shelf behind it, and he wanted to know which of them was going to carry it.

It was Tuesday, the six hundred and seventeenth day of the Bare Month. There had been a wind down off the flats in the night and it had gone off again before the light and had taken the damp out of the joints of the flags for the first time in a week. The daylight came in level under that awning and lay the whole length of the wood. Out on the top step of the stair the bar lay dry for once in its two sockets out. Chalked on the wood of the door at the foot of that stair was the day, and the board under the day stood clear and empty. The step below that door stood bare now that the chalk had been carried in, and the box was on the table inside with the cloth on it and the lid off at a corner.

There was the man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall two stalls along in the middle of that bench with his own board propped against his own knees, the bare back of it standing out to the room. At the far end of the wood the woman of about twenty-nine who keeps a public register had her own register open in front of her and her own hand along the edge of the page. At the near end the man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board stood with his own board propped face out and the cloth over his own shoulder. The woman of about fifty-two kept the far wall with the stool standing against the wood where she had put it. The man of about thirty-one who carries things for a living was against the near wall with the satchel held up under his arm. The man of about twenty-seven stood by the table with the rag over the rim of his bucket. There was room in that room for standing.

---

The question went along that bench the way a question goes along a bench when a thing has been standing in the same place for a while. It went from the leg of the table to the far end and then back along to the middle and then out to the wall, and it took the better part of an hour to get round all of it, and it came back a second time before the light got past the awning.

**"It goes back on the shelf behind the bench the way it has gone back every morning of this week, and there is room on that shelf for it and nobody has to be told anything before anybody puts it there,"** said the woman of about twenty-nine who keeps a public register, at the far end with her own hand flat on the wood.

**"I have a board to get down that stair before the row fills and a cloth to fold over my shoulder going, and I am not taking anything else down with them,"** said the man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board, at the near end with the cloth in his own hand.

**"I carry my own board off that bench every night of my life and I have never once carried anything of anybody else's off it, and I am not starting with a thing in it that I could not say where it came from,"** said the man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall two stalls along, in the middle of the bench without taking his own hand off his own board.

**"I carry for money and I know what is in my own satchel and I do not take a thing up that stair that nobody has paid me to take,"** said the man of about thirty-one who carries things for a living, at the near wall.

About four of the people in that room answered it and as many again said nothing at all, and the woman of about fifty-two said nothing at both times it came round, and the man of about twenty-seven who had put it into the room said nothing after he had heard it back. Not one of the answers helped the person who had asked. The shelf behind the bench had room on it and always had. The stair was the same stair. Every hand in that room was already full of something that belonged to the person it belonged to.

---

What it came to was that the second book was still standing on that bench at the light's going with its cover slab drawn back off the leaf, and the not-acting was not an oversight. The keeper could have carried it to the shelf in the time it took her to turn one leaf, and she turned the leaf and kept her own hand on it. The carrier could have gone down the stair with it and up again, and he shifted the satchel against his ribs and left the flap where it was hanging away from his own side. The stallholder could have taken it in his own hand off the bench and gone down with it under his board, and he sat in the middle of that bench with his own hand where it had been on the wood of it until the light went off the flags. Nobody forgot. Everybody knew where it was standing. Everybody went on with what they had in their own hands.

---

At the light's going the shelf behind the bench stood with the register in front of it on the shelf and the register's own dust-mark in front of that, and where the second book had stood every morning of this flood before it came out to the bench there was a clear place in the wood with a sharp edge round three sides of it, where the air had got at a thing that had stood there long enough to keep the dirt off it. In a fortnight the weather would have the whole of that place in it again. Nothing had been put on that shelf that morning and nothing had been taken off it, and the second book was still on the bench where it had been standing since Tuesday last, and no mouth in that room had said one word about where it was going to sleep.
