# Chapter 0944 — The Bucket Will Not Sit Down

The rim of a bucket went into the low place in the stone at the side of that yard and stopped, and a hand went round that rim with a stone off the flags and stopped with it, and the bucket stayed where it had got to.

It was Sunday, the six hundred and twenty-ninth day of the Bare Month. The wind had come up in the night off the flats and had gone round to the west before the light, and it had dried the top of the ground. The crust in the joints of the flags had gone hard again with the water still standing underneath it. The low place in the stone at the side of the yard was full to the level of the flags, and standing a finger above them at the low end of it. Through the open door at the foot of the stair that room could be seen going on, the long light lying along the bench, and two books open on the wood one beside the other with a leaf showing on each of them, and the people in it went on with what they had in their own hands. The bar lay on the top step out of its two sockets with the frost standing along it that morning. The day in chalk stood out on the outside of that door, and the bare board under it had nothing on it whatever.

The man of about twenty-seven had the bucket in his own hands at the side of that yard with the rag folded over the rim of it. The man of about thirty-eight at that salt wharf was eleven miles the other way with his own knife in his belt. The rag was still stiff where the cold had got into it that week, and it stood up off the rim of the bucket instead of lying over it. He turned it over and laid it along the rim, and it stood up again.

He had a thing he wanted to do with that low place in the stone, and it was his own, and it belonged to nobody else in this market. The whole of it was that the water came up out of the ground under the flags and not off them. If a thing that held water stood down in the hole itself, then the hole would empty into the thing, and the stone under it would be dry while the thing was carried away. He had had the idea since the water first came back. He had not had a bucket that would go into the hole until this one came loose at the rim.

It did not come out.

The rim of it would not go down into that stone, because the stone under the hole had gone hard on the top of it with the water still under that. The rim caught on the edge of the hole at about the depth of two fingers and stayed caught. He went round the rim with the flat of a stone off the flags and turned it against the edge, and it cut the stone about the width of a nail in a ring all the way round. Then it caught again in the ring it had cut for itself and stood there. He got his own fingers down to the rim on the far side and lifted and pushed, and it went down about the depth of a nail further and stopped.

He was at it a long time. When the bucket stood down in the hole the water in the hole came up round it and stood on the flags and went into the bucket over the rim. He lifted the bucket out and carried it to the low place in the stone at the far end of that yard, where the flags fell away to the drain, and tipped the water out. He carried it back and set it in the hole again, and the water in the hole was standing at the same level as the water in the bucket, and the bucket lifted nothing at all. It went on not coming out. He said so out loud once, to nobody, because the step was his to keep and it wanted known that he had been at it.

**"I can get the rim down into it far enough to stand a finger and a half out of it, and that is as far as it goes. The water in this hole stands level with the water in that bucket whichever of the two I lift. It has taken me the whole of the morning to learn that, and not one minute of it has changed what is under this yard."** said the man of about twenty-seven, at the side of that yard with his own hands black to the wrist and the bucket standing down in the stone.

Nobody in that yard said anything to him about it, and nobody in the room over the market came out to the yard that day. He carried the bucket out and back twice more after the light had begun to go. Each time the rim went down into the ring it had cut for itself and came up out of it wet on the outside, and each time the water in the hole and the water in the bucket stood at the same level. He went on with it because the step was his.

By then the bucket was standing lower in that hole than it had been standing at the light, and the ring of stone it had turned against stood pale and scraped all the way round the rim of the hole about the width of a nail deep. The two flags that had been dry at the edge of the hole at the fourth hour were dark to a hand's breadth out from it. The rag was still standing up off the rim of the bucket where he had left it, and it would not lie over.
