# Chapter 0937 — The Water That Comes Back Into It

A hand went down into the low place in the stone at the side of the yard and came up with a rag that was not wetter than it had been when it went in, and the man of about twenty-seven wrung it over the bucket and nothing came out of it at all.

It was Sunday, the six hundred and twenty-second day of the Bare Month. The night had gone hard with a clear sky and the frost had come off the roofs and off the top of the walls by the fifth hour and left the crust white in the joints of the flags and on the lip of the market step, where the trough stood with ice standing in it and a crack going thin in its near end. The light came in low and level under the awning and lay along the bench from the far end to about the middle of it. The frost had got into the bar where it lay on the top step out of its two sockets and stood along the whole length of it. On the wood outside that door at the foot of the stair was the day in chalk, and under the day the bare board stood clear and empty. The chalk box was on the table inside the room with its lid standing off at a corner.

The woman of about fifty-two had the far wall, her own hands down the sides of her dress, and the stool stood where it has stood for a year with the wood behind it. The man of about twenty-seven had his bucket down on the flags by the table with the rag laid across the rim of it. At the far end of that bench the woman of about twenty-nine who keeps a public register had her own register open in front of her with the dry cloth gone along the head of the leaf. The man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall two stalls along sat in the middle of the bench with his own board held flat across his own knees. The man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board stood at that near end with his own board standing face out. The man of about thirty-one who carries things for a living stood at the near wall with his own satchel under his own arm. Almost everything in that room was on its feet, and the second book lay on the bench at the far end with its cover slab lying across the leaf, a finger off it, and a strip of the leaf standing up to the low light where the edge of the slab had come down and left no room.

---

The low place in the stone at the side of the yard had water standing in it again, the way it had stood every morning of this week, and the water in it came up out of the ground under the flags rather than off them. He got down on his knees at it and put the rag flat into the water and held it there and it filled the cloth and came up full, and he wrung it over the bucket and the bucket took it and he carried the bucket to the low place in the stone at the far end of the yard where the flags fell away to the drain, and he tipped it out and came back and went at it again, and the water was back in the hole before he had the bucket down on the flags.

He went at it four times that morning. The water came up out of the ground as fast as he took it out of the hole, and each time he came back from the drain the hole was full to the level of the flags again and standing over its own edges. What he was doing belonged to him and to that step and to nobody else, and he went on with it, and the room went on with what it had in its own hands and never once came out to the yard to look at him at it.

**"That water is coming up out of the ground and not off the flags, and there is no rag in this market that will take the ground out from under it, and I have been at that hole again and again this morning and it fills behind me as fast as I empty it, and I am going to be at it again after the light goes because the step is mine to keep and nobody else in this row has ever kept it."** said the man of about twenty-seven, on his knees at the side of the yard with his own hands black to the wrist.

**"Then be at it again after the light goes,"** said the man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board, from the door with his own board under his arm, **"and I will be in my own row with a cloth on my own board and not looking."**

Nobody in that room came out to the yard at any hour of that day, and nobody in that yard came up that stair, and the rag went back over the rim of the bucket at the foot of the table with the water standing in the hole in the stone as full as it had been at the fifth hour.

---

By the light's going the rim of the low place in the stone had gone dark all the way round it and wider than it had been at the fifth hour, because the water had come up out of the ground and gone over the stone edge of it four times over while he was at it, and the rag was standing stiff on the rim of that bucket where the cold had got into it and it would not fold over the rim at all.
