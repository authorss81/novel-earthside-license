# Chapter 0948 — A Rate That Still Wants Its Day

His own thumb went along the foot of a board that stood face in against its own trestle and stopped on a rate that had nothing standing against it, and the thumb went back to the end of that foot and did the same thing again.

It was Thursday, the six hundred and thirty-third day of the Bare Month. The night had gone hard and clear, and the frost had come off the roofs and off the trestles before the light and left a white rim on the tops of the boards in that row. The sun came over the far end of that market with the shade of the awning in front of it and lay along the walking spaces from the far end toward the store's end. The trough at the top of the market step stood with the crack in its near end quiet and no water coming out of it. The step below the door at the foot of that stair stood bare, and the box of chalk stood on the table inside that room with its lid off at one corner as it always stands.

The man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall two stalls along had his own trestle with his board face in against the wood and the bare back of it out to the row behind his own shoulder. On the other side of that walking space the man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board had his own trestle with his board standing out on it face out and the cloth over his shoulder. The woman of about fifty-two came down out of that room and along the row to the store's end of it, and went on to the end of the flags and stood there with the wind at the back of her dress. The row went by on its own business, and some of it stopped at one board or another and some of it went along without stopping at anything.

---

On an earlier morning of that month a thing was said out loud at the far end of the bench in the room over that market. It was said once, and it was not said into the middle of that floor.

**"The night puts water into the bare back of a board and takes it out again in a dark line along the foot, and I cannot get that line off with a dry cloth, and I am not going to have it off with a wet one. So the face of it stays face in against the trestle, and the rate at the foot of it goes on wanting its day."** said the man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall two stalls along, at the far end of that bench on that earlier morning, with his own board standing against his own hip.

Nobody in that room said anything back to him about it then or afterwards. Nobody asked him about it, and nobody said that it was a good thing to have said or a bad one. No day was put against that rate, and nothing was marked against it, and the rate went on standing on the foot of that board with nothing against it.

On that Thursday morning the same thing was said a second time in that row, in different words, by a different man. It was said once, and the man who said it said it into the walking space and not to anybody in it.

**"A rate at the foot of that board has stood there with no day against it for as long as I have traded on this row, and there is no reason anybody in this market could give me that I would act on. I am not going to put one there this morning. I am saying so out loud in the open, because a thing said out loud in the open is a thing nobody has to believe, and a thing written on a board is a thing everybody has to believe whether they mean it or not."** said the man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board, standing at his own trestle with the cloth in his own hand and his own board turned out to the row.

The stallholder did not turn round. He went on with his own thumb along the foot of his own board and stopped where he had stopped, and he did not say a word back, and he did not ask the other man a question about it. He did not say afterwards that he had heard it before. Nobody in that row put one of the two things beside the other, and nobody improved on either of them, and nobody said what either of them had settled. The rate at the foot of that board went on standing there with nothing against it, no day was entered against it, and nothing was entered in either of the books in that room above.

The woman of about fifty-two stood at the end of the flags with the wind off the flats going at the back of her dress. She did not turn round, and she did not say one word about what either of the two men had said. The man of about twenty-seven went down to the low place in the stone at the side of the yard with the rag over the rim of the bucket, came back up the step again, and had nothing to say about either of the two men. The tradesman took the cloth off his own shoulder, put it on at the top of the top column of his own board and brought it down, and it stopped at the foot of the middle one and did not go over the space. He lifted it off and folded it over his own shoulder again and went on standing at his own trestle. The stallholder went on standing at his own with his own thumb where it had been all morning.

---

By the light's going the shade had come back along that row from the store's end of it, and the two boards had gone over from the sun into it within about as long as it takes to walk between them. The stallholder took his own thumb off the foot of his own board at last and looked at it before he put his hand down. The tip of it had gone white, and the white had gone hard and cracked, and a flake of the skin of it had come away and was stuck to the wood of that board at the foot of it. He left it there, and he did not put his own hand back on the wood.
