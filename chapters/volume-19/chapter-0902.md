# Chapter 0902 — Two Worn Places In The Stone

A hand went down onto the latch of the gate at the near end of the shut road and found it cold and loose where it hangs, and the gate stood open on the wet stone as it has stood since before this flood, and the hand came off the latch and went into the coat.

It was Sunday, the five hundred and eighty-seventh day of the Bare Month. The rain came off the flats before light and stopped about the fourth hour, and the sky over the shut length of road was low and grey and not raining. The grass stood up between the ruts with its roots holding the mud together, and the water in the ruts was not moving. Along the top of the road on the one side a fence of sixteen willow posts stood with eleven withies bound along them, the wood dark to the height where a man's hand goes. Nobody had been on the road since the rain stopped, and no cart-track came into it from the market side at all.

The man of about thirty-one who carries things for a living went out along it with the satchel under his arm and the flap of it down, alone, at about the fifth hour, and nobody walked behind him at any hour of the day.

---

He walked the whole length of it and stood at the far end long enough for the water on the back of his coat to come into his shirt. At the far end the metalling went under the grass and the last of it lay flat in the mud as a strip about as wide as a hand with the grass standing up through it, and past that strip the ground went on flat and open with the wet standing on it out to a line on the far side. There was nothing at the end of the road but the end of it. He turned about and came back up the shut length with the wind behind him and the water in the ruts going past his boots on the same side both ways.

At the gate he got down onto the wet stone on his knees and put his hands into the two places where it is worn. Both of them took his hands the way a shallow cup takes a hand, and the water in them stood about the depth of a knuckle and was cold. The one on the near side of the opening was deeper than the one on the far side of it by the depth of a thumb. He put his fingers into the bottom of each and they came away with green in them, and he wiped them on the front of his coat and stood up and looked back down the length of road he had walked.

---

He came into the yard at the salt wharf at about the ninth hour with the satchel under his arm and the flap of it down and the wet standing on his coat, and the man of about thirty-eight at that salt wharf was at the handles of his barrow with the tally-board standing face up on the frame and its two lines of grey grit lying across it, and the knife in his belt.

**"There is no cart-road on the far end of the road and there has not been one since before this flood, and I walked the length of it and back and I could not have told you so before I went,"** said the man of about thirty-one who carries things for a living, at the mouth of the yard.

**"You could not have told me then and you have told me now,"** said the man of about thirty-eight at that salt wharf, at the handles, **"and there is a barrow to load at the tenth hour and it will want the flat going."**

**"It is the same gate and the same stone and the same two places worn in it,"** said the carrier, **"and there is nothing on the stone that would tell anybody anything, and I have walked past the gate every morning of this flood and it is the same gate."**

The man of about thirty-eight at that salt wharf did not ask him a second thing about it, and put his hands back on the handles and looked at the two hollows in the flags at the side of the yard, one of them with its crack gone deeper and one of them with pale grit standing in the bottom of it, and he brought the barrow round the far side of both of them so neither came near his wheel. The carrier stood in the mouth of the yard a while longer with the satchel under his arm and then went off up the market row toward the stair at the back of the store.

---

The barrow was loaded and went out of the yard and up the market row at about the eleventh hour with the tally-board still face up on the frame and its two lines of grey grit not broken by anything. The near wheel went over the seam between two of the flags and left a black band across the wet stone where the water in the seam had been lying, and before the light had gone off the row the band had begun to go back into the seam from its two ends.
