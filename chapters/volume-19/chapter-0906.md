# Chapter 0906 — The Face Wiped Once From The Top Down

A cloth went round the face of a board standing out on a trestle in the open, once, from the top of it down, and stopped at the foot of the middle one of the three columns and did not go past that, and then it came back up the face the way it had gone down and left the writing standing in the wet at the foot of it.

It was Thursday, the five hundred and ninety-first day of the Bare Month. The hard had gone into the morning and the frost was in the shade of the row and on the stone lip of the step at the store's end until about the seventh hour, and there was ice standing in the trough at the top of the step with the crack in its near end going thread-thin underneath it. The bar was where it always was, lying along the top step at the back of the store with its two sockets empty and the frost standing on the whole length of it. Outside the door at the foot of the stair the day in chalk had gone soft at its edges again in two days of wind.

The man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall two stalls along was in the middle of the bench with his own board on his knees and his own thumb lying along the foot of it. The woman of about fifty-two was against the wall with her hands at the sides of her dress. The woman of about twenty-nine who keeps a public register was at the near end of the bench with the register open in front of her and her own hand flat on the page. The man of about thirty-one who carries things for a living stood against the near wall with the satchel under his arm. Behind all of them the room over the market was full and most of what was in it was standing. The boards on the rest of those trestles showed their backs, and about four people a day went past the row on their own business while the light was on it, and some of them stopped clear of the walking space in front of the one face standing out and some of them went up the row.

---

**"The face has been out in the row since before the frosts and the water has been across the top of the writing more than once, and about four people a day go past this row, and some of them stop in front of it and some of them go up the row without stopping,"** said the man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board, standing in front of his own trestle with the nineteen rates standing in their three columns on the face of it, **"and I have never once found out which of them it is that stops, and I am not going to find out this morning either."**

**"There is a board on the outside wall at the far end of this market that anybody can read off the flags without going up on the step in front of it, and it is on one nail, and the hole above that nail is empty,"** said the man of about twenty-seven, at the window with the bucket in his hand, **"and there is ice in the trough at the top of the step and it is not going to be broken by anybody in this market this morning."**

**"That hole has gone its colour about the nail in it,"** said the man of about thirty-nine, at the mouth of the row, **"and it will be there when both of us are gone out of this market, and so will the face."**

The morning went on the way the morning before it had gone. The stallholder got his thumb to the foot of his own board, came to the rate that wanted its day, and left it there and did nothing more with it. The keeper turned a leaf and squared it against the fold and put her own hand back flat on the page. The man of about thirty-one turned about against the near wall and looked in at the flags inside the room, and the flap of the satchel stayed hanging away from his side. The woman of about fifty-two looked at the shelf and then said nothing at any hour of the morning.

Just after the ninth hour the man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board stood in front of his own trestle with the cloth in his hand and did not use it, and he stood there for about the length of a minute with his own hand on the near end of the trestle and then came back up the stair at about the tenth hour and put the cloth over his shoulder.

---

About the eleventh hour the stallholder came in from the row with his own board under his arm and the rain gone over him to the shoulder, and he went straight out of the room again and down the stair without stopping at the bench. The water coming off the edge of the awning had run in behind the near leg of the trestle outside and was standing in a band along the flags under it that had not been there at the ninth hour.
