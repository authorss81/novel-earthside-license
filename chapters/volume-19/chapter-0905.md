# Chapter 0905 — What It Has Cost To Say That Word

A hand went into the coat of the man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall two stalls along, on the inside breast of it, and came out again with nothing in it, and the piece of chalk was not in the hand, and he put the hand flat on his own knee and nobody remarked on any of it.

It was Wednesday, the five hundred and ninetieth day of the Bare Month. The night had gone hard again with no cloud in it, and the frost had gone off the walking spaces of the row by the fifth hour while it was still standing in the shade of them, and there was water standing in the low place in the stone at the side of the yard with a skin on it. From the bottom step of the stair at the back of the store to wherever the paving gave out the ground was bare and had been bare for the whole of this flood, and about four people a day went past it on their own business while the light was on the flags. The bar lay along the top step out of its two sockets with the frost standing along it. The date in chalk on the outside of the door at the foot of the stair stood white on the wood, and the bare piece of board under it had nothing on it whatever.

The woman of about fifty-two had come up before the light and was at the wall side of the bench with her hands at the sides of her dress. The man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board had got the near end of the bench to himself with his own two hands flat on the wood of it. The stallholder, the man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall two stalls along, sat in the middle of the bench with his own board on edge across his knees and the bare back of it turned out. At the far end, about nine feet off from him, the woman of about twenty-nine who keeps a public register had the register open in front of her with a cloth in her own right hand going along the wet margin at the head of the page. The man of about twenty-seven stood at the window with the bucket in his hand. The man of about thirty-one who carries things for a living had himself against the near wall with the satchel under his arm and the flap of it hanging away from his side. That room was full and nearly all of it was on its feet.

---

**"What it has cost me to say that word out loud in this room is that it stops being mine, and it goes to whoever in here is standing near enough to have heard it, and there is nothing in this room that will put one mark of it on anything, and I have not been asked for it by anybody, and nobody here owes me an answer to it and nobody here owes me anything else over it either."**

He said it once and he did not say it again at any hour of the day. At the far end of the bench the keeper went on along the wet margin with the cloth and said nothing at all about it.

**"Nobody has asked you for it,"** said the woman of about twenty-nine who keeps a public register, without lifting the cloth off the margin, **"and I have a wet margin on this page to get to before the light goes off it."**

The man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall two stalls along put his own two hands flat on his own board across his knees and said the second thing then, and he said it into the middle of the room and not to anybody in it.

**"There is a name for what those people did at the gate at the far end of the shut road on Friday in the rain, and the name of it is a crossing, and I am saying it out loud in this room this morning."**

Nothing whatever was done about it at any hour of that day. The man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board kept his own hands on the wood and looked at the window. The man of about twenty-seven took his own hand down into the water in the bucket and brought it up dripping and set the bucket down again. The stallholder took his own hand off his own board and put it back and did not take the chalk out of his inside breast pocket at any hour of the morning or the afternoon. The woman of about fifty-two looked at the shelf and then at the window and said nothing. The man of about thirty-one turned about from the near wall and looked at the flags inside the room instead, and the flap of the satchel stayed hanging away from his side.

The morning went on the way the mornings of this flood go. The bucket went down the stair and up it twice and the water stood flat in it both times. The keeper finished the head of the page at about the eighth hour and set the cloth down on the corner of the bench and put her own hand flat on the open leaf. About four people a day went past the foot of the stair with the door standing open behind them, and the room went on with what it had in its own hands, and not one word of what had been said in it came out past the bottom step. At about the ninth hour the stallholder said one short thing to the man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board about the water standing in his own stall trough and then said nothing else, and the man of about thirty-nine gave him a plain nod and kept his own hands where they were.

---

The two flags of the floor inside the room where the stallholder had been standing had gone pale by the time the light was off the row, and the wet had come up to the edges of them and stopped there, so the shape of two boots stood lighter than the rest of the floor with his own heel-mark not quite inside the shape of either of them.
