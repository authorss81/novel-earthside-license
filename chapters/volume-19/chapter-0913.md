# Chapter 0913 — Two Shapes The Cloth Would Not Lift

A wet rag went along the sill of the one window from the far end toward the near end and came back with the wood under it clean behind it, and the near end of the sill was exactly as it had been an hour before, and the man of about twenty-seven put his own hand flat on that near end of it and left it there.

It was Thursday, the five hundred and ninety-eighth day of the Bare Month. The sky had gone over in the night and come white again by the second hour with no rain in it anywhere, and the light in the room came off the flags in a flat way without any direction in it. The bench at the far end of the room stood with the register on it and the second slate stood on the shelf behind the bench at the height of a person's shoulder with the register in front of it, and the bench in front of that shelf was bare from end to end. Along the top step at the back of the store the bar lay out of its two sockets. Outside the door at the foot of that stair the day in chalk stood on the wood and the bare piece of board under it had nothing on it whatever, and on the step below the box of chalk had its lid standing off it at one corner.

The man of about thirty-one who carries things for a living was at the near wall with the satchel held against his ribs and the flap of it hanging away from him. The man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board had the near end of that bench with his own board propped against it face out to the room and a cloth over his own shoulder. The man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall two stalls along was in the middle of it with his own board on edge across his knees. The woman of about twenty-nine who keeps a public register was at the far end with her own hand flat on the open page. The woman of about fifty-two came in off the row about the fourth hour and took the near side of the window with her hands at the sides of her dress and her own face turned down onto the sill. That room was full and most of what was in it was standing.

---

The sill is a shelf for putting things on, and it gathers what is in the air of a room into the wood of it. The man of about twenty-seven went at it with the wet rag from the far end to the near end four times over, and each time he came back and looked at the near end of it before he had wrung the rag out, and each time the near end of the sill was the same as the time before, and at the fifth hour he wrung the rag out over the sill and the water went over the near edge of it and down onto the stone of the step, and he came back to it with the dry end of the rag and went over the near end with the dry end and it made no difference at all.

In the dust on the wood of that near end there were two shapes pressed into it. One of them was near the middle of the wood and the other nearer the near end, and the edge of the one nearer the middle had gone over the edge of the other, and both of them were as deep in the dust as the dust itself was deep and no more. A thing that has been set down and taken off again and set down again in the same place goes into the dust and stays there, and no rag goes under a shape and lifts it, and cloth only takes the dust off the top of a shape and leaves the shape lying there with its edge standing clean against the loose dust round it.

**"There is no getting a thing up out of that with a cloth,"** said the man of about twenty-seven, at the near end of the sill, **"and I have been at it with a wet one and a dry one and there is nothing in this room that will do it but somebody sitting down with the point of a knife, and I am not doing that to a man's window."**

**"Nobody has asked you to do that to it,"** said the woman of about fifty-two, at the near side of the window with her own hands at the sides of her dress.

**"I know nobody has,"** said the man of about twenty-seven, **"and I have said it out because I have been at it four times and I want it known that I have been at it four times."**

The woman of about fifty-two looked down at the two shapes for a while longer and did not ask about them, and nobody else in that room came near enough to the window to look at them, and nobody said whose hand had put the newer one down or on what morning, and nobody said when the older one had been put there either. At about the ninth hour the man of about twenty-seven took the rag and went along the whole length of the sill from the near end to the far end and back again with the wet one, and the water standing in the groove along the back of the sill where the window frame meets the wood went black and then went pale again where it dried, and he wrung the rag out into the bucket and stood it against the leg of the table. He did not stop doing it. He came back at about the tenth hour and went over the near end of the sill a fifth time and a sixth time, and the shapes were where they had been at the fifth hour of that morning. The man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board wiped his own board once from the top down and stopped at the foot of the middle column and did not go over the space there. The keeper turned a leaf and squared it against the fold. Nobody thanked the man of about twenty-seven and nobody in that room was waiting for him to be useful and he was not useful to them.

---

By the light's going the wood of that sill had been gone over six times from end to end and had been wrung out over twice, and the dust on the whole of it was off except in the two shapes near the near end, which stood up out of the bare wood whiter than anything else on the sill and with their own edges standing clean and hard against the wood all round them, and the one nearer the middle with its edge over the edge of the other under the loose dust that had been taken off it.
