# Chapter 0934 — The Date On The Door Behind Her

A hand set the dry cloth down on the far end of that bench and squared the edge of it with a thumb, and the date in chalk on the outside of the door at the foot of that stair stood behind the shoulder of the woman who set it down, and the daylight came in under the awning at the store's end of the market and lay the length of the wood.

It was Thursday, the six hundred and nineteenth day of the Bare Month. The rain had gone off the flats before light and the flags were wet through in the joints and beginning to dry pale in the middle of the walking spaces, and the light came over the roof line, came in under that awning and came down onto the wood, and stopped about the middle of it. The bar was still in the shade where it lay out of its two sockets on the top step. On the outside of the door at the foot of that stair the day in chalk had gone the colour of the stone round it, and the bare board under the day stood clear of the wood by the width of a finger with nothing on it whatever. The chalk box was on the table inside, carried in off the step the day before, with the lid off at a corner and the two sticks lying in it one across the other. The second book lay at the far end of the bench with its own cover slab slid back off the leaf under it.

The woman of about twenty-nine who keeps a public register had the far end of the bench and her own register open in front of her. At the near end of that wood the man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board had his own board standing against the end of the bench, turned out to the room, with a cloth in his right hand going over the face of it from the top down. The woman of about fifty-two kept the far wall with her own hands down the sides of her dress, and the stool she had carried out of a room a year ago stood against the wood about a foot off her own skirt. The man of about twenty-seven had his bucket down beside that table with the rag lying over its rim. Sat in the middle of the bench, the man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall two stalls along had the bare back of his own board turned out against his own chest. Against the near wall stood the man of about thirty-one who carries things for a living, with the satchel held up under his own arm. There was room in that room for standing and for very little else.

---

**"I have sat at that far end with a book in front of me for a long time, and nobody in this room has ever asked me what a line in it is for, and I have wanted that, and I have had it a long time and it has suited me. This morning I am putting a name into the air of this room for the sort of thing that would answer a question like that, and this is what it is going to cost me: that from this morning there is a word in this room for what I do up this stair, and the word will be the right one, and people who have never been up this stair at all will use it about me when they talk of me, and I will not be able afterwards to say that nobody in this room ever asked me anything, and that is what I am losing. I know what it is before I say the word and I am saying it before anything else this morning because after the word there is no way of saying it quietly, and I am saying it into the middle of that floor and not to anybody in it, and I do not want it answered, and I am not going to ask anybody in here to do a thing about it."**

She said it into the middle of that floor and not to anybody in it, and she said it once, and that was the only time that morning she said it.

**"The head of my own leaf has got a finger's gap under it at the wet line and the dry cloth wants going along it again before the light is off the flags."** said the woman of about twenty-nine who keeps a public register, at the far end of the bench, and then she put her own hand flat on the page and left it there.

The man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board was nine feet off her at the near end of the same bench with his own shoulder to the light and his own face down the length of the wood, and the cloth was going over the face of his own board from the top down and stopping where the middle column came to an end, and he did not turn his head while she was speaking and he did not stop the cloth while she was speaking either, and what she said was in no hand and on no page.

**"The thing that holds records apart from one another and binds them together has a name, and I have not used it in this room since I was a girl, and I am using it now because there is a day chalked on the outside of that door behind me and this is a morning for using a word in, and the name is charter. I am saying it out loud in this room this morning and I am not saying it to anybody in it."**

---

Nothing whatever was done about it at any hour of that day. The woman of about fifty-two kept her own hands at the sides of her dress and did not come off the wall. The man of about twenty-seven carried the bucket down the stair and up it again and the water stood flat in it both times. The man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall two stalls along put his own thumb along the foot of his own board across his knees and left it there and said nothing. The man of about thirty-one who carries things for a living turned about off the near wall and looked at the flags inside the room instead, and the flap of the satchel stayed hanging away from his own side. The keeper went back to the head of her own page and laid the dry cloth along it and the gap stayed open under the cloth and let the damp stand where it had got to. Not one of them thanked her for the first thing or for the second, and not one of them had a word of their own to set against either of them, and nothing in that room improved on one word of what she had said.

About four people a day went past the foot of that stair on their own business while the light was on the flags, and the door stood open on its latch the whole of it, and not one word of what had been said in that room came out past the bottom step.

---

By the light's going the cover slab on the second book had settled a finger down off the leaf and lay across it, and the daylight that had been standing on that leaf all morning had narrowed to a strip along the far edge of it, and nobody had shut it, and there was nothing whatever on the face of it.
