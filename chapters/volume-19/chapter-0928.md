# Chapter 0928 — Cold Water At The Top Step

A hand went into the water in a trough and down to the bottom of it and came up with the cold off it, and the hand went back in a second time, and the ice did not go.

It was Friday, the six hundred and thirteenth day of the Bare Month. The night had gone hard and the trough at the top of the market step had ice standing in it with a crack in its near end and a thread of water coming out of it. The bucket stood on the stone with the rag folded over the rim. The step had a low place in the stone at the side of it. On the step below the door the box of chalk stood with the lid standing off it at one corner. The frost had settled along the stone lip of the step and stayed there past the fifth hour, and the flags along the row were dark where the shade had been.

The man of about twenty-seven had his own hand in the trough at the top of the step. The man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall two stalls along stood at his own trestle two stalls along with his own board face in against it and the bare back of it turned out. That row was busy and about four people a day went along it on their own business while the light was on the flags, and none of them stopped at the trough. The keeper's bench stood empty in the room over the market with the register shut on it, and the date stood outside on the wood with nothing under it.

---

The washer worked the rag along the stone lip of the step and wrung it out over the trough, and the ice stood where it stood and the thread out of the crack went on coming out of the near end. The bucket stayed where it was and the rag went back over the rim of it, and the heat that would have shifted the ice was not fetched by any hand at any hour of the day, and the man who could have fetched it had said on an earlier day of this volume that he was not going to, and nobody in that market asked him again. He tried the bottom of the trough with the flat of his hand and felt the silt under the ice, and he left it where it was. What went through him while he worked was the week of mornings with the bucket in the same place and the ice in the same trough, and he went on with the rag because the step was his to keep.

**"The bottom of it is silted and the rag will not shift it,"** said the man of about twenty-seven, at the trough with his own hand still wet, **"and the ice will stand till it goes off on its own."**

**"The bare back of mine will stand face in till the damp goes off,"** said the man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall two stalls along, at his own trestle, **"and the piece of chalk in my own pocket will stay where it is."**

**"Then let it stay where it is, and leave the trough to stand,"** said the man of about twenty-seven, and put the rag back over the rim of the bucket.

He did not break the ice and nobody fetched a heat for it, and the water stayed cold in the trough past the tenth hour. The box of chalk stayed shut in the way it stays shut with the lid off at one corner, and nothing went under the date on the door at the foot of the stair. The stallholder wiped his own hand on his own coat and left the chalk where it was, and the washer set the bucket a little out of the wind and left it there.

---

By the light's going the thread out of the crack had gone thin and then come back again along the stone lip, and the low place in the stone at the side of the yard held a little water that did not run off, and the washer wrung the rag out once more over the trough and the water went over the edge and down onto the stone, and the rag lay stiff over the rim of the bucket where the cold had taken it.
