# Chapter 0903 — What It Has Cost To Say It

Both hands of the woman of about fifty-two were set flat on the wood of the bench to hold herself still, and the wood was cold under them and stayed cold, and then she folded them again at the sides of her dress.

It was Monday, the five hundred and eighty-eighth day of the Bare Month. The wet had come back up out of the seams of the flags in the night and the row was dark from end to end again, and the rain had gone off the flats without coming down. At the near end of the bench the man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board had his own hand flat on the wood of it and the grain running under his thumb. The bar had lain along the top step at the back of the store out of its two sockets since before the flood and lay there still. The woman of about twenty-nine who keeps a public register had put the register back on the shelf behind the bench on the Sunday morning, out of the draught off the open door, and it stood there in front of the second slate with the head of the page dry. Outside, a day in chalk on the door at the foot of the stair stood out white on the wood, and under it a bare piece of board about as wide as a hand had nothing on it whatever.

In the middle of the bench the man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall two stalls along sat with his own board across his knees and the bare back of it turned out. Beside the leg of the table the man of about twenty-seven had the bucket standing at his feet and the rag folded over the rim of it. Adrian Vale was at the one window with his own hand flat on the sill. At the far end of the room, with his back to the window, the man of about thirty-one who carries things for a living had the satchel under his arm and the flap of it hanging down. The woman of about fifty-two stood with her hands at the sides of her dress where the light coming across the floor came to her. Nearly every body in the room was on its feet and most of the bench was not being sat on.

---

**"What it has cost me to say this out loud in this room is a hundred mornings of coming up the stair and being spoken to in it and never once being the first one to say a thing in it, and the cost is the mornings and nothing else, and I want it said in here this morning in my own mouth and into the middle of the floor and not to anybody, and I am saying it because a thing that has been said out loud in a room with a day chalked on the door behind it is a thing that has been said, and a thing I have only thought is not that, and I am not asking anybody here to answer me and I am not asking anybody here to do anything about it."**

She said it once and she did not say it again at any hour of the day. The stallholder looked down at his own board across his knees and then up at her and did not say one word back. The man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board kept his own hand flat on the wood of the bench and looked at the window. The man of about twenty-seven put his hand down into the water in the bucket and left it there. The keeper went over a leaf and squared it against the fold and left her own hand lying on it.

**"The sill wants wiping before the dust sets round what is standing on it,"** said the man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall two stalls along, **"and there is a rag in this room and there is a man beside the table with one."**

Adrian Vale stayed at the one window with the flat of his own hand down on the sill. The dust in the wood of the sill had two shapes pressed into it, one of them near the middle of the wood and one of them nearer the near end, and the edge of the one near the middle had gone over the edge of the other, and there was no way in the world to tell from the wood which hand had put the newer one down or on what morning. He had been at the sill since before the light with his hand flat on it, and the man of about twenty-seven came to the window with the rag folded over the top of his hand and wiped along the sill from the far end and stopped when he came to Adrian Vale's hand, and went along it again on the near side and stopped again at the same place, and then wiped the rest of the sill and left the near end of it with the dust standing in it exactly as it had been standing. Adrian Vale took his hand off the wood and stood to one side of the window and gave the width of it, and the man of about twenty-seven came back along it once with the dry end of the rag and did not reach the near end. He wrung the rag out over the sill and the water went over the near edge of it and down onto the stone of the step, and he did not come back to that end of the sill again at any hour of the morning.

Nobody thanked anybody and nobody said anything to anybody about it. The man of about twenty-seven filled the bucket at the far end of the trough and brought it up and put it back against the leg of the table with the water standing flat in it.

**"There is a thing going out of the near end of the trough all morning and every morning, and it will keep on going till somebody puts a heat to it, and there has not been one fetched since the middle of the week,"** said the stallholder, **"and the man who could fetch it is not in this room."**

**"There is nobody in this room with one,"** said the man of about twenty-seven, at the leg of the table.

---

At about the tenth hour the woman of about fifty-two got up from the wall side of the bench and went down the stair. On the bottom step outside the door at the foot of it she stopped and put her own hand flat on the wood of the door an inch to one side of the bare piece of board, and held it there, and the board itself was not touched and nothing went on it and nothing went under the day in chalk, and she took her hand off the wood and went on down into the row and did not come back up before the light went off the flags.
