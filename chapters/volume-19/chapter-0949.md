# Chapter 0949 — The Chalk Does Not Come Out

A hand went flat against the front of a coat over an inside pocket and stayed there long enough to be a thing that had been done on purpose, and the pocket did not open, and the hand came away and went back to the board.

It was Friday, the six hundred and thirty-fourth day of the Bare Month. A cloud had come up off the flats in the night and had not let the sun onto the roofs before the light, and the air in that row stood cold and close until about the seventh hour. Then it thinned and let the light through in patches that came and went along the walking spaces. The frost had been off the tops of the boards before the light, and the wood of them had begun to sweat in the close air. There was water standing on the bare back of one of them that had not had water standing on it the day before.

The man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall two stalls along had his own trestle in that row, and his board stood face in against the wood of it with the bare back out to the row behind his own shoulder. Two trestles along the man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board stood at his own with his board face out and the cloth in his hand. Neither of them was at the other one's trestle, and neither of them said a word to the other one that morning. The row went by on its own business while the light was on it. The stallholder took the bare back of that board in and out of the light as the patches came through it. There was a dark line along the foot of it where the damp had come out of the wood and dried. It was pale now, and it had been dark the day before.

---

He had his own hand over the pocket twice over that morning, and both times it went flat against the front of that coat over the breast pocket and stayed there while the row went past. Both times the pocket did not come open, and nothing came out of it. The first was at about the eighth hour, when there was a bit of light on the walking space and nothing else doing anywhere. The second was at about the eleventh hour, when the patches had come and gone twice and he had turned the bare back of his own board about with his own hand to get at the foot of it. Between the two of them the pocket went down against his coat and stayed down, and whatever was in it stayed in it. No hand in that row reached for it, and no mouth in that row asked him about it.

**"It is in that pocket. It has been in that pocket all morning and it will be in that pocket this evening. It is not coming out in this row, and nobody has asked it to, and nobody has asked me why it is not. The rate at the foot of my own board wants a day the way it wanted one yesterday and the day before, and it will want one this evening. I have not given it one and I am not going to, and there is nobody in this row I would give it to."** said the man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall two stalls along, at his own trestle with his own hand down the side of his own coat.

He said it once and said nothing further that day. Nobody in that row asked him a second thing about it, and nobody in that row told him that it had not been wanted, and nobody came along and stood beside him at that trestle. Two trestles along the man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board put his own cloth on at the top of the top column of his own board, and it came down and stopped at the foot of the middle one and went back up to the top again. Neither of those two men said one word to the other one about any of it.

---

The bare back of that board had come away from the trestle at the top by the time the light was off the row. It had been standing close against the wood of the trestle all morning, with a bit of slack at the foot of it, and the close air had come out of the face of the wood and gone into it. The top of the board had drawn back off the wood about the width of a card and stood there with a daylight's line of dark along the joint, while the foot of it stood as close as it had ever stood. The dark line along the foot of that bare back had dried the whole length of it and taken the colour of the wood above it, so that the only thing a person could see to say that board had ever been wet behind was the place where the top of it no longer met the trestle.
