# Chapter 0950 — The First Mark On The Bare Board

The flat of a hand went down on the open page at the far end of that bench and went down the column that holds figures from the head of it to the foot of it and came back up again, and the hand did not stop anywhere on the way down and did not stop anywhere on the way up.

It was Saturday, the six hundred and thirty-fifth day of the Bare Month. The morning came up hard and white with a wind off the flats behind the roofs, and the light in that market was thin and sideways. It came in under the awning at that store's end and did not lie down the length of the bench the way it did on the days when there was no wind. The bar lay along the top step out of its two sockets, and the two sockets on the far side of the door below stood empty, and nobody went near either of them. The step below that door stood bare, and had stood bare since the box of chalk was carried in off it. The box stood where it was carried to, with its lid off at one corner and the two sticks in it lying one across the other.

At the far end of that bench the woman of about twenty-nine who keeps a public register had her register open in front of her. The second book lay on the wood beside it with the cover slab off its leaf, and that leaf lying flat and open and showing what had gone into it. At the near end of that bench the man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board had his own board propped against the end of it and the cloth over his own shoulder. He read the outside face of the open door across the way a man reads a board across, from the far end of the writing to the near end of it. The day in chalk stood written out in full along that wood, and the bare piece of board under it stood below the day with nothing on it. Across the knees of the man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall two stalls along lay his own board, held flat, with the bare back of it turned out into the room. The man of about twenty-seven had his bucket on the stone at the leg of that table with the rag over the rim of it. The man of about thirty-one who carries things for a living was on his feet by the near wall with the satchel held against his own ribs. The woman of about fifty-two had her own wall and the stool standing against the wood a foot off her own skirt. The corner of that room against the leg of the bench was bare, and nobody in that room had asked where a thing that had stood in it on an earlier morning of that flood had gone.

---

**"I carried that stool out of the room it came out of and set it against that wall, and it has stood against that wall ever since. Nobody has ever sat down on it, and nobody has ever asked me why I brought it out here, and I would not put it back where it came from now if a person came up that stair and asked me to. That door has stood open on its latch on the foot of this stair every morning of this flood. This morning it is standing open wider than it stood yesterday, because the wind got at it in the night. I am the only one in this room who looks at that door. I am not going to say what I make of it, and I would not thank anybody who did."** said the woman of about fifty-two, at the far wall with her own hands down the sides of her dress.

She said it once. Nobody in that room answered her, and nobody in that room agreed with her, and nobody in that room said a word back to her. She stood where she was and the stool stood against the wood a foot off her own skirt, and it was still not sat on.

At about the tenth hour she took a stick of chalk out of the box on that table and went down the nine steps with it in her own hand. She was outside at the foot of that stair for a while, with her own shoulder against the frame of the door and the wind off the flats going at the back of her dress, and no mouth in that room asked her where she had gone or how long she meant to be. When she came back up the nine steps she put the stick of chalk into the box on the table. The two sticks lay in that box one across the other again, and the lid stood off at one corner where it always stands. She came and put her own hands at the sides of her dress and stood at the far wall.

The keeper went on with her own page, and the two books lay open on that bench side by side with a leaf showing on each of them, and the hands that had been on them that morning stayed off them. The tradesman read the outside face of the door across a second time, and came away from the door and stood at the near end of the bench with the cloth over his own shoulder. Nobody in that room compared one of those two leaves with the other of them, and no mouth in that room said which of the two accounts of one day was the one that had happened. Nothing whatever was done about either of them that day.

---

By the time the light had gone off the roofs the bare piece of board under the date in chalk on the outside of that door was carrying a mark, and it was carrying one mark and one only. It was a single upright stroke the width of the chalk and about the length of a finger, gone once from the top of it to the bottom of it and not gone over a second time, with the chalk thick at the top of it and thin at the bottom, and a rim of white standing along both sides of it where it was pressed into the grain of that board. The date above it is written out in full along that wood in chalk. This one is not written out in full, and there is nothing else on that board under that day. It is in the hand of the woman of about fifty-two, and nobody in that room asked her what it was, and nobody in that room thanked her for it.
