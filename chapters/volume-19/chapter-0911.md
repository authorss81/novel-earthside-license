# Chapter 0911 — Nine Feet Off, Doing Something Else

Nobody had gone up the stair at the back of the store since the light, and at the far end of the bench the woman of about twenty-nine who keeps a public register took her own hand off the open page, turned it over, looked at the wet standing on the back of it, and put it down flat on the wood of the bench beside the book where it could not be seen.

It was Tuesday, the five hundred and ninety-sixth day of the Bare Month. The night had gone hard with the sky clear over the roofs and no wind in the market, and by the fifth hour the frost had come off the walking spaces of the row while it was still standing in the shade of them about the depth of a coin. At the top of the market step the trough had a skin standing on the ice in it and nobody had broken the skin. Along the top step of the stair at the back of the store the bar lay out of its two sockets with the frost standing along the whole length of it, and the two sockets on the far side of the door below were empty. Outside that door the day in chalk had gone the colour of the stone round it and the bare piece of board under the day stood clear of the wood by the width of a finger with nothing on it whatever, and on the step below the box of chalk had its lid standing off it at one corner.

The man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board had the near end of the bench and his own board propped against the end of it with its face turned into the room and a cloth in his right hand going over that face from the top down. The woman of about fifty-two had the wall at the far end of the room with her hands at the sides of her dress, and the stool she carried out of a room a year ago was standing against the wood at a foot's reach from her with nobody on it at any hour of that morning. Beside the leg of the table the man of about twenty-seven had the bucket standing on the stone with the rag folded over the rim. The man of about thirty-one who carries things for a living had the near wall and the satchel up against his ribs, with the flap of it hanging away from his own side. In the middle of the bench the stallholder, the man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall two stalls along, sat with his own board on edge across his knees and the bare back of it turned out against his chest. That room was full and most of what was in it was on its feet, and the light lay along the bench from the far end to about the middle of it and stopped.

---

**"What it has cost me to say this out loud in this room this morning is that there is a name in this world for a person who stood somewhere and saw a thing done, and I have had that name in my own mouth since I was a girl, and saying it in here takes it out of my mouth alone and puts it into the air of a room with nine feet between me and the far end of this bench, and from this morning anybody in this room who has seen anything at all will bring it to me before they bring it to anybody else, and I keep the book, and I have not asked one person in this room where they were on any morning of this flood and I am not going to start this morning."**

She said it into the middle of the floor and not to anybody in it, and she said it once, and she did not say it again at any hour of that day.

**"The face of this board wants wiping and there is no water in this room,"** said the man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board, at the near end with the cloth turned over in his own hand, **"and I have said that twice this week already and I am saying it a third time, and I am going on saying it until somebody brings me a bucket."**

The woman of about twenty-nine who keeps a public register had not turned round off the bench at any point of it. She was nine feet off the near end with her own shoulder to the window and her own face toward the middle of the room, and the man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board was doing something else at the near end with the cloth, and the cloth was going over the face of the board from the top down and stopping at the foot of the middle column and not going over the space there, and he did not turn his head while she was speaking and he did not stop the cloth while she was speaking either.

**"There is a word in this world for a person who stood somewhere and saw a thing done and cannot show anybody afterwards that they were standing there, and the word for it is witness, and I am saying it out loud in this room this morning, and I am not saying it to anybody in it."**

Nothing whatever was done about it at any hour of that day. The woman of about fifty-two looked at the window and then at the stool against the wall and said nothing. The man of about twenty-seven carried the bucket down the stair and up it again and the water stood flat in it both times. The man of about thirty-one turned about from the near wall and looked at the flags inside the room instead, and the flap of the satchel stayed hanging away from his side where it was. The stallholder put his own thumb along the foot of his own board across his knees, where a rate wanted its day, and left it there and said nothing and did not improve on one word of what had been said in that room. No thank you came her way for the first thing or the second, and not one person in that room said that either of the two things was right.

About four people a day went past the foot of the stair on their own business while the light was on the flags, and the door stood open on its latch the whole of it, and not one word of what had been said in that room came out past the bottom step. The morning went on the way the mornings of this flood go.

---

The pale line where the wet had stopped on the head of the page had gone a thumbnail's length further into the paper overnight, and at the end of the pale line the paper had cockled and stood up off the leaf about as thick as a nail, so that the woman of about twenty-nine who keeps a public register's own thumbnail fitted into the gap in it when she put the hand down there at about the tenth hour, and the wet had not reached the pale line again and the pale line was still going.
