# Chapter 0912 — Nobody Came Up That Stair

A hand came off the flap of a satchel where it had been lying flat and went back under his own arm, and the flap of the satchel stayed down where it was, and the man of about thirty-one who carries things for a living turned about against the near wall and stood looking at the door at the foot of the stair.

It was Wednesday, the five hundred and ninety-seventh day of the Bare Month. The hard had gone off in the night and the water was coming back up out of the seams of the flags in thin lines again, and the light came on the row late and grey and lay along the wet stone without drying any of it. At the top of the market step the trough had gone from standing ice to running water in about two hours and the thread out of the crack in its near end was going along the stone lip of the step and off the end of it. Along the top step at the back of the store the bar lay out of its two sockets and the two sockets on the far side of the door below were empty. Outside that door the day in chalk stood white on the wood again after the frost had gone off it, and the bare piece of board under it stood clear of the wood by the width of a finger with nothing on it whatever.

The woman of about twenty-nine who keeps a public register had the far end with the register open in front of her, her own hand flat on the page and a dry cloth folded along the head of the leaf where the wet had been standing in it. The man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board had himself at the near end of the same bench with his own two hands down on the wood and his own board propped against the end of it face out to the room. The woman of about fifty-two kept the far wall and kept her hands at the sides of her dress, and the stool she carried out of a room a year ago was standing against the wood at a foot's reach from her with nobody on it. The man of about twenty-seven had the bucket by the leg of the table with the rag over the rim of it. In the middle of the bench the man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall two stalls along sat with his own board across his knees and the bare back of it turned out. That room was full and had been full since before the light, and nobody had gone up that stair at the back of the store between the light and the light's going who had not gone up it before, and one man who had gone up it in the week before this one did not go up it that day, and nobody in that room said one word to anybody about either fact.

---

Nobody came up it and the room went on. The keeper turned a leaf about the seventh hour and squared it against the fold and went on with the page under her own hand. The man of about twenty-seven carried the bucket down the stair and up it three times and the water stood flat in it every time, and the third time he set it against the leg of the table with the rag still folded over the rim and the water standing a finger's width below the top of it. About four people a day went past the foot of the stair on their own business while the light was on the flags, and the door stood open on its latch the whole of it, and not one word of anything said out loud in that room came out past the bottom step.

For the better part of a quarter of an hour the man of about thirty-one who carries things for a living did nothing at all against the near wall with the satchel held against his ribs and the stair in front of him and his own face turned to it. He had walked out to the far end of that shut road on the Sunday morning and had got down on his knees on the wet stone at the foot of the gate and had put his own two hands into the two worn places in it, and the stone had told him nothing whatever, and he had not said one word about the Sunday morning since, and nobody in that room had asked him one word about the Sunday morning since. There was no way of putting the Sunday morning to anybody in that room without it being a thing asked, and he did not ask, and at the end of the quarter of an hour the satchel was against his ribs where it had been at the start of it and the flap of it was down and had not been opened.

**"Your trestle is standing at the store's end with the face of the board out and the cloth off it, and it has been like that since before the light,"** said the man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall two stalls along, in the middle of the bench without turning round, **"and anybody that comes along that row this morning will read it and nobody will tell them it was Wednesday when they read it."**

**"Then somebody will read a board on a day that is not the day it was wiped on,"** said the man of about thirty-one who carries things for a living, at the near wall, **"and that is what boards are for, and I have not got anything to add to it."**

**"I have not asked you for anything to add to it,"** said the man of about thirty-four, **"and I will not ask you again this morning."**

About the ninth hour the man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board went down the stair and out to the row and stood in front of his own trestle with the cloth over his own shoulder and came back up at about the tenth hour and put the cloth on the end of the bench beside him, and it was not folded and it was not laid flat. The woman of about fifty-two went along the bench and looked at the open page for the length of a minute without touching it and went back to the wall and left her hands at the sides of her dress. Nobody thanked anybody for anything at any hour of that day, and nobody in that room was waiting for anybody to be useful.

---

Where the strap of the satchel had gone across the plaster of that near wall, morning after morning for as long as the man of about thirty-one who carries things for a living had stood in that place, the lime had been kept off it and the plaster under it had gone a shade paler than the plaster round it, and the strap had been about two fingers wide, and by the light's going the shape of it stood plain on the wall above where he was standing with nothing against it.
