# Chapter 0918 — Two Hands At One Bench

A hand went up onto the rail at the top of the stair at the back of the store and came the rest of the way up on the rail, and the hand on the rail was the hand of a man who had not come up that stair for a long time, and one sleeve of his coat was wet to the shoulder and the other sleeve was dry.

It was Tuesday, the six hundred and third day of the Bare Month. The night had gone hard and dry and the light came in low under the awning at the store's end of the market and lay along the bench from the near end to the far end of it. At the top of the market step the trough had ice standing in it with the crack in its near end going thread-thin underneath. The bar lay along the top step out of its two sockets. Outside that door the date in chalk stood on the wood and the bare piece of board under the date had nothing on it whatever. On the shelf behind the bench at the height of a person's shoulder the second slate stood with the register in front of it, and nothing stood on the bench in front of that shelf from end to end.

The woman of about twenty-nine who keeps a public register had the register open in front of her at the far end of the bench. The man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board had the near end with his own board propped against it face out to the room. The woman of about fifty-two stood at the far wall with her hands at the sides of her dress, and the stool she had carried out of a room a year ago stood against the wood a foot's reach from her. The man of about twenty-seven had the bucket on the stone at the leg of the table with the rag folded over the rim. The man of about thirty-one who carries things for a living stood at the near wall with his own satchel against his ribs and the flap of it hanging away from his own side. In the middle of the bench the man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall two stalls along sat with his own board on edge across his knees. The man who came up that stair stood at the near end of the bench and put the flat of his own hand on the wood of it. That room was full and the greater part of it was standing up.

---

**"What it has cost me to be a person that is in no book is that nobody in this room has ever had to find out where I stood on any morning of this flood, and I have had the good of that a long time and I have spent all of it, and this morning I am putting the end of it down in a hand of my own in front of all of you, and the cost of that is that a line can be read after this with my name at the end of it, and the man that goes up that stair after this is not the man that went up it before it, and no voice in this room has asked me one word about it and none of them is going to, and I would rather it were done in the daylight with the door standing open than anywhere else."**

He said it into the middle of the floor and not to anybody in it, and he said it once, and he did not say it again at any hour of that day.

**"There is a bevel along the foot of the column on that thing and a nib will run off it into nothing without the hand doing anything at all,"** said the woman of about twenty-nine who keeps a public register, at the far end of the bench, **"and I will not look at it while it goes in and I will not ask you afterwards what it says."**

**"That is the arrangement then,"** said the man who came up that stair, **"and I will take my own time over it."**

She took the slate off the shelf and set it down on the bench in the middle of it and put her own hand flat on the wood at the far end of the bench and left it there, and he put his own hand flat on the wood at the near end of the same bench and left it there, and between the two of them the bench lay along the room doing what a bench does, holding a slate on one part of it and a register on another and neither of them going anywhere. Not one of them looked at what was under his own hand while his hand was on it, and every one of them was doing something else, and the man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board went on with the cloth over his own shoulder and the man of about thirty-four kept his own thumb along the foot of his own board across his knees and the man of about twenty-seven carried the bucket down the stair and up it again.

Otto Marsh wrote his own name onto the slate in his own hand at the foot of the column that holds figures, one word under the other, and set a day against the line in the same hand, and the bevel along the foot of the column took the bottom of the second word about as deep as the width of a nail, and not one of them read it while he was writing it or after it, and not one of them asked him what it said.

While his hand was still on the slate the woman of about twenty-nine who keeps a public register went along the open page in front of her with her own right hand, and a sixth stroke went into the column that holds figures with a day against it, in her own hand, and it was not struck, and she did not say whose line it was and nobody in that room asked her whose line it was. Nothing whatever was done about either of the two lines at any hour of the rest of that day. He got no thank you for his line and she got none for hers, and not one of them said which of the two was right.

---

About the tenth hour the woman of about twenty-nine who keeps a public register carried the second slate back the length of the room and set it down on the shelf at the height of a person's shoulder in the place it had been standing since before this flood, and then put the register down in front of it on the same shelf in its own place, and the dust on the wood of that shelf had taken the shape of the slate while it was standing there and had taken nothing of the book that stood in front of it, and along the near edge of that shape there was a gap in the dust about the width of two fingers where the bevel along the foot of the slate had not been touching the wood for as long as the slate had stood there.
