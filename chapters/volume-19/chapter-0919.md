# Chapter 0919 — A Stack That Would Not Go Through

A hand went to the near edge of the store door at the back of that yard and pushed, and the door went as far as it had ever gone and stopped against the frame, and the hand came off the wood and the door stood where it had stood with its face and the frame holding one another about as wide as a hand.

It was Wednesday, the six hundred and fourth day of the Bare Month. The wind had come up again in the night off the flats and had gone off again before the light, and the ground in that yard was hard on the top of it and wet underneath, so that the flags at the side of the yard showed two hollows in them, one with its crack gone wider across the skin of it and one with pale grit standing in it and nothing broken anywhere. The stack of boards stood at the near end of the yard with its near end still the wet end, and on the top of it the tally-board lay face up with two lines of grey grit across it. Eleven miles of flats lay between that yard and the last house on the other side of them.

The man of about thirty-eight at that salt wharf stood at the near end of the stack with the knife out of his belt and held down in his right hand and nothing done with it. The man of about thirty-one who carries things for a living had the satchel off his own ribs and standing on the stone at the foot of the frame with the flap of it up, and he was the one who had pushed the door. Out at the mouth of the yard the handcart stood where it had stood all morning with the man of about sixty behind it, and the hazel mallet lay in the bed of the cart with its head bound in cord, and a coil of used rope lay dark with the cold beside it, and the cart had not been loaded.

---

The stack came down a board at a time onto the ground and went along the yard to the door, and the boards went to the door and stood there in a row with their ends against the step and stopped, because a hand's breadth is not enough to get a board through on its side, and a board on its edge is no narrower than a board on its side. Three of them were tried flat and one was tried on its edge and one was turned on its end and stood up in the gap like a post, and the gap took the end of that one and held it, and the board stood in the door while nobody said anything and the man of about thirty-one who carries things for a living took it out of it again and laid it down with the others.

Nobody gave a reason. The door at the back of that yard stands a hand's breadth across and has stood that way since before the flood and nothing has gone through it since, and not one of them gave a reason for it at any hour of that day, and the reason was not said out loud and not one of them was asked for one.

**"A stack that cannot go into a building is a stack in the yard, and that is all it is for the rest of the day,"** said the man of about thirty-eight at that salt wharf, at the near end of the row of boards, **"and I have been at this stack since the frost and I will be at it again tomorrow."**

**"I can carry them as far as that step and no further and you know that as well as I do,"** said the man of about thirty-one who carries things for a living, at the door, **"and there is nothing at the far end of it for me to find out, because I have not been through it to look."**

**"Then carry them back to the stack,"** said the man of about thirty-eight, and turned the knife over in his own hand and put it back in his belt.

The row of boards went back along the yard in the same order it had come off the stack in, and the near end of the stack was a foot shorter at the light than it had been at the fifth hour and the boards that went onto it had the wet faces up because they had been standing with their wet faces out against the step. The man of about sixty did not load the handcart and did not go away and did not ask the man of about thirty-eight at that salt wharf for one thing to do. At about the ninth hour the man of about thirty-eight got the boards on their edges and stood them in a row leaning against the wall of the yard with the air going between them, and the water came up out of the ground under the bottom of that row and stood along the bottom edges of them within the hour. Not one of them said a thank you and not one of them was standing about waiting for a hand off anybody.

---

The man of about thirty-one who carries things for a living put the satchel down on the high dry part of a hollow in the flags at the foot of the frame to get both his hands free, and when the row of boards had gone back along the yard he picked it up off that same dry part again, and the stone under it had kept the wet off a place about as deep as a thumb and in the shape of the bottom of the satchel, and the dew that had come up out of that hollow all morning had not got into the place at all.
