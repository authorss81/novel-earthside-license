# Chapter 0916 — The Third Cost And The Word After It

A hand went to the step under the board on the outside wall at the far end of that market and took the grit out of the corner of it where the water had been standing, and about four people went along that row on their own business while the hand was at the corner, and half of them got a thing off the face of that board and half of them went past without stopping at it.

It was Sunday, the six hundred and first day of the Bare Month. The wind had come down again in the night and gone off before the light and left the row dry on top and wet underneath, and the boards standing out in the row had their faces dry and their feet standing in the damp that came up out of the seams. The board on the outside wall at the far end of the market stood on its one nail above its step with the hole of the second nail empty above it and the writing on it coming out at the height where a man walks, so that a person going past could read all of it off the flags without going up on the step in front of it. At the top of the market step the trough had running water in it and the thread out of the crack in its near end was still going along the stone lip of the step and off the end of it.

At the far end of the bench in the room over the market the woman of about twenty-nine who keeps a public register had the register open in front of her with her own hand flat on the page and a dry cloth folded along the head of the leaf. The man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board had the near end of that bench with his own board propped against it face out to the room. The man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall two stalls along was in the middle of it with his own board on edge across his knees and the bare back of it turned out. Beside the leg of the table the man of about twenty-seven had the bucket standing on the stone and the rag folded over the rim. The man of about thirty-one who carries things for a living had the near wall with the satchel up against his ribs and the flap standing away from him. That room was full and nearly all of what stood in it was off the bench.

---

**"What it has cost me to say this out loud in this room this morning is that there is a word in this world for a set of people who are in a room together on purpose about a thing, and I have got no other word for it and I have used no other word for it in my own mouth since I was a girl, and saying it in here means that from this morning I am a person who has said it, and I am standing in the room as well, and there is no way of having said it from outside a room, and I have come up that stair a hundred mornings without one person asking me a single thing and nothing that happens this morning changes that, and I am saying it into the middle of the floor and not to anybody in it, and I do not want it answered and I am not going to ask anybody in here whether they agree with it."**

She said it once and she did not say it again at any hour of that day.

**"The wet at the head of the page has come up through the cover again and it wants a dry cloth on it twice a day for as long as that cover stands up where it stands,"** said the woman of about twenty-nine who keeps a public register, at the far end of the bench without lifting her own hand off the page, **"and I would rather be at that than at this."**

The woman of about fifty-two had not turned round off the bench at any point of it. She was at the wall side with her own face toward the middle of the room and the stool she had carried out of a room a year ago standing against the wood at a foot's reach behind her, and the man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board at the near end of the bench was going the flat of his own hand along the face of his own board and was not looking at her.

**"That is what it is, and I have given it the only word I have got, and the word is assembly, and I am saying it out loud in this room this morning, and I am not going to do anything else after saying it."**

Nothing came of either of the two things at any hour of that day. The man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall two stalls along kept his own thumb on the foot of the board that lay across his knees, put it there because a rate wanted its day, and took his hand off it again and said nothing. The man of about twenty-seven carried the bucket down the stair and up it twice and the water stood flat in it both times. The man of about thirty-one came off the near wall and looked out at the flags in the room and then turned about and looked at the window instead. The keeper went on with the head of the page and put the cloth down on the corner of the bench and turned a leaf and squared it against the fold. Not one of them thanked her for the first thing or the second, and not one of them had a word of their own to set against either of them, and nothing in that room improved on one word of what she had said.

About four people a day went along that row while the light was on it, and half of them got a thing off the face of the board on the outside wall and half of them went past without stopping at it, and none of them came up the stair at the back of the store.

---

About the eleventh hour a woman with a basket on her arm stopped in front of the board on the outside wall and read the top of it with her own head on one side, and she put her own hand flat on the face of it and rubbed the heel of that hand across two of the figures in the middle of it, and the wet came off the wood onto the skin of her hand and left a smear about as wide as four fingers across the two figures and the writing under it, and she went on up the row with the basket on her arm and did not stop again that day, and by the light's going the smear had dried and the two figures under it could not be read again from that row.
