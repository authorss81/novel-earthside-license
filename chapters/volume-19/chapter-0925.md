# Chapter 0925 — A Slate Taken Down To The Bench

A hand went to the near edge of the second slate where it stood on a shelf at the height of a person's shoulder, and the hand went under it, and the slate came off the shelf and went onto the bench.

It was Tuesday, the six hundred and tenth day of the Bare Month. The night had gone still and the light came in low over the row and lay along the bench from one end to the other. On the shelf behind the bench the register stood with six strokes in the column that holds figures and a day against each. Outside the door at the foot of the stair the date stood in chalk on the wood and the bare piece of board under the date had nothing on it whatever. The bar lay along the top step out of its two sockets, and the box of chalk stood on the step below with the lid standing off it at one corner. That room was full and most of what was in it was on its feet, and the bench stood in the middle of it with the slate on one part of it and the register on another, and the light lay along the wood from the far end to about the middle of it.

---

**"What it has cost me to say this out loud in this room this morning is that I have kept a book on this bench through every morning of this flood and every line in it has gone through my own hand before it stood, and from this morning there will be lines standing on that bench that did not go through my hand and I will have no way of keeping them from standing, and any person in this room who has seen anything at all may set it down without me and I will not be able to lift it back off the page, and I will still be the one who keeps the first book and I will have to stand beside the other one and let it stand, and I am saying it into the middle of the floor and not to anybody in it and I do not want it answered."**

She said it once and she did not say it again at any hour of that day.

**"The foot of my own board wants its day and it has wanted it all week,"** said the man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall two stalls along, in the middle of the room with his own board on edge across his knees, **"and I have kept it face in and I will keep it that way."**

**"A second book, and it does not answer to me."** said the woman of about twenty-nine who keeps a public register, at the far end of the bench with her own hand flat on the wood.

Those in the room kept to what they had in their own hands. Some of those in it said they had heard, and some of them went on with what they had in their own hands, and nobody in that room agreed with it and nobody argued with it and nobody improved on one word of it. The man of about twenty-seven shifted the bucket at the leg of the table and left the rag folded over the rim, and the woman of about fifty-two kept her own hands at the sides of her dress and did not come off the wall. The keeper kept her own hand flat on the wood and did not lift it while the room settled.

**"There is a word in this world for the number that makes a body a body, and the word for it is quorum, and I am saying it out loud in this room this morning, and I am not saying it to anybody in it."**

Nothing further came of any of the four things at any hour of that day. The keeper went back to the head of her own page and squared the leaf against the fold, and the slate stood on the bench where her own hand had set it, and the date stood outside on the wood with nothing under it. The dust on the shelf held the shape of where the slate had stood, and the bench held the weight of it where it stood now. Not one thank you came her way for any of the three things she had said, and not one mouth in that room set one word against any of them.

---

At about the ninth hour the man of about twenty-seven carried the bucket down the stair and up it again and set it on the stone at the leg of the table, and the rag stayed folded over the rim where it had been all morning, and the keeper turned the leaf back and smoothed it with the flat of her hand, and he wiped his own hands on his own coat and said one thing and stopped.
