# Chapter 0925 — A Slate Taken Down To The Bench

A hand went to the near edge of the second slate where it stood on a shelf at the height of a person's shoulder, and the hand went under it, and the slate came off the shelf and went onto the bench.

It was Tuesday, the six hundred and tenth day of the Bare Month. The night had gone still and the light came in low over the row and lay along the bench from one end to the other. On the shelf behind the bench the register stood with six strokes in the column that holds figures and a day against each. Outside the door at the foot of the stair the date stood in chalk on the wood and the bare piece of board under the date had nothing on it whatever. The bar lay along the top step out of its two sockets, and the box of chalk stood on the step below with the lid standing off it at one corner. That room was full and most of what was in it was on its feet, and the bench stood in the middle of it with the slate on one part of it and the register on another, and the light lay along the wood from the far end to about the middle of it.

---

**"It has taken me nine years of standing at this bench to get to the place where I can give a morning away, and I want what is coming this morning said out loud before I say anything else in this room, because after it is said nobody in here will be able to stop me and I will not be able to take it back off the air of it. From today lines will stand on that bench that have not gone through my hand on the way there, and I will have no way of keeping them from standing, and any person in this room who has seen anything at all may set one of them down without asking me, and I will not be able to lift it back off the page, and I will still be the one who keeps the first book and I will have to stand beside the other one and let it stand, and I am saying this into the middle of the floor and not to anybody in it and I do not want it answered."**

She said it once and she did not say it again at any hour of that day.

**"The foot of my own board wants its day and it has wanted it all week,"** said the man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall two stalls along, in the middle of that room with his own board flat against his own knees and the bare back of it to the room, **"and I have kept it face in and I will keep it that way."**

**"A second book, and it does not answer to me."** said the woman of about twenty-nine who keeps a public register, at the far end of the bench with her own hand flat on the wood.

Those in the room kept to what they had in their own hands. Some of those in it said they had heard, and some of them went on with what they had in their own hands, and nobody in that room agreed with it and nobody argued with it and nobody improved on one word of it. The man of about twenty-seven shifted the bucket at the leg of the table and left the rag folded over the rim, and the woman of about fifty-two kept her own hands at the sides of her dress and did not come off the wall. The keeper kept her own hand flat on the wood and did not lift it while the room settled. She put the dry cloth down folded at the near end of the bench when she went back to the head of her own page, and it stayed where she put it, and the stallholder's own board stood face in at the other end of that same bench with his own knee against the leg of it and the cloth out of his reach across the wood.

**"The number that makes a body a body has a name too, and I have said the decision before this and I am saying the name of it after, because a decision with no name for the number behind it is only a want. The name is quorum. I am saying it out loud in this room this morning with the chalk date on the outside of that door behind me, and I am not saying it to anybody in it."**

Nothing further came of any of the four things at any hour of that day. The keeper went back to the head of her own page and squared the leaf against the fold, and the slate stood on the bench where her own hand had set it, and the date stood outside on the wood with nothing under it. The dust on the shelf held the shape of where the slate had stood, and the bench held the weight of it where it stood now. Not one thank you came her way for any of the three things she had said, and not one mouth in that room set one word against any of them.

---

At about the ninth hour the man of about twenty-seven carried the dry cloth the keeper had left folded at the near end of the bench and put it back on the shelf behind the bench, where it had stood all week, and the shelf held nothing on it now but the flat dust-mark of the slate and the cloth lying on top of that, and no hand went into the space the slate had come out of.
