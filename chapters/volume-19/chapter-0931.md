# Chapter 0931 — A Third Thing On That Bench

The woman of about twenty-nine who keeps a public register put her own hand flat on the head of the open page and went down it, and at the near end of the same bench the man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board laid the flat of his own hand on the face of his own board and went across it, and between them on the wood the second book lay with its own cover slab slid back off the leaf under it and nothing whatever on the face of it that either of the two of them had touched.

It was Monday, the six hundred and sixteenth day of the Bare Month. There had been rain in off the flats in the night and it had gone off again before the light, and it had left the joints of the flags dark with the walking spaces between them pale. The daylight came in low under that awning and lay along the wood from the far end of the bench to about the middle of it. The water had been standing along the whole length of the bar where it lay out of its two sockets on the top step, and on the outside of the door below that step the day in chalk stood white on wet wood, with the bare board under the day standing clear of it and empty. At the foot of that stair, on the step below that door, the chalk box stood with its lid off at one corner and the two sticks lying in it one across the other.

The keeper had the far end of the bench and her own register open in front of her. The man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board had the near end with his own board propped against the end of the wood and a cloth over his own shoulder. The woman of about fifty-two had the wall at that far end, her own hands down the sides of her dress, and the stool she carried out of that room a year ago standing against the wood where she had set it down. The man of about twenty-seven had his bucket down on the stone by the table with the rag over its rim. Sat in the middle of that bench, the man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall two stalls along had his own board propped against his knees with the bare back of it out. The man of about thirty-one who carries things for a living had the near wall and the satchel up against his ribs. There was room in that room for standing and for a good deal else besides.

---

The two of them went over their own surfaces the way they went over them every morning of this flood, and neither of them read out one word of either. The keeper's eyes came down the column that holds figures and went to the foot of it and came back up again, and the tradesman's own hand went along the middle row of rates and stopped where the middle column came to an end and did not go over the space that stands at the foot of it. The bench stood between the two of them doing what a bench does, holding the register at one end of it and a board propped against the other, and the second book lay in the middle with its cover slab off and its face up to the daylight.

**"The head of my leaf has got a finger's gap under it at the wet line, and I want the dry cloth along that head again before the light is off the flags, and the slab on that other thing stands between me and the window the whole of the morning, and it has caught at the loose of my sleeve every time I have reached past it."** said the woman of about twenty-nine who keeps a public register, at the far end of the bench without lifting her own hand off the page.

**"The face of mine wants the damp off it and there is no water in this room to take it with,"** said the man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board, at the near end with the cloth still over his own shoulder.

**"Then it will want it again later, and the cloth will want folding over the shoulder where it lies,"** said the woman of about fifty-two, at the far wall without coming off it.

Nobody answered her and nobody said she was right.

What had come of it was that the room had arranged itself around the second book without anybody deciding to arrange it. The keeper reached over it for her own page. The man of about twenty-seven came up the stair with the bucket and stopped short of the leg of the table and set it down against the bench instead, because the bench had the second book on it and the leg of the table stood where the bucket could not be got at without turning round. The carrier had moved from the far wall of the room to the near wall over the morning and had not been asked to. The stallholder had his own board across his own knees with the bare back of it turned out, because the face of it could not be turned up on a bench that a book was lying open on. A thing was standing where a thing had been put, and everything in that room had gone round it, and not one mouth in that room had said a word about how any of it had come about.

---

At about the tenth hour the man of about twenty-seven went down the stair with the bucket in his own hand, and at the bottom step he turned along and took the box of chalk off the step where it had stood since before the flood, and came back up the stair with the bucket in one hand and the box under his other arm, and set the box down on the table with the cloth on it and the lid standing off at one corner, and then went down the stair again for the bucket and brought it up and put it at the leg of the table where it had been.

Nobody asked him where the box had been put or who had carried it in or when, and nobody said anything about the step below the door being bare, and no mouth in that room thanked him for the carrying of it.

---

At the light's going there was a clean rectangle on the grit at the top of that step where the box had stood, and the rain coming down that step all day had not got into it, so that the grit inside the rectangle was the colour it had been before the flood and the grit round it had gone black. That rectangle would be gone inside a week, and nothing else in that room had moved an inch all day except a bucket that had gone down a stair and come back up it twice.
