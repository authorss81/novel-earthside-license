# Chapter 0945 — Carried Down The Row And Back

A board was put into a man's own hands at the near end of that bench and he took it and went down the stair with it and his own satchel against his ribs, and the flap of that satchel stayed hanging away from his own side the whole way down and the whole way back up.

It was Monday, the six hundred and thirtieth day of the Bare Month. The night had gone cold and dry and there was no water standing anywhere that had been standing the day before, and the crust in the joints of the flags had come up hard with the damp still underneath it, so that the light came in under the awning and stopped about the middle of the bench and the near end of it stood in the shade of the stair. The bar lay along the top step, out of its two sockets, with a white rim of frost down one side of it. The day in chalk stood out on the outside of the door at the foot of that stair, and the bare board under it had nothing on it whatever. The second book lay on the bench at the far end with its cover slab off the leaf and the register lay open beside it with that dry cloth still along the head of its own page, and nobody in that room had read a line out of either one of them at any hour of that morning.

The man of about thirty-one who carries things for a living had the near wall with the satchel against his own ribs. The man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board had the far end of that bench with his own board propped against the wood of it, face out. The woman of about fifty-two kept her own wall where she keeps it with her own hands. The middle of that bench had the man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall two stalls along sitting on it with his own board lying flat across his own knees and the bare back of it out to the room. The bucket stood on the stone at the leg of the table with the rag standing up off the rim of it where the cold had got into it.

---

What came of the morning was a thing passed between two men in the ordinary course of it. The man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board had his own board to get down the stair and along to his own trestle and back up again, and his own hands were full of the cloth and could not hold the top of it, and he put the near end of that board into the hands of the man of about thirty-one who carries things for a living without saying one word about what it was for. He did not say what the carrying would come to. He did not say where it was going beyond his own trestle, and the man who took it did not ask him and did not ask for it in front of anybody, and that is the arrangement the two of them have and neither of them brought it up on that morning or on any morning of that week.

The man of about thirty-one who carries things for a living carried it down the nine steps and along the row past four hundred yards of flags to the far end of that market and set it up at the trestle in the walking space, and came back down the stair and along the row to the store's end of it and back up the nine steps, and did it again after the light had begun to go. The flap of the satchel came up off his own coat once on the way out at the end of the row where the wind came round the corner, and it stood out from his side for the length of about ten steps and nothing came out of it and it went down again by itself. He set the satchel down on the flags in the yard at the store's end of that market when he was at the low place in the stone, and stood over it with his own hand on the top of it while the water came up out of the ground at the side of the yard, and the flap stayed down where it was lying and he picked the satchel up by the strap and carried it back up the stair without having opened it. That was twice over on one morning, once with the row going past and once with nobody in that yard at all, and neither time was there anybody in a position to say afterwards whether he had or had not.

**"I carry for money and I have carried this morning and I will carry it back up that stair, and nobody told me what was in the thing I had off him and I did not ask and he did not tell me, and that is the whole of the arrangement between the two of us and it has not changed in a long time. What is in my own satchel is my own business and it does not get opened in this row by anybody, and if it ever wants opening then it will get opened where there is nobody standing about."** said the man of about thirty-one who carries things for a living, at the near wall of that room with the satchel against his own ribs.

Nobody in that room asked him a second thing about it and nobody in that room was told why it does not get opened. The tradesman took his own board back up the stair himself at the end of the morning and put the cloth over his own shoulder and did not say what the carrying had come to then either.

---

When the light went off the flags the satchel was standing on the stone by the leg of that table where he had put it down, and the flap of it had curled at its own edge where the damp of that morning had got to it and dried, so that it stood out from the leather about the width of a card instead of lying against it, and it had to be put down on the stone on its side to get into the room under the table without catching on the corner of that leg, and it was still shut.
