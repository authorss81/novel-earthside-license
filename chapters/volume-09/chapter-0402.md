# Chapter 0402 — A Room Over A Market, And A Slate On A Shelf

A room over a market had the eighty-seventh day of the Bare Month in chalk on its door from the sixth hour and the light had gone off the low ground by the fifth hour of the afternoon and was up under the eaves at the market end, and the cold was out of the floor and in the grain of the table, and what was under about four pairs of hands that evening was a slate, a book open with three columns ruled in it, a second slate on the shelf behind the table with a date on it and nothing under it, a sheet on the table with a heading on it and four places ruled under the heading, and a length of new rope over the back of a chair with a man in it whom nobody was going to ask a question.

---

Adrian Vale had the sheet flat in front of him and had been reading it for about as long as it takes to fill a pail, and the room belonged to the woman of about twenty-nine who keeps a public register and he was in it because there was a page in it and he reads pages, and nobody had sent him and nobody had asked him to come.

The heading was in a hand he did not know. Under the heading there were four places ruled in the same hand, and he had read the first of them and the second of them and had stopped, and the first of them was a place and the second of them was a line, and he had read each of them twice, and a person who reads the same two lines twice in a cold room in the afternoon has stopped because he has run out of the ordinary reasons and not because he has understood anything.

The third place was ruled under the second one. There was a crease pressed down the length of it with a bone, and the crease had been pressed hard enough to break the surface of the paper the same as the two it had been folded on, and there was nothing at all in it.

---

The man of about fifty-seven came in at about the sixth hour and did not say anything, and he sat down at the end of the bench with a length of new rope over the back of his chair, and about four people in this city have been in a room with a man carrying a length of new rope over the back of his chair and not one of them has ever put a question to him about the rope.

He had been in this room before. He had been in a great many of them in about a fortnight and had been asked one question in none of them, and there is no form in four hundred miles with a person in it, and a man who has been in a great many rooms and been asked nothing in any of them has learned that the fastest way through a room in this city is to hold still in it.

Adrian Vale looked at him once and then looked back at the sheet, and looking at a man in a room and then going back to a page is not a question and nobody in that room treated it as one, and that is the whole of what the man of about thirty-one would have had to carry down nine steps to make it different.

---

"You have got that thing open."

"**I have not got anything open. It is not folded shut, it is a page with a crease down one of its columns, and the crease is not a thing anybody has to undo to read what is printed above it.**"

"It came up the road in a satchel."

"**It came up the road in a satchel on Wednesday morning and nobody in this city sent for it and the man who carried it has not opened one of the seven things he has carried in this flood and is not going to be asked what is on this one either, by me or by anybody in this room.**"

That last part was not said to Adrian Vale. It was said to the middle of the room, at the pitch it was meant for the whole of it, and about four people who have been in rooms with that man in them have worked out since that he does not address people. He addresses rooms, and a room that has a sheet on a table in it is the thing he is talking to, and a man who has been asked one question in every room he has stood in has stopped expecting the person who is going to ask him the next one.

---

Adrian read the third place. It took him a moment longer than the others had taken because of the crease, and then he had read it, and the fourth was under it, and he read that too, and the four of them were a place, and a line, and a person, and a day, in that order, in the order they are printed in, and the order they are printed in is the order somebody in a building nine hundred miles off decided they would be printed in and it is not an order anybody in this room had any part in choosing.

He put his left hand flat on the table and set the first finger of it on the paper a finger's width back from the edge of the third column and left it there. He did not touch the column. He did not touch the crease. He held his finger in the margin of a ruled place on a sheet of paper in a cold room over a market and he did not know why and nobody in that room asked him why, and a man who has been in a great many rooms in this city and has never once been asked why his hand is where it is has got very careful about where he puts it.

"You have read all four of them."

"**I have.**"

"Then say what they are, and do not tell me what they are for, because I have not asked you for that and I am not going to."

"**A place, a line, a person, and a day.**"

---

The man of about fifty-seven looked at the sheet for a while from the end of the bench, and then he said the thing, and he said it to the page and not to a person, and it was six words and it was true.

"**A person fills in a page.**"

Nobody answered it. The woman who keeps the register had her hand on the book and did not look up, and Adrian Vale did not look up, and about four people in this city who have heard a true thing said out loud in a room in this flood have watched what a room does with the next one, and this room did nothing at all with it, because a man who is right about a page in a room with three people in it has spent the whole of what being right is worth and cannot spend it twice, and there is no second turn for him in this flood.

Nobody told him he was right. Nobody told him he was wrong. The rope stayed over the back of his chair and his hands stayed in his lap and he did not touch it and nobody in that room asked him where a length of new rope came from or what it was for or what a crease meant or why a page gets folded twice before it is sent nine hundred miles to a city where nobody has asked for it.

---

Adrian took his finger off the table.

"It is a form," he said. "It was printed where it was sent from, and it came up a road in a satchel with somebody else's writing at the top of it, and there is no copy of it in this city and there is not going to be one, and a page that exists in one building only is a page that one office knows the whole of. That is a fact about the paper and not about what is on it, and I am not going further than that this evening."

Nobody in that room asked him for the rest of it. He had put it down at the exact place where putting it down was still free, and about four people in this city have worked out since that a man who stops one step short of a thing is not a man who does not know what the next step is.

Adrian went to the shelf behind the table and read the second slate where it lay, without lifting it, without turning it over and without putting a hand on the frame of it. There was a date on it in chalk and nothing under the date, and the slate had been on that shelf since a day in the Short Month with a word on it, and the word had been taken off it with a cloth in this room in daylight with a date on the door, and four people in this city have known that since. Not one of them has said so out loud and none of them is going to now.

The woman who keeps the register shut the book, and the third column in it has been empty on purpose for fifty days, and she did not close it and she did not open it and she did not put a mark in it, and the second slate stayed on the shelf behind the table with a date on it and nothing under it and nobody went near it again.

---

"She said she would read it out."

"Who."

"The woman who keeps the register, in this room, in the local language, in daylight, with the date on the door, and she said she will read it in the order it is printed on and stop at each one long enough for the people at the back to hear it, and about nine people are going to be in this room when she does it, and every one of them has been up these nine steps before."

"And nobody in this room has been told which morning it is going to be."

She did not say a day. That is what a person does when there is nothing in four hundred miles that will bring anybody to a room, and she has written the date on the door every morning for a month anyway, because the door takes a day whether anybody comes or not.

He went down the nine steps at about the ninth hour with nothing in his hands. The man of about fifty-seven was still at the end of the bench with the rope over the back of his chair and he had not asked Adrian Vale one question, and nobody in that room had asked the man of about fifty-seven one question, and the two of them had been in the same cold room for about four hours and neither of them had spent a thing on the other one, and the sheet was on the table with a crease down its third column and nothing in it, and the reading of it was going to happen on a day nobody in that room had been told.
