# Chapter 0420 — A Woman Of About Fifty-Four Is Asked Where Her Line Is

A room over a market had the hundred and fifth day of the Bare Month in chalk on its door at about the seventh hour, a Monday, and the light came in low and sideways off the low ground and stopped about two feet short of the middle of the table, and what was under about four pairs of hands that morning was a length of blue thread tied round a woman's own wrist, a sheet of paper on a table with four places ruled under a heading on it, a lump of chalk on the edge of that table, and about nine people who had all been in that room before.

---

The woman of about fifty-four came up the nine steps at about the seventh hour and sat down at the end of the bench nearest the step and put her hands in her lap, and there is a length of blue thread tied round her left wrist and it has been there long enough to have gone into her skin a little at the back of it, and about four people in that room have worked out since that a woman who keeps a thread round her wrist is a woman who has been measuring something by hand for long enough that the measuring has worn a mark.

Nobody in that room had asked her anything in any of the mornings she had been in it, and nobody in that room looked up when she came in, and the man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall was at the back with his hand flat on his own inside breast pocket and the man of about fifty-seven was at the other end of the same bench with a length of new rope over the back of his chair.

The woman of about twenty-nine who keeps a public register had the sheet flat in front of her and the chalk on the edge of the table and she asked the question standing up, in the local language, in daylight, with a date on the door, and she did not ask it twice and she did not put her hand on the paper while she asked it.

"**Where is your line.**"

---

Nobody in that room breathed. The chalk stayed on the edge of the table, and about four people in that room have worked out since that a room in this city has been about nine people for a week waiting for somebody to ask that question and not one of them had worked out that the person it was going to be asked of was a woman with a thread round her wrist who had been in the room nine mornings and had not been asked anything at all.

The woman of about fifty-four looked at the sheet on the table for about as long as it takes to count nine, and she did not touch it, and she answered it, and she answered it correctly, and the whole of the instrument in that room was the asking.

"**It is where the water stops on my own step, and it is at the second board, and it is not the same line twice in the same week, and I know a length by a thread and there is one of them round my wrist for it, and I have never once put that line on a page, and I have not come up those steps to have it put on one.**"

Nobody in that room said anything for about as long as it takes to fill a pail. The man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall did not take the chalk out of his pocket and the man of about fifty-seven did not look up and the length of new rope stayed over the back of the chair it was on, and a woman who was asked one question this morning and has answered it and has not been thanked for it has done the whole of what this flood has asked anybody to do, and she did it on a Monday morning in a cold room without a name at the top of anything.

Nobody asked her a second question. Nobody in that room asked her where her step is, how long it is, who is on the other side of it, or how she came to know a length by a thread, and nobody said thank you, and a room which has just got the answer to the only question anybody was going to ask has not thanked her and is not going to and will not be asking her anything again.

---

Then the woman of about twenty-nine who keeps a public register said the thing that is the whole of what that morning cost, and she said it in her own mouth and she said it to the room and not to the woman at the end of the bench.

"**I have asked you and I have got an answer and there is nowhere in four hundred miles to put an answer that somebody gave out loud in a room.**"

"The second of the four will take a line."

The man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall said that from the back of the room and then stopped, and the piece of chalk in his own inside breast pocket did not move, and nobody in that room thanked him for saying it and nobody argued with him.

"**And I have a line, and I have had it since about two minutes ago, and it is on no page in the world, and that is not a thing I am going to complain about in front of her, and it is a thing somebody in this room is going to have to think about.**"

Nobody answered her. The chalk did not move, and the four places on the sheet stayed as empty as the four of them had been when the page came up the road in a satchel, and the crease pressed down the third of them was still the only mark on that paper and was still a crease and not an entry.

The woman of about fifty-four got up off the bench and went down the nine steps with her hands in her sleeves and the thread on her wrist, and nobody in that room followed her and nobody in that room asked her anything else on the way out, and a line that is at the second board of a step in this city is on no page at all, and nobody in this city is going to ask her about it.
