# Chapter 0429 — Whether It Goes Out Is Asked At A Stall Front

A market at the bottom of this city had the hundred and fourteenth day of the Bare Month over it, a Wednesday, and the light came off the water and lay along the flags and had done since the seventh hour. A stall board stood on its string at the market end with the ordinary line on it in chalk and a length of good cord coiled beside it, and about four people had come through that market before the ninth hour and about four of them had stopped at it. Three people went down the road that goes out of this city on the three hundred and ninety-first day of this flood, one each with a barrow, and nobody in this city can be shown one person on it, and there was no count of anything in that market that morning.

---

The man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board came through that market at about the ninth hour on his own way to somewhere and stopped at the end of the stall the way a man stops at the end of a stall, and he has gone past the end of that stall on the whole of every morning of this flood and has never once stopped at it, and four people in that market have counted that he has not.

"Does it go out."

"**It goes out. A page that sits on a table in this city is not a page, it is a thing nine people have been talking about, and a thing nine people have been talking about is worth nothing to anybody, and it goes out on a date and the date gets written in chalk on a door like everything else in this city does.**"

---

The man of about thirty-nine did not go on his way. He put his own board down on the flags at the end of the stall and squared one end of it with his heel, which is a thing he does with a board, and he asked the second question and he asked it in the middle of a market at about the ninth hour on a Wednesday where anybody could hear it.

"Then there is nothing on it that says who drew what."

"**There is nothing on it that says who drew what, and there is nothing on it that says what anybody thinks a line is, and the one thing in this flood that has got a name on it is the day it goes out.**"

The man of about thirty-nine took his thumb off the end of his own board and looked at the stall board instead, and he looked at it for a while, and did not read it.

"**And when it has gone there will be four lines on it and no person on it, and anybody in four hundred miles who comes along after that can put a hand on any one of the four, and there will be nothing on that page that stops them.**"

---

A woman at the stall bought about two feet of the cord and paid for it and did not know there was a page in this city at all, and she was gone down the market before either of those two men had finished the sentence. A thing said out loud at a stall front on a Wednesday morning is heard by people who were not going to be told anything and cannot be told anything. This market is at the bottom and the room is at the top of nine steps, and that is the whole of the distance between the two.

The man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall coiled the cord that was left and put the board straight and did not look at the man beside him, and the man of about thirty-nine picked his own board up off the flags and did not go on his way for about as long as it takes to count nine and then did.

"Then somebody is going to be standing behind one of those four and not be able to show anybody which one it is."

"**I have not been asked which one is mine and I am not going to be. I wrote a line on a wall a long way up a road once, last, on purpose, and I have not said whether it works and I am not going to say today either, and neither of those two things is the one you have just said.**"

---

Nobody in that market thanked either of them and about four people in it have worked out since that two men who have both been in every room this city has had for about a fortnight spent a morning at the end of a stall and never once said one word about the page in the room over them, and that the whole of what was said at that stall front was said out loud where thirty people a day go past, and that the woman of about twenty-nine who keeps a public register is nine steps above all of it and was not told one word of any of it and is not going to be asked.

Then he wiped the string board down with the flat of his hand and put the ordinary line back on the stall board in the chalk that hangs on the string, and the piece of chalk in his own inside breast pocket did not move, and the ninth line and the four lines on a page are not the same thing, and the man who wrote the first of them has been the one person in this flood who has been asked nothing about either.
