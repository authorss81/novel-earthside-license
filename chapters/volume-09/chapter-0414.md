# Chapter 0414 — A Book With Three Columns In It And One Line

A room over a market had the ninety-ninth day of the Bare Month in chalk on its door at about the eighth hour, a Tuesday, and by then the light had come round off the low ground and onto the middle of the table and was the only warm thing in the room, and what was under about four pairs of hands that morning was a book with three columns ruled in it lying open at a page, a tally-board under a man's arm, a knife with the edge gone blunt on it, and a sheet of paper with a crease pressed down the third of the four places under its heading.

---

Adrian Vale came up the nine steps at about the eighth hour and was not asked why he was in the room and was not asked for anything while he was in it, and about four people in that room have worked out since that a man who has been in a great many of them and read a page in most of them has never once been given a reason to be there and has never once asked for one.

The book was open on the table at a page and the page had three columns ruled in it. The first column had a day in it and nothing under the day. The second column was empty. The third column was empty, and it has been empty on purpose for fifty days and the woman who keeps it has said so out loud in that room once and has not been asked about it by anybody since, and it is the one thing in this city that everybody in that room has agreed about without ever once saying so.

He read it where it lay. He did not lift the book and he did not turn a page and he did not put a hand on the frame of the shelf it lives on, and his right hand stayed on the table beside the open page for about as long as it takes to count nine and then he took it off.

The man of about thirty-eight at the salt wharf came up at about the ninth hour with the tally-board under his arm and the knife in his hand, because he had brought the knife up the nine steps with him, and nobody in that room has ever worked out why, and he sat down at the end of the bench and put the tally-board on his knees.

---

"You have been in this room every morning this week."

"**I have.**"

"You are not going to write anything down."

"**I am not, and nobody is going to ask me to, and nobody has asked me anything in this flood that I know of, and I am not going to start on a Tuesday.**"

The man of about thirty-eight did not look up from the tally-board. He turned it round on his knees so that the face of it was away from the room, and about four people in that room have worked out since that a man who has been asked nothing for nine years and has just been told in a room with a date on the door that nobody is going to ask him has turned a thing he keeps to the side rather than be thanked for it.

Adrian Vale read the open page again and looked at the margin beside the third column, and the margin there was worn pale in a curve about the width of a thumb and the margins on either side of the other two columns were not, and that is the whole of what he had and he was not going to get any more out of it in a cold room on a Tuesday.

"There is wear in the margin beside the third one."

"**There is.**"

"There is not any beside the first one, and there is not any beside the second one."

"**No.**"

---

The man of about thirty-eight at the salt wharf said the number then, and he said it to the room and not to anybody in it, and it was the number he has put into a column four times a year since the winter before the water and it is the number for eleven miles of flats with a channel in it that has been dry since the one hundred and ninety-sixth day of this flood and about four hundred and forty people on them.

Nobody asked him a second question about it. Nobody in that room asked him where it came from and nobody asked him where it went, and about four people in that room have worked out since that a man who has put a figure into a column four times a year without anybody ever asking him what the column was for has worked out by himself in about nine years that the fastest way through a room is to give a figure and then stop, and that this morning somebody in this room gave one and it went exactly the same way.

Adrian Vale put his hand flat on the open page beside the three columns and left it there, and then he said the thing, and then he took his hand off it, and he said it once and did not say it twice and did not say it a third way.

"**There are three columns in that book and one line in it, and the line is in the first one, and the margin is worn beside the third one and not beside the other two, and a book that is worn where a column is is a book somebody has been keeping for fifty days without writing in it, and that is all I know about it and I am not going further than that this morning.**"

Nobody in that room asked him for the rest of it. Nobody thanked him. A man who stops one step short of a thing is not a man who does not know what the next step is, and the woman who keeps that book has never once asked him anything and is not going to start.

The book went back on the shelf it lives on with the three columns ruled in it, and the second column was empty and the third was empty and the first had a day in it and nothing under the day, and the man of about thirty-eight went back down the nine steps with his tally-board under his arm and the knife in his hand, and nobody in that room asked him anything else that day.
