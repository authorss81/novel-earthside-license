# Chapter 0842 — What His Shoulders Had Carried All Winter

The man of about twenty-seven laid both hands on the lip of the trough at the top of the market step to hold the water still for the bucket, and the skin of it settled under his palms. A thin wind moved over the step and lifted the edge of his sleeve.

He filled the bucket half way, set it down out of the wind, and went up the stair with his rag over his shoulder. Wednesday had come in grey, on the five hundred and twenty-seventh day of the Bare Month, with the date in chalk outside the door below and the bare board under it empty. The room above was filling. The woman of about twenty-nine who keeps a public register was at the near end of the bench with the page flat under her hand. The man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board stood against the leg of it with his board in his hand. At the side stood the woman of about fifty-two with her hands at the sides of her dress, listening.

At about the sixth hour the keeper looked across at the man of about thirty-one who carries things for a living, where he stood at the far end with his satchel under his arm and the flap down, and she said it into the middle of the floor, not to him and not past him, but where everybody could hear the shape of it.

**"His shoulders have carried a load up this stair every morning of the flood, and his back gives him no rest at night for it."**

Nobody bettered one word of it. The man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall two stalls along took his hand out of his coat, looked at the palm, and put it back. The keeper drew the page toward her and squared it again. The woman of about fifty-two let her hand leave the bench wood and fall again, and the soft knock of it on her skirt was the only other sound while the words stood in the air.

---

**"It is wet work,"** he said at last.

**"It is,"** she said, and turned a leaf.

The man of about thirty-one shifted the strap on his shoulder and said nothing for himself, and nobody asked him to. The man of about thirty-nine turned his board a quarter round and set it down again. He lifted the bucket the man of about twenty-seven had left by the table leg, found it too heavy with one hand, and set it down again without a word. Talk went on after a while about a trestle with its feet in standing water and about a load waiting till the flags dried, and nobody in the room picked up the cost again or tried to mend it or to make it smaller.

---

Late in the morning the man of about twenty-seven carried his bucket to the step, set it down in the sun coming over the store's end, and left it. By the tenth hour the lip of the trough where his hands had been was dry in two pale prints, and the water inside lay still again.
