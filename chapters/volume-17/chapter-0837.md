# Chapter 0837 — One Thing On The Wall Along The Far Side

The man of about twenty-seven took the chair at the far side of the room by the back of it and pushed it in under the table with the cloth on it until the seat of it was well under the edge of the table, and put his hand flat on the back of it to leave it there, and set his bucket down on the flags by the leg of it.

It was the fourth hour on the five hundred and twenty-second day of the Bare Month, a Friday. There was a date in chalk on the outside of the door at the foot of the stair and below that date a bare piece of board about as wide as a hand with nothing on it. There was no place left on the bench at either end of it, and everybody who had come up with nowhere to sit was standing along the wall behind it where the shelf came down. The light was on the bench wood from the near end as far as about the middle of the floor and had not got to the far end of it.

There was one thing on the wall along the far side of the room. It was plain, and it stood in a block of its own, and it carried no mark on it of any kind. It was not said out loud by anybody, and no mouth in that room repeated one word of it, and it is not on the bare board under the date on the outside of the door, and it is not about that board.

> A door that is barred is a door that has been decided about, and a door that is not barred is a door that has
> not been decided about, and a room that does not decide has not lost anything except the right to know who is
> coming up its stair, and there is no other way to have that and there is no cheaper one.

Nobody in that room said that was right and nobody in that room said it was wrong.

---

Nobody asked the man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board whether he agreed with it. He was at the near end of the bench with his own board propped against the leg of it, the face of it out, and the flat of his hand lying on the face, and he kept the hand there for the rest of that morning. He was not asked about it at any hour of that day, and nobody asked anybody else in the room about it either.

The keeper turned a leaf, drew the page flat under her hand and turned the leaf back, and squared the edge of it against the fold. The man of about thirty-nine turned his own board a quarter round on the leg of the bench and put it back with the face of it out. The man of about twenty-seven picked his bucket up off the flags, carried it to the door, set it down on the step outside and came back and stood against the wall.

**"You have put that bucket out on the step,"** the keeper said.

**"It is in the way where it was."**

**"It is in the way in here as well."**

He left it on the step and stood against the wall with his hands at his sides, and nobody thanked him for moving it and nobody asked him why he had moved it. The woman of about fifty-two with her hands at the sides of her dress was standing at the side of the bench and said nothing whatever about the wall. With his satchel under his arm and the flap of it down, the man of about thirty-one who carries things for a living stood at the far end of the bench.

Then the man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall two stalls along put his right hand into his coat and brought out the piece of chalk he keeps in his own inside breast pocket. He turned it over once between his finger and his thumb, and he put it back into that pocket and took his hand out of his coat and put both his hands on his own board. He did not write with it. He did not go to the board on the outside wall, or to the bare board under the date on the outside of the door, or to any other surface in that room or anywhere else. Nobody in that room saw him do it, nobody remarked on it, and nobody was told anything about it, and it was nobody's asking that brought his hand into that coat at that hour of that day.

---

The talk went on about the weather and about a trestle at the far end of that market row and about nothing else that anybody in the room thought worth saying out loud. Nothing whatever was done about the thing on that wall at any hour of that day, and nothing whatever was done about the man of about thirty-four's hand going into his coat and coming out again. By the ninth hour the light had gone off the bench wood and up onto the wall above the shelf, and the far end of the bench was in the shade with people standing along the wall behind it.

The rain that came off that roof between the ninth hour and the tenth stood on the flags between the bench and the window in a line the width of the room with a bright edge on it where it had stopped, and every boot in that room was on the far side of the line and stayed there.
