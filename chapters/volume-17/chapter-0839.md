# Chapter 0839 — A Stool Out Again And Rocked Once

The man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall two stalls along laid his right hand flat along the top of the bench and looked down the length of it at a stool standing at his side with the seat of it turned into the room and the dust of the flags standing round its legs.

It was the fourth hour on the five hundred and twenty-fourth day of the Bare Month, a Sunday. The corner behind the near leg of the bench was empty, and the shelf at the height of a person's shoulder came down over it. The table with the cloth on it stood against the wall in front of it. The stool was not in the corner, and it had not been in the corner when the room had filled up. The light came in at the one window and lay along the middle of the bench, and neither end of it was in that light.

Nobody in that room said one word about the stool being where it was.

---

At the near end the keeper put both hands flat on the wood on either side of her page.

**"There is a stool at that side again."**

**"There was one at that side before."**

**"Before it went into the corner."**

**"It is not in the corner,"** the man of about thirty-four said, and took his hand off the top of the bench.

Nobody asked who had put it there, and nobody asked the woman of about fifty-two with her hands at the sides of her dress anything about it. She stood at the side of the bench and did not say one word about where it had been or where it was. The man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board did not remark on it either. He came up, stood his board against the near leg of the bench and said to the keeper that the trestle at the far end of that market row had its board face in to the weather again.

**"It has been like that all the week."**

**"It is not my trestle,"** he said, and went on with his own business.

The room had a place to sit at the side of the bench, and nobody sat on it that morning. The man of about thirty-four came and stood beside it and looked at the seat of it, and then at the corner behind the near leg of the bench, and he went and stood at the far end instead. At about the sixth hour the man of about thirty-one who carries things for a living stood up at the far end with his satchel under his arm, shifted his weight, and put the toe of his boot against the lower rail of the stool to turn it out of the way of that bench leg.

The stool rocked once across the flags and came down again with the seat of it facing the wall instead of the room.

---

Nobody said anything about that either. Nobody put the stool back and nobody set it right and nobody asked the man of about thirty-one why he had turned it. At about the ninth hour the man of about thirty-four came past it on his way to the window and went round it where it stood, and the light off the sill was on the seat by then, and the wood along the front edge of that seat had begun to go pale where it had not been pale at all that morning.

The seat of that stool had gone pale along its front edge where the light came along the bench wood and had not gone pale at the back of it at all, and it was standing a foot further out from the bench than the woman of about fifty-two had set it down at the fourth hour.
