# Chapter 0844 — Nobody On The Stair Till Noon

The man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall two stalls along rubbed both hands across the face of his own board to take the damp off it before the middle of the day, and the wood came duller and drier under his palms. He stood it up against the bench leg and wiped his hands on his coat.

Friday had come in fine, and the paving below was drying in patches. Nobody came up the stair from the fourth hour on. The woman of about twenty-nine who keeps a public register was at the near end of the bench with the register open in front of her and her hand flat on the page. The man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board stood with his board propped at his knee. The woman of about fifty-two with her hands at the sides of her dress was at the side of the bench. On the five hundred and twenty-ninth day of the Bare Month the date stood in chalk outside the door at the foot of the stair, and the stairwell kept the cool of the night in it while the room above warmed.

A boot sounded once on the paving below and went past. A barrow crossed further off. The stair below stayed empty all through.

**"The sun is on the flags at last,"** she said, with her hand on the page.

**"It will be off them by the middle of the day,"** he said.

**"It is enough to dry a board by."**

He stood his board up to the light from the one window and turned it, and the face of it showed the damp going back along one edge. The man of about thirty-one shifted his satchel from one shoulder to the other and eased the strap where it had cut. The keeper turned a leaf, blew the dust off the fold, and laid the page flat again. Nobody went down to look at the door, and nobody said the door ought to be watched, and nobody said it ought to be left. The morning filled itself with a rate still wanting a day against it and with talk of a load to be moved before the rain came back.

The man of about twenty-seven had carried the bucket up at the third hour, before the light was on the flags, and it stood by the leg of the table with a hand's depth in it, and it was not carried down again that morning. At about the seventh hour the man of about thirty-four got up and looked down past the sill, and came back and stood at the bench end.

**"The paving below is dry all the way to the store's end, and there is nobody on the stair,"** he said.

**"Then we go on,"** the keeper said.

---

The room went on. The man of about thirty-nine laid his board flat on the bench to let the sun get at the back of it, and the woman of about fifty-two moved her hand along the bench wood to make room for it and said nothing. Before the tenth hour the keeper rose, crossed the room, and eased the window wider on its hinge, and the air from the market came in with the smell of wet stone going warm.
