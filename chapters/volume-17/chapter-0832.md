# Chapter 0832 — A Hollow That Had Dried Hard

The man of about thirty-eight at that salt wharf put the near wheel of his barrow into the hollow at the side of the yard and stopped, and the wheel did not sink, and the flags under it rang like a board.

It was the fourth hour on the five hundred and seventeenth day of the Bare Month, a Sunday. The wind had dropped in the night and the hollow had been dry for several mornings, and the grit of the stone had come up out of the bottom of it and made a skin across it like ground, with the edges of that skin gone down into the flags on either side and the middle of it sound. The low place in the stone at the side of the yard was dry as well.

The awning along the store had been dripping off the row of boards under it all night, and there was a line of it along the flags where the water had come down and stopped. He wanted the loaded barrow down that yard and in under the awning, and the dry crown on the high side of the run of flags was the wrong line for a load, because the ground beyond the far side of the yard was soft. He stood on the crown a while with both hands on the handles and looked at the hollow, and the hollow looked back at him like a floor.

---

He took the load down the hollow.

The skin went down under the near wheel first, and it went with a noise like a board breaking and not like stone. The barrow dropped into the hollow with the load still on it and went over on its side, and the boards came off it and went into the hollow from four sides at once. The man got both his hands clear, stood up out of the water that came at him round his boots, and stood looking down at the barrow for the length of a breath before he said anything.

**"That is not ground."**

Nobody in that yard said anything back to him. He got hold of the barrow under the axle and got it up off its side and stood it on its legs again, one-handed at a time, and then he began on the boards. He carried them up out of the hollow one at a time and stacked them along the dry crown with their ends squared. Where a board came up with a skin of the grit stuck along its lower edge he broke the skin off against the step rather than carry it back into the yard. The water stood in the hollow where it had stood before, with the grit of the stone coming into it from four sides in a skin that would set again by tomorrow and be no more use to him than it had been that morning.

He ran the barrow up onto the crown by the long way round, with the shafts up and one hand on each, and left the wet boards lying at the wall to go under the awning when the sun got at them. Nobody came through that yard to help him and nobody came through it to watch.

At about the seventh hour the man of about thirty-one who carries things for a living went across that market row with a crate on his shoulder, past the foot of the stair at the back of the store's end of it, and did not stop there.

---

Up at the near end of the bench the keeper turned a leaf and then said that the man of about thirty-one had not come up.

**"He has not come up."**

**"He goes past that door every morning and has never once come up it,"** the man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board said, with his board under his arm and his own hand flat on the face of it.

**"Then that is not the same thing,"** the keeper said, and turned a leaf.

The man of about thirty-nine took his hand off the face of his board and looked at the door at the foot of the stair for a moment, and then he put the hand back where it had been. Nobody put a morning to it, nobody went down that stair to look at anything, and nobody said out loud that there was no way of settling it. The room went on with the weather and the trestles at the far end of that market row, and the crate went up the row and out of sight with the same man on it that always carried it.

---

By the tenth hour the piece of the broken skin was lying up on the dry crown of that run of flags where the wheel had come out of it, and nobody had picked it up.
