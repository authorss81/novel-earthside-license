# Chapter 0840 — He Said What It Had Cost Him

The man of about thirty-one who carries things for a living came up the stair with his satchel under his arm and the flap of it down, and put the flat of his left hand on the wood of the bench at the far end of it and left it there, and the strap of the satchel stayed over his shoulder.

It was the fourth hour on the five hundred and twenty-fifth day of the Bare Month, a Monday. On the outside of the door at the foot of the stair a date stood in chalk, and under that date a piece of board about as wide as a hand had nothing on it whatever. The man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board stood at the near end of the bench with his board propped against the leg of it. The man of about twenty-seven was by the table with his bucket at his feet and the rag over his shoulder, and the keeper was at the near end of the bench with the register on the shelf behind her.

At the side of the bench stood the woman of about fifty-two with her hands at the sides of her dress. At the far end of it the man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall two stalls along sat with his own board propped against his knee, and not one of them had asked him to bring it. The man of about thirty-one went down the stair once in the middle of the morning and came up again with the same thing under his arm and stood where he had been, and nobody in that room asked him what he had gone down for.

The keeper drew the page flat under her hand, turned it back, and ran one finger down the fold to square it. The man of about twenty-seven took his bucket up off the flags, carried it out to the step, set it down outside and came back and stood against the wall. The man of about thirty-nine turned his own board round on the leg of the bench and set the bare back of it out into the room again, and nobody at either end of the bench said anything about any of that.

---

At about the seventh hour the man of about thirty-one took his hand off the strap of the satchel and said it out loud into the middle of the floor, and nobody in that room had asked him for it.

**"I have carried a thing up that stair every morning of this flood and I have gone past the foot of it every morning of this flood, and I could not tell anybody on which morning that door began to stand open."**

The keeper's hand stayed where it was on the page. The man of about thirty-four took his right hand out of his coat, looked at the palm of it as though something had been left in it, and put it back. The woman of about fifty-two took one hand off the wood of the bench and let it go back to the side of her dress. Nobody thanked him for saying it and nobody improved on one word of it, and nobody asked him a second thing about it, and nobody in that room looked at the door at the foot of the stair while he was saying it.

Then the room went on. The man of about thirty-four put his own board up off his knee and stood it against the leg of the bench.

**"Put your page back."**

**"It is not your page,"** the keeper said, and she put it back.

Nobody bettered one word of what had been said at any hour after that either. At about the eighth hour the man of about thirty-nine said one flat thing into the middle of the floor about the trough at the top of that market step having a crack in the near end of it, and nobody answered him about that, and nobody took either of those two things any further than the middle of that floor.

At about the ninth hour the man of about thirty-one put his hand back on the strap of the satchel and went down the stair and out into that market, and did not come up it again at any hour of that day.

---

At the tenth hour his hand was off the strap of the satchel for the first time in that day, and the skin of the palm of it was wet to the first joint out of the trough at the top of that market step, and the flap of the satchel was still down.
