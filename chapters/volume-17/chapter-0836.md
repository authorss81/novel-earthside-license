# Chapter 0836 — He Looked At That Door And Went On

The man of about thirty-one who carries things for a living came along that market row with a coil of rope under his arm and put the toe of his boot on the bottom step of that stair and stopped there, and the rope came down off his hip and hung against the flags.

It was the fourth hour on the five hundred and twenty-first day of the Bare Month, a Thursday. It had been raining in the night and the flags were wet, and the strip of ground at the foot of the stair was dark along its whole width with a channel cut down the middle of it where the water had gone. The date in chalk stood on the outside of that door and the bare piece of board under the date had nothing on it, and the weather had not put anything on it either. The light came in past the door and lay along the steps of that stair.

He looked at the outside of the door. He looked at the chalk and at the board under it, and then he looked up the open length of the stair to the top step, where the iron lay across it with the wiping still standing in the dust of the wood. He stood there looking at all three of those things, and the coil of rope hanging off his hip went on dripping into the channel, until a woman coming the other way along the row had to step wide of him in the wet. Then he picked the coil up off the flags and went on up the market row and did not stop and did not come up that stair.

A man at the store's end of the row put his hand on his arm as he went past and asked him whether he would take a crate up to the store's end before the middle of the day.

**"Not this morning."**

**"It is not the middle of the day that is asking."**

**"It can ask me again,"** the man of about thirty-one said, and went on up the row with the coil under his arm. The man who had asked him stood at the trestle and watched him go and said nothing else about it.

---

He has gone past the foot of that stair every morning of this flood and he was asked nothing about it then, and he was asked nothing about it on that Thursday. He took the coil up to the trestle at the store's end and put it down on the boards of the trestle itself and squared it, the way a man puts a thing down where the next man can lift it, and then he stood in the wet and looked up and down the row at nothing in particular. The row was coming up for the day and the boards were being turned face out along it, one stall after another, and the sound of that was the whole noise of the market.

Nobody in that market row knew that he had looked at the door, and nobody on that strip of ground knew it either, and he did not tell anybody and he did not go back and look a second time. The rope he had brought up lay on the trestle with the water still coming out of it, and the boards of the trestle were dark under it in a ring that would be there at the middle of the day.

The rain came on again a little after the seventh hour and went along that row and into the channel at the foot of the stair and down the flags at the store's end, and the marks of that morning's boots went soft at their edges and most of them went away by degrees. The light lay along the wet flags from the foot of the stair to the far end of the row, and there was one line of prints going up the middle of those flags that did not come back that day.
