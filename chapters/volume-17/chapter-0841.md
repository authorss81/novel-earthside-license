# Chapter 0841 — The Yard Is Where A Load Waits

The woman of about twenty-nine who keeps a public register set both hands on the edge of the register to square it on the shelf, so the page would lie flat for the morning, and left the second slate standing behind it where it always stood.

Rain had come in the night and the paving below was still dark with it. A man nobody in the room had seen before stopped at the foot of the stair with water on his shoulders, wiped his boots on the bottom step, and came up without being asked and without being told to stay below. It was Tuesday, on the five hundred and twenty-sixth day of the Bare Month. The date stood in chalk outside the door below, and under it the bare board about as wide as a hand had nothing on it whatever. The man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board was at the near end of the bench with his board against his knee. The man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall two stalls along sat at the far end with his hands out of his coat.

He came into the room with his hat in his hand and stood inside the doorway while water ran off him on to the boards.

**"I was told upstairs keeps the market's hours."**

**"Upstairs keeps its own,"** she said, and did not get up.

**"I have a load of laths down below."**

**"Then the yard is where a load waits,"** he said, without looking up from his board.

The stranger looked from one end of the bench to the other, at the shelf, at the table with the cloth on it, at the one window, and found nobody standing to meet him and nobody pushed aside to make a place for him. The woman of about fifty-two with her hands at the sides of her dress had moved along the bench to let the keeper pass and said nothing at all. He waited through four or five breaths for somebody to ask what his laths were for, and nobody asked. The man of about thirty-one who carries things for a living shifted his satchel on his shoulder at the far end and looked out of the window.

---

He tried once more, turning his hat round in his hands.

**"Is there somebody here to speak to about storage."**

**"There is nobody here for storage,"** she said, and squared the page again with the flat of her hand.

He set his hat on his head again and went back down the way he had come, with the stair taking the water off his boots, and nobody went to the head of the stair to call after him or to the window to see him out, and nobody said he should have stayed.

The morning went on with the damp on the boards where he had stood going dull at the edges. The man of about thirty-nine laid his board flat to dry the back of it, and the keeper turned a leaf, found the place she wanted, and ran a finger down the fold. The man of about thirty-four asked her whether the rate on the board was the one that was on it yesterday, and she said it was, and he turned his own board over and set it against the bench leg.

---

By the eighth hour the keeper sent the man of about twenty-seven down with the bucket to empty it at the trough, and he brought it up full and set it by the table leg. He crossed to the window with the rag, wiped the sill where rain had come in, and wrung the rag out over the bucket before folding it on the table edge, and the light moved off the middle of the bench.
