# Chapter 0834 — The Lid Ajar And Nothing Gone

The keeper stood at the top of that market step with both hands on the box of chalk on either side of it and looked down into it, and the lid was standing ajar on its pin, and the chalk was standing in the box with the dust of the step lying on the top of it.

It was the fourth hour on the five hundred and nineteenth day of the Bare Month, a Tuesday. The sun had been off that step for the better part of an hour and the near corner of it was still dark with the wet, and the trough beside it stood with its rim dark all round. The board on the outside wall at the far end of that market carried its three sets of figures with nothing under any of them, and nothing had been gone near that stone since a cloth had been over it. Nothing had been walked off that step, because nothing on that step had been walked off it in any morning anybody could remember.

She lifted the chalk out of the box and held it in her own hand and looked at it. There was a clean crescent in the dust of the step at the near side of the box where a thumb had been, and the dust of the step lay on the top of the chalk itself and not in the hollow of her hand. She put the chalk back the same way up and brought the lid down with her thumb on the face of it. The lid came down to within a finger of the rim and stopped on the same pin that it had always stopped on.

The man of about twenty-seven came up that step with his bucket in his hand and filled it out of the trough while she stood there, and the water came up brown and then cleared.

**"It will keep the brown in it till the light is on it."**

**"It has not done so yet,"** she said, and stood with her hands on the box.

He took the bucket down off the step and went on along the row with it, dripping, and she wiped the rim of the trough with the flat of her hand and left the box where it stood.

---

The man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall two stalls along came along that market row with his own board face in and both his hands in his coat, and stopped at the step and put his head on one side and looked at the box.

**"That box belongs to the room over this step and there is no reason on earth for it to be standing out here."**

**"There has been no reason on earth for it to be standing out here for as long as it has been standing out here,"** the man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board said, coming down the row with his board under his arm.

**"It stands out here because nobody has ever brought it in."**

**"It stands out here because that market row uses it where it stands."**

The keeper picked the box up off the step and set it down again with the lid at the same angle, and she said nothing about it to either of them. The man of about thirty-four's hands stayed in his coat where they had been the whole morning. Nobody agreed with anybody, nobody argued with anybody about it past that, and nobody improved on one word of what the other had said. Nobody thanked either of them for saying anything at all.

They went on up the row together without settling it. The man of about thirty-nine went on down the market with his board under his arm, and the box of chalk stayed on that step with its lid standing where it had always stood. The man of about thirty-four got his board square at his own trestle and stood it face in with the bare back turned out into the row, and the sun came off the step and took the damp out of the near corner of the stone at last.

---

By the tenth hour the dust of that step lay on the top of that chalk with the print of a thumb in it and the print of a second thumb across the first one, and the lid of that box stood at the angle it had stood at before any of them had said anything.
