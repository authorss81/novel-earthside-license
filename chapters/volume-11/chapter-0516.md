# Chapter 0516 — The Seventh Hour At The End Of That Row And A Hand On A Wall

The board came off its two nails at the fourth hour on the two hundred and first day of the Bare Month, a Saturday, and went into the trough that was standing against that wall on its own spot again, and came out of the water with all three sets of figures on it and none of the three rubbed out, and went back up on its two nails. The man of about twenty-seven who washes that board put the rag on the stone lip of the step, left the bucket standing in the trough, and went and sat down at the end of the row on an upturned crate with his hands hanging over his knees. He had been on that crate at that end of the market every seventh hour of every day for as long as anybody there had been counting.

---

At about the seventh hour that end of the market filled the way it fills, and the man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall had his own board face in against his own trestle two stalls along with the bare back of it out in the row, and he was doing the thing he does to that trestle. The man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board came down the row with his own board under his arm and the face of it out, and stopped about six paces short of that wall and turned round so that his back was to it. Nobody in that row said one word to the man on the crate, and about four people at that end of the market had worked out that he sits there and about four had not, and not one of them in either half had ever said so to him.

He said it himself, at about the seventh hour, to the trough and not to the row, and he said it once.

"**I have not washed that wall and I am not going to and I am not going to say why.**"

A woman at the end of the row put her hand flat on the top of her own board and said the thing that is said, to the flags.

"Nobody had asked you."

"**Nobody had asked me and I have said it anyway, and I would rather have said it out loud than had this row decide between them that I had never noticed it.**"

Nobody asked him why. Nobody improved on what he had said and nobody argued with it, and about four people at that end of the market went on with what they were doing. The man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall kept his own hands on the frame of his trestle and said nothing at all about the wall above that board, and the man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board stayed with his back to it and looked down the row, and about four people in that row had worked out that a man had said a thing about that wall and about four had not, and not one of the two of them asked him a second question.

---

The light came off the flags at the end of that row at about the ninth hour and went up that wall in one piece, the way it does when there is no cloud off the low ground, and it stopped about the height of a man's shoulder and did not go any further that evening, and it has not gone any further on any morning of this flood. At about the ninth hour the man of about twenty-seven who washes that board got up off his crate, put his hand flat on the wall above the top of that board, held it there while the last of that light was on the roofs and a man went past him on the flags, and then took it off again without a rag and without water and without saying anything at all.

The wall above that board is as dirty at the ninth hour on this evening as it is at the fourth hour on any morning of this flood. The rag is in the trough and not on the stone lip, where it has been every morning since the seventh week of this flood, and the bucket is standing in the trough behind him with the water in it going down about a finger every minute.
