# Chapter 0508 — Two Figures That Do Not Agree And A Slate Put Back In A Satchel

The hundred and ninety-third day of the Bare Month was in chalk on the door at the back of the room over that market, a Friday, and about nine people were in that room by about the tenth hour and none of them had been sent for anything. The register was on its shelf behind the bench with its spine to the wall and the cover shut, and above it on the same shelf there were two sheets of paper standing on their edges against the wall, one of them with a figure in its one place and one of them with its one place empty, and they were not laid beside each other and were not compared out loud by anybody in that room and were not in one hand.

---

There is a working slate that goes in and out of that room on a barrow and there is another one that belongs to the woman of about twenty-nine who keeps a public register and stands on the bench under the window. The second one of those has a long count made on foot on it. It is a count of one kind of thing across eleven miles of flats, and it was made by a person walking with a slate under one arm and a tin in the two hands, and the figure on that slate and the figure on the other face of it are of the same kind of thing and are not the same figure, and neither of them has been rubbed out.

On the morning of the hundred and ninety-third day of the Bare Month the man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board came up the stair with his own board under his arm and put it against the wall by the door and went back down the stair and up it again with a lump of chalk off the step at the bottom, and wrote the figures off the store's board onto the back of that slate, and put the chalk back on the step where he had got it. The board on the wall at the bottom of that stair has three sets of figures on it and none of the three has been rubbed out.

---

The two faces of that slate were not the same and the two did not agree. A man at the bench put his thumb on the place where the two met and said the thing that is said to the table.

"That is the same street counted twice and it has come out two ways."

"**I have watched it come out three ways and I have watched the weather be the same coming off the low ground on two of those mornings, and I am not going to say what makes the difference, because I do not know, and there are about four of you in this room who do and about four of you who do not, and not one of the two of them is going to say it out loud this morning.**"

Nobody in that room said it out loud. The woman of about twenty-nine who keeps a public register put her hand flat on the face of that slate with the long count on it and left it there for a while and did not write one word on it.

---

A woman of about fifty-two came up the stair at the back of that room at about the middle of the morning and sat down at the end of the bench. Nobody in that room asked her one question about anything at all, and nobody in that room opened her door, and she said the four sentences about what she was asked and said nothing else.

"**I was asked for the days of my house, and I gave them the days of my house and not a rota, and that is the whole of what happened on a step at the end of that lane. A house is a thing that stays where it is and a rota is a thing that goes round, and there is no instrument in four hundred miles that has ever turned the one of them into the other one.**"

"**I said that out loud on that step in about four seconds and nobody argued with me about it and nobody thanked me for it either.**"

"**Nobody in this room is going to ask me a second question and I would rather say so here than sit here waiting to be asked one.**"

A man at the bench said the thing that is said to the table and not to her.

"You have said four sentences in a room this morning and you have not once said what you were asked in front of anybody."

"**I have said what I was asked in front of everybody in this room and that is twice more than the man who asked me said it in front of anybody, and I am not going to say a fifth sentence in this room today.**"

Nobody asked her a second question. Two men at the far end of that bench half turned towards her and did not finish turning, and the man nearest the door got up off the bench and went out of the room and down that stair and came back up it about as long as it takes a man to count nine later and sat down again in the same place. The man who had gone out brought the cold in off that stair with him and put his hands flat on the table in front of him and left them there, and neither of those two men said one word to her or to anybody else. Her door is at the end of a lane past two gates and it was shut all day and nobody in this city has ever been asked to open it.

---

At the end of the bench the man of about fifty-seven sat with his hands flat on the table in front of him and a length of new rope over the back of his chair, and it had been over the back of that chair every morning of this flood. It was not cut and it was not taken up and no hand in that room lifted it off that chair and no mouth at that table said one word about it, and nobody asked that man anything at all. He was not asked where the fence is and he was not told anything about it.

Nobody in that room was shown anybody on a road. Out of this city, past the last named house, a road goes up and there are three barrows on it, and they went down that road with a barrow each, and a hundred and seventeen days out at that moment, measured from the day they went, which is the three hundred and ninety-first day of this flood. There is not one person on that road anybody in this city can be shown, and there has never been, and a party of four set out and three went and one said no before anybody asked her, and no person in that room counted anything about that, and nobody in that room named a fourth of them going.

---

The man of about thirty-one who carries things for a living put his satchel with the flap on the table at about the second hour of the afternoon and the working slate went into it face up, with the long count on one face and the wall's figures on the other, and neither of them was turned over on the way in and neither of them was turned over on the way out. The flap came down over it and the strap went round twice. A piece of paper the size of a man's two hands together was still lying face down on that table with the writing on it against the wood, and nobody in that room turned it over that day and nobody looked at the back of it.

The slate is in a satchel with a flap and a strap round it twice, going down a road four hundred miles long with a long count on one face of it and a wall's figures on the other, and nobody turned it over on the way into that satchel and nobody turned it over on the way out, and the woman of about fifty-two was still sitting at the end of that bench when it went.
