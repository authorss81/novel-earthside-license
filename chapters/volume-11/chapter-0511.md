# Chapter 0511 — A Line Of Doorways And A Figure Under An Arm

At the near end of eleven miles of flats there is a stack of boards against the end wall of the first building under a tarred cloth that has been wet and dry so often it has stopped being one or the other. On the hundred and ninety-sixth day of the Bare Month, a Monday, the cloth came off that stack at about the first hour and went back the way it was lying, and a woman put a tin down on the boards and went up the row with a slate under her arm.

---

What she counts on a morning like that is one kind of thing and not two. The kind of thing is the doorways of the flats that are shut. She goes up the outside of eleven miles of them and she marks each one on that slate with the flat of her finger while she is still standing in front of it, and she does not stop at any of them and she does not go in at any of them.

The weather came off the low ground in the middle of the morning and stayed in. There is a channel of wet silt the length of those flats where the water goes when it comes, and it had more in it that morning than the morning before, and it came up over the sills of the first row twice while she was still on that part of the row and did not go back down either time.

---

The man of about thirty-eight at the salt wharf came up the row from the near gate at about the second hour with his barrow behind him and his tally-board lying face up on the bed of it. He stopped at the stack and put his hand flat on the boards and turned the tally-board round so that the face of it was towards the near gate instead of up the row. About four hundred and forty was on it in his own hand, and it is a figure of this city's own, and it is the figure he sends.

A woman at the end of that row said the thing that is said, to the flags.

"You are up here at the second hour."

"**I am up here at the second hour on every morning that I send one, and there is nobody standing at this end of the row at the second hour to watch me send it, and that is the whole of why I come at this hour and it is not a better hour for it.**"

He put his hand flat on those boards again and said one word more, to the boards and not to her.

"**It is the same eleven miles of flats that she is going up this morning and I have never once been up them on foot and I am not going to start, and I would rather that was in the record than that somebody found out about it later.**"

Nobody at that end of the row asked him what the figure on that board was a figure of. Nobody asked the woman with the slate what she was doing on eleven miles of flats at the second hour of the morning either, and she did not say, and the man of about thirty-eight went on up the row with his barrow behind him and stopped at the near gate and stood there a while.

---

She was a long way up the row before the light came properly off the roofs. She came back down it about the fifth hour, slower than she had gone up, and she stopped twice on the way and stood with her back to the water both times, and at the near end of the row she put the tin down on the stack of boards where it had been standing at the first hour.

Then she wrote on the slate. She wrote one figure with a nail she had in the fold of her sleeve and she wrote the day beside it, and she did not rub anything out, and she did not say the figure out loud, and about four people at that end of the row came within reach of that slate before the market filled up and not one of them read it.

---

The market had been going behind her a good while by then. The store's board was on its two nails above the trough at the end of that market with all three sets of figures on it and none of the three rubbed out, and the wall above the top of that board was the colour it has been every morning since the water came. The man of about twenty-seven who washes that board was filling the trough at the step with his bucket, and the rag was on the stone lip, and the man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall had his own board standing face in against his own trestle two stalls along with the bare back of it out in the row.

She came down to the end of that market with the slate under her arm and the tin in her two hands and she stopped where the flags are wet at the end of the row, and she did not go up the stair at the back of the room over that market, and nobody at that end of the market said one word to her about the morning or about the board.

---

The figure was on the slate under her arm at the end of that market and the three sets of figures were on the board on its two nails above the trough, and not one person at that end of the market that morning had both of them in front of them at the same time, and there is nobody in this city who can say which of the two kinds of figure on that morning agrees with the other.
