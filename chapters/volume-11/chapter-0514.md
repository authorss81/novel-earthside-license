# Chapter 0514 — The Fourth Hour Without A Trough And A Chalk That Is Not His

The store's board came off its two nails at about the fourth hour on the hundred and ninety-ninth day of the Bare Month, a Thursday, and the man of about twenty-seven who washes that board turned it over once and laid it flat on the flags at the foot of the wall with the figures up and the wet side to the air, because the trough was four rows away at the far end of that row with his last bucketful still standing in it. He worked the rag across that board from the bottom left-hand corner to the top right-hand one and did the second half the same way, and the water came off it grey and went out across the flags and off the end of the step into the channel. He put the rag on the stone lip of that step to keep it out of the silt, and the bucket went to the trough at the far end of that row and came back empty, and then the bucket went again and came back with a little in it.

He did it four times between the fourth hour and the seventh, and about four people at that end of the market saw him do it, and not one of the four of them said a word about where the trough had gone. The store's chalk was out of its box on the step where it stands every morning of this flood, in a box with a lid that does not shut, and it had been rained on and dried and rained on and it stood on end in the box with about a thumb's length of it showing.

---

The man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall came along the row at about the fifth hour with his own board under his arm and stopped at the step and put his hand flat on the lid of that box and left it there.

"**That is the store's chalk and it is not mine, and mine is in this pocket and has been since the two hundred and ninety-eighth day of this flood, which is the forty-third day of the Short Month, and it is a different chalk, and I am telling you that because you have all been looking at that box for two days as though somebody were going to take something out of it.**"

A woman at a stall two along said the thing that is said, to the flags.

"It is all the same chalk from the same place."

"**It is all the same chalk out of the same box and it is not in the same hand, and there is a difference between those two things that nobody in this row has ever had to care about until this week.**"

He took his hand off the lid and looked down the row at the flags, where the water off that board had been running since the fourth hour and had made a channel of its own in them that was about the depth of his two fingers at the step and standing wider than that at the far end.

"**There is about four feet of standing water at the near end of that row where the channel has cut down. Nobody has taken a cord to it. Nobody ever will.**"

---

At about the seventh hour the man of about thirty-four went down that row to the far end of it and got both hands under that trough and lifted it and carried it back the length of that row himself, with a foot under the bottom of it every few paces so that it did not catch on the flags, and he put it back against that wall under the board on its own spot and he left it there. Nobody asked him to do that and nobody told him to, and the man of about twenty-seven who washes that board was at the step with the rag on the stone lip and did not look at him do it.

About four people at that end of the market saw a man carry a trough back the length of a row and put it where it had always been, and about four did not, and nobody in that row thanked him and nobody asked him why. His own piece of chalk stayed in his own inside breast pocket all the way down that row and all the way back up it, and he did not put a hand into that box on the step at any hour of that morning.

---

The second morning's business began at the near gate about the seventh hour, where the counting begins because there is nowhere else in this city that a person begins a thing, and she came up that row in the first light carrying a tin and a slate and stopped at the stack of boards at the near end, put the tin down on the boards, and did not write anything on the slate.

The trough is against that wall under that board where it has been since the seventh week of this flood, and the rag is on the stone lip where it goes every morning, and the chalk is in its box on the step with a thumb's length of it showing, and the man who carried that trough the length of a row and put it back is standing at his own trestle two stalls along with his hand flat on the bare back of his own board and is not saying anything about it.
