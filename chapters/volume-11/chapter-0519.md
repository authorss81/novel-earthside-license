# Chapter 0519 — Eleven Miles Of Flats With A Slate And Nothing On It

The cloth came off the stack of boards at the near end of those flats at about the first hour on the two hundred and fourth day of the Bare Month, a Tuesday, and went back the way it was lying, and a woman stood at the end of that row with a tin in her two hands and a slate under her arm and did not go up it. The second face of that slate was clean. The first face had a figure on it and the day beside that figure and nothing on it since, and she stood there about as long as it takes a man to count nine and then she went and got her hands wet in the channel and came back and stood at the near end of that row again.

She went up the row as far as the fourth hour and she came back down it, and the slate had nothing on the clean face of it when she got to the near gate, and there was no figure come out of that morning. There are four mornings and this was not one of them, and nobody in this city has asked her why a morning on eleven miles of flats with a slate and a tin under her arm came out with nothing written on it, and nobody is going to be the first.

---

The man of about thirty-eight at the salt wharf was at the second hour standing at the near gate with his barrow across the flags behind him and the tally-board face up on the bed of it where anybody going along that row could have read it, and about four hundred and forty was on that board in his own hand, and it is a figure of this city's own of the same kind of thing as the figure that is on a slate in a room over the market.

"**I put my figure face down and leave it where somebody will find it, and I do that because a figure with nobody's name on it that goes down a road ends up being a figure somebody stands behind, and I am not going to be the reason there is a face on a thing in this city.**"

He took his hand off the tally-board and put both hands on the shafts of his barrow, and he did not say one word about the woman with the slate and she did not say one word about him. About four people at that end of the row had worked out that those two figures are of the same kind of thing and about four had not, and not one of them said so.

At about the sixth hour he came back down that row, and he put his barrow across the row with its bar down, and he picked the tally-board up off the bed of it and turned it over and laid it face down on the stack of boards at the near end and put the back of it uppermost and did not put anything on it and did not tie it to anything.

A woman standing at the end of that row said the thing that is said, to the boards.

"You have left your figure face down on a pile of wood in the open."

"**The four hundred and forty is on the other side of it where the sun does not get to it, and if anybody in this city wants to find it they can come and turn it over, and nobody in this city is going to and neither am I.**"

Then he took his barrow up the row by the shafts and did not stop, and he did not look back at the stack of boards on his way to the end of it.

---

The room over that market had an old book in it. That book has been in that room since before the water came and it is not the register and it is not on the shelf, and there is a figure written in it on the left-hand page and the day that figure belongs to is the seventeenth day of this flood, and that figure has stood on that page through five hundred and two days without anybody taking a pen to it and without anybody reading it out.

About the ninth hour the working slate that goes in and out of that room on a barrow was lying on the table under the one window with its two figures on one face of it, and the second figure stood under the first one and neither of them had been struck out, and the old book was put down open on that table beside the slate and not laid against it. Three figures were on that table and the three of them do not agree with one another, and about four people in that room had looked at all three of them and about four had not, and nobody in that room said one word out loud about the difference. The register stood on its shelf behind the bench with its spine to the wall and the cover shut and the second slate stood behind it on its edge with a day across the head of it and nothing whatever under that day, and neither of them was picked up and neither of them was turned over and neither of them was written on.

---

At the end of that market the store's chalk was in its box on the step with the lid that does not shut and about a thumb's length of it showing, and the man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall walked past that step four times between the ninth hour and the eleventh with his own piece of chalk in his own inside breast pocket and did not stop at the box and did not put his hand over it.

There is a tally-board lying face down on a stack of boards at the near end of eleven miles of flats with about four hundred and forty on the other side of it where the sun does not get, and it is not on a bench in that room and it is not on a barrow and nobody in this city has picked it up, and there is nobody standing at the near end of that row of flats and the cloth is back on the stack and the tin is gone off it.
