# Chapter 0507 — A Trough Turned Upside Down On The Flags

It was the hundred and ninety-second day of the Bare Month, a Thursday, and it was an ordinary morning at one end of that market from the first hour of it to the last. The flags at the end of that row were wet where the low ground came off and dry two feet away from them, and the wet had done what it does. Nothing was sent anywhere that morning. Nothing was entered anywhere that morning. Nobody in that row asked anybody else a question before the light went off the flags.

---

The man of about sixty stood in that row from the seventh hour with his handcart standing behind him and its bar down, and in the cart there was a coil of rope that had been used and a hazel mallet with its head bound in cord. He had put the fence up along the top of the shut road out past the last named house and nobody had sent him, and nobody asked him that morning why he was in that row, and he did not offer it. He took the mallet out of the cart and turned it over in his hand and put it back in the cart and took the coil of rope out and put that back too, and then he stood with his hand on the frame of the cart and watched a woman at a stall two along put her awning out over her own board.

"**Sixteen willow posts and eleven withies and I am not going to say them again, and I have not said them to anybody this morning and nobody has asked me for them.**"

A woman at the stall two along said the thing that is said to the flags and not to him.

"You have got the cart out in this weather for nothing."

"**I have got the cart out in this weather because the flags are soft at this end and a cart will not come off them with a wheel on it, and that is all of the reason there is, and it is a reason and it is not a story.**"

Nobody thanked him for standing in a row. He put his hand on the frame of the cart again and left it there and went on watching the awning go out along its two poles.

---

The trough against the wall under the store's board had been in that spot for the whole of this flood and had been filled twice every morning and dipped into and filled again, and it had never once been emptied. The man of about twenty-seven who washes that board came out at about the eighth hour with his bucket, put the bucket down, got both hands under the trough and lifted it, and it was heavier than a thing that size ought to be, and he turned it over on the flags on its side and then over again on its head. What came out of it was a bed of grit about the thickness of a thumb, grey, and going hard in the bottom of it, and it was grit that had come off that board and out of that trough and off the rag every morning since the seventh week of this flood. He scraped the bottom of the trough with the edge of the bucket and put what came off it in a heap on the flags, he did the two ends of it, he turned it over once more and did the underside of it, and then he stood the trough back up against the wall under that board and left it there in the sun.

Nobody at that end of the market said a word about it, and about four people at that end of the market watched a man turn a trough over and go on with what they were doing, and not one of the nine who were on that row that morning asked him why he had done it.

---

He had a decision about that wall above the board and he had had it since the seventh week of this flood. The washing of that wall stops at the top of the board where the wall begins, and the bucket goes into the trough and the rag goes on the stone lip and nothing goes above that line, and the whole of that wall is the colour it has been every morning of this flood. Nobody at one end of that market has asked him about that wall and nobody is going to, and on that morning he did not mention it and did not look up at it once.

The market went on. A barrow came the length of the row and went back. A woman at a stall washed her own board down and hung her rag over the edge of it and did not take it down. The man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall had his own board standing face in against his own trestle and the bare back of it out in the row, and he was at it, and the piece of chalk of his own was in his own inside breast pocket and did not come out of it. About four people at that end of that market were still asking which of the three sets of figures on that wall is the one anybody ought to be reading, and not one of the nine people on that row put that to anybody else.

---

At about the fourth hour the store's board came down off its two nails and went into the trough and was washed and went back up on them, and all three sets of figures came out of the wet as clean as they went into it, and the board's being unwritten was not a thing that morning, because there were three sets of figures on it and had been for a fortnight. The man of about twenty-seven filled the trough at the fourth hour and once more before he went, and the water in it stood a finger under the stone lip, and the heap of grit on the flags had gone down to about a third of what it had been by the middle of the day.

"**There is a morning when a man empties a trough and the whole of the row sees it and nobody says one word about it, and that is not an event and I am not going to make it one.**"

Nobody argued with him and nobody improved on it.

---

The trough was standing against that wall full to within a finger of its lip when the light came off the flags at the end of that row, and the grit that had come out of the bottom of it is on the flags at the foot of that wall in a heap that a person walking past it has to step round. The man of about sixty had the handcart behind him in the row and the mallet in it and the rope in it and neither of them had been used that day. The man of about twenty-seven took the bucket in his right hand and walked the length of that row once to the end of it and back, and about nine people were on that row when he started and about nine people were on it when he got to the end.
