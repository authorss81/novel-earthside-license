# Chapter 0512 — A Trough Four Rows Away And The Beginning Nobody Was Asked For

Adrian Vale was on the flags at the end of that market on the hundred and ninety-seventh day of the Bare Month, a Tuesday, with a scrap of iron in his right hand and his left knee down in the wet, and the silt that comes off eleven miles of flats every morning comes off those flags at the end of that row and had been going into the channel by the store's step in a band about the width of a hand since the water came. He worked that band from the step outwards and piled it against the sill of the step, a heap about the depth of his two hands laid along the stone and banked up at the board end of it, and there was no barrow for it and nobody had given him one. He went back along it twice with the scrap of iron and turned the silt over at the bottom of the pile so it would not run, and by about the second hour the flags from that step to the end of that row were bare stone and the pile was the only thing on them.

---

That was the whole of what he did with his morning. The end of that market was the only place in this city where a person could stand and count a thing without anybody in the row stopping what she was doing to come and look, and he wanted the count to begin there, at the step, at the quiet end, and he did not ask her and there was nobody in that row he could ask. The tin stood on that sill with its strap hanging over the edge of it and about two hands away from where he was kneeling, and the bottom half of that strap was wet from the flags and the top half of it was not, and the water on it had not been there when he started. He did not touch the strap and he did not touch the tin and he did not move the sill, and he was six rows up with his back to the row for the rest of that morning doing nothing at all that anybody in that market was waiting for.

---

The man of about twenty-seven who washes that board had the board off its two nails and down on the flags by the fourth hour, and the trough was not under it. Two people had carried that trough off its spot against that wall at about the second hour of that morning and set it down at the far end of that row, and he had watched them do it from the step and had not said one word about it to either of them and neither of them had said one word to him.

"**I have washed this board every morning since the seventh week of the water and this morning I have washed it on the ground, and there is a heap of somebody else's silt banked against my step that I have to get my two feet clear of before I can stand on it at all.**"

A woman at the stall two along looked at the silt in the trough and said the thing that is said, to the flags and not to him.

"You could carry it back."

"**I could carry it back. I have not carried it back and I am not going to, and I am not going to stand in this row and explain to anybody what a trough is for either, because the last man who did that in this market got a bucket turned over his head for it.**"

Nobody asked him who had moved the trough and nobody told him. He took the board up in both arms and put it flat on the flags under the wall on its own edge with the figures up, and he filled the bucket at the channel and carried it the length of that row to the trough and tipped it in and carried the empty one back, and did that three more times before the seventh hour.

The water that came off that board went out across bare flags in a sheet about as wide as two hands and found the trough at the far end of the row and did not go in it, because he had it full. It went past it and off the end of the row and started down the other side, and it took the silt with it out of the end of that heap and made a channel of its own the whole length of that market, and about four people at that end of the market saw a channel of water make itself across flags that had been dry an hour before.

---

She came to the end of that market about the fifth hour with the tin in her two hands and the slate under her arm, and she stopped at the step where the heap had been and looked at the flags and then at the near gate, and nobody in that row said one word to her about the morning or about the board.

She went out through the near gate.

The near gate is the only place in this city where a count of anything is expected to start, and she started there, and she did not start at the quiet end of the row where the flags were bare stone and the step was clear, and no one had asked her to start at the near gate and no one had asked her not to start anywhere else. At the near gate there was a finger of water coming across the flags from the row above her, and it was not falling there and it had not been falling there the day before, and she stepped over it four times in going up to the stack of boards with the tin in her two hands and the slate under her arm, and the bottom of that tin was wet before she got to the boards.

---

The board went back up on its two nails at the fourth hour with all three sets of figures on it and none of the three rubbed out, and the wall above the top of that board was not washed, and at the end of that day there is nothing at all standing under that board, where the trough has stood since the seventh week of this flood, and the flags underneath it are the ones the water went out across.
