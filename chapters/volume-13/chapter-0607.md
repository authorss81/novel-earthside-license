# Chapter 0607 — A Box Emptied Onto The Flags And A Lid Put Back Where The Lid Is

Adrian Vale had both hands on the box on the step and he turned it over and shook it once, and what was in it came out onto the flags at the foot of the wall in a grey heap with the chalk in it broken down to a grit at the bottom of that heap. It was about the fourth hour on the two hundred and ninety-second day of the Bare Month, a Saturday. His hands were on that box and on the flags and on nothing else at any hour of that morning.

The trough was against that wall with the water in it up to about two hands and the rag was on the stone lip of that step. The box on that step is the store's own box of chalk and the lid of that box lies down on the rim of it and has never shut once, and it is not going to shut on any other day of this flood either. The board on the wall above that step came down off its two nails and went back up on them that morning, and it carries the account of the two hundred and twenty-fourth day of this flood and two figures of this city's own that do not agree with each other, and none of the three has been rubbed out and not one of the three carries a line of words under it. The stone above the top edge of that board went on up in the colour it keeps and was not washed at any hour of that day by anybody.

---

Adrian Vale got down on the flags and gathered that heap up in both hands and carried it to the channel at the end of that row and put it in the water, and he did it four times, and on the fourth time the grit that had been lying under that box on the flags went with the rest of it, and the flags at the foot of that step were bare stone before the light was up on the front of that store. He had that board and that trough and that bucket and that rag within reach of that step for the whole of that morning, and nobody in that row spoke to him and nobody in that row had been waiting for him to clear anything off anything.

The man of about twenty-seven who washes that board came along that row with the bucket in his right hand and a rag over his shoulder, and he has a wall he washed once in this flood and has not washed since, and that wall is the stone above the top edge of that board. He put his shoulder under the near edge of that board and lifted it off its two nails and set it down on the flags with its face up and ran the water down it from the near end to the far one, and then again from the same end, and set it back up on its two nails. Nobody in that row asked him anything at any hour of that morning, and nobody in that row said one word to him about the flags at the foot of that step being bare stone when he got there.

A man with a handcart came to the end of that row about the fifth hour and looked at the heap on the flags and said the thing that is said, to the man at the trough, and it was short.

"Leave it where it is. That cart comes at the eighth."

"**That board is not coming off those nails until the flags under it are clear, and they are not clear while that heap is on them, and nobody in this row is going to tell me to put water over a heap.**"

Nobody in that row improved on that and nobody thanked him for it, and the man with the handcart went on up the row with the cart where it was. The man with the handcart said one more thing about the channel and the man at the trough said one more thing about the step, and neither of those two said one word about the lid on that box, and about four people in that row heard the whole of it and about four did not.

Two stalls along a man said the thing that is said, from behind his own trestle, where his board stands face in with the bare back of it out in the row, and he has a piece of chalk in his own inside breast pocket that has not been out of that pocket since the two hundred and ninety-eighth day of this flood. Nobody in that row spoke to him at any hour of that morning. He said three things, and one of them was about a lid.

"**That lid has lain down on the rim of that box every day of this flood and it has not shut once, and nobody in this row is going to ask the man who owns that box why it was made that way.**"

He said a second thing about the lower edge of that board and a third thing about the stone above the top edge of it, and he said all three without stopping work and without turning round, and about four people came past him in that hour and none of them spoke to him and none of them looked up at the rim of that box, and nobody in that row said afterwards that he was right.

---

The box went back onto that step at about the ninth hour and its lid went back down onto the rim of it, and there was a thumb's width of grit under that rim on the near side, and the lid sat on the near side of that box and not on the far side of it and did not shut. Nobody in that row had seen the grit go into the channel. Nobody in that row had seen the flags at the foot of that step cleared at all, and about four people in that market had walked over the bare stone there without looking down at it.

The store's own chalk was in a heap on the flags at the end of that row and about a third of it had gone into the channel with the grit, and the box on that step was empty and there is no second box behind that step and nobody in that row has a second box. The date on the outside of the door at the foot of the stair at the back of the room over that market was in chalk that morning, and the two hundred and ninety-second day of the Bare Month was written in full on it, and there is no name under it.

That lid will not shut today and will not shut on any other day of this flood, and the man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall went home along that row at the end of the afternoon with a thumb's width of somebody else's grit sitting on the rim of a box he has never once had any reason to look at, and he does not know it is there, and nobody in that row is going to tell him, and the rag on the stone lip of that step and the trough against that wall and the board on its two nails were all where they were at the fourth hour, and the box on that step was not, and that lid is lying on a rim it does not meet, and nobody in this city is going to be told what is under it.
