# Chapter 0621 — A Thumb On One Corner Of A Line Of Grit And Four People Going Past The Open End

Adrian Vale had both hands on the shafts of that barrow and he wheeled it the length of that yard and set it down at the open end of it with the shafts up and a tilt in them. It was about the seventh hour on the three hundred and sixth day of the Bare Month, a Saturday, in a yard at the near end of eleven miles of flats, in weather. There was nothing on that barrow and nobody in this city puts anything on a barrow.

The yard is a rectangle of beaten ground between a wall and the top of the flats, and a stack of boards stands along the wall with its ends squared off and its top face about as high as a man's chest. On the top of that stack, squared with the boards under it and not squared with anything else in that yard, is a board with a figure of this city's own cut into the side of it that the sun gets to. A line of grey grit lies across that figure from one end of it to the other. The grit came off those boards on the five hundred and ninety-second day of this flood and nobody wiped it, and it is still in the same place, and it is not a figure and it is not a count of anything. Nobody in this city has ever been shown where the figure under that grit came from and nobody has ever asked. Nothing in that yard turns that board face down, because a board turned over carries its grit to the other side and leaves it there.

The man of about thirty-eight at the salt wharf was at the foot of that stack with his own tally-board against his own hip and a knife in the belt of his coat and another barrow standing behind him with its shafts on the ground. He has a tally-board, a knife, a barrow, and a figure of this city's own on the side of it that the sun gets to, and he has been at that foot since before the light, and nobody in this city has ever asked him one thing about that board or about the figure on it or about the grit.

---

A woman with a hand-basket came up the side of that yard at about the eighth hour to get past it to the flats and stopped at the open end with the barrow standing across the near half of the opening, and she said the thing that is said, to that barrow, and it was short.

"That is in my way."

The man of about thirty-eight said the thing that is said, to that barrow and not to the woman with the basket, and he did not move off the wall he was standing against to say it.

"**Nothing in that yard is in anybody's way except a barrow somebody has left at the open end of it, and I did not put that one there and I am not going to be the one who moves it.**"

Nobody in that yard improved on that and nobody thanked him for saying it. The woman with the hand-basket went sideways round the near post of the wall with her basket held out in front of her and did not say a second word about it.

Adrian Vale came back down the top row with the flat of his hand on the top face of that stack and put his thumb on the near corner of the line of grit where it had lifted at one end of the figure, and he pressed that one corner back down where it had lain, and he took his hand off the top of the stack and went down the row with his hands in his pockets. The corner lay flat where he had put it. The rest of that line came up off the board again by itself over the next half hour as the air went across it, and the corner did not, and nobody in that yard watched him do it and nobody in that yard knew afterwards that it had been done.

---

Two men came into that yard at about the ninth hour with a board between them, one at each end of it, and they got as far as the near post of the wall and stopped, because the barrow was across the way in and a board is longer than the gap beside it. They set what they were carrying down on the wet flags at the mouth of the yard. The man at the end nearest the man of about thirty-eight said the thing that is said, across the yard to him, and he wanted to know whose barrow it was.

He said it to that barrow and not to the man who had asked him about it, and it was over in about as long as it takes a man to look at a barrow.

"**That is not my barrow and I did not bring it to the open end of my own yard, and there is a man on those flats who will know whose it is, and I am not going to be asked to keep it somewhere else.**"

Nobody in that yard said that he was right and nobody argued with him about it. The two men stood at the near post for about as long as it takes two men to decide that a yard is not worth an argument, and then they went out to the top row, because Adrian Vale was on the top row with a bucket in his hand and he came back to the mouth of the yard and put one hand on that barrow and turned it on its wheel about as far as a barrow goes on ground like that, and it stood against the wall instead of across the opening, and the two men came back in and got their board to the foot of the stack that way.

The board they set down on those flags had a wet edge along the bottom of it by the time they had it up again, and the man at the end nearest the wall looked at that wet edge and said one thing about it, to the other man and not to Adrian Vale, and the other man said nothing back. Neither of them said a word to Adrian Vale about the turning of that barrow and neither of them said a word to the man of about thirty-eight about whose it was. They carried their board to the foot of the stack and set it down against the end of the stack and went out of the yard again, and the time it had taken them was about as long as it takes a man to be wet along one edge of a board and not to be able to do anything about it.

The man of about thirty-eight said one more thing in that yard that morning, out loud, to the stack and to nobody, and it was about the grit and not about the board lying under it.

"**That line of it has lain across that figure since the five hundred and ninety-second day of this flood and nobody has wiped it and nobody has come to this yard to ask what is under it, and I have not turned that board over in all that time, because a board turned over takes its grit to the other side and leaves it lying there.**"

Nobody in that yard improved on that and nobody thanked him for saying it, and no hand in that yard went near the top of that stack at any hour of that morning but the one that pressed one corner of that line down where it had lifted, and that hand belonged to a man who went down the row with his hands in his pockets.

---

The two pieces of chalk in this flood are not the same chalk and they were not in one hand at any hour of that day. The store's own chalk is in a heap on the flags at the end of the market row, with about a third of it gone into the channel with the grit, and its box is on the step at the end of that row with the lid of it lying down on the rim and the lid has never shut. Two stalls along, the man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall has a piece of chalk in his own inside breast pocket, and it has not been out of that pocket since the two hundred and ninety-eighth day of this flood, and it was in that pocket at the seventh hour of the three hundred and sixth day of the Bare Month and it was in that pocket at the eleventh hour of it, and nobody in that market has ever asked him one thing about it.

One kind of thing in this flood was counted on foot out along those flats on four mornings, and the four of them are the five hundred and eleventh day of this flood and the five hundred and fifteenth and the five hundred and seventeenth and the five hundred and twenty-first, and the four figures they came out with are in a book in a room over a market with a day written in beside each of them, and the four of them do not agree with one another and were not allowed to. The three hundred and sixth day of the Bare Month was not one of those four mornings. Nobody counted anything in that yard at any hour of that day, and the man of about thirty-eight at the foot of that stack was not asked whether the figure under that grit was one anybody could check, and there is no instrument in four hundred miles that could check it, and he did not say so.

---

By the eleventh hour the light had come far enough across that yard to reach the near edge of the stack, and the line of grey grit lay across the figure on the top of it with one corner of it flat where a thumb had been on it that morning and the rest of it up off the wood where the air had got under it again. About four people went past the open end of that yard that day, one after another, at their own times, and not one of them stopped at the top of that stack, and nobody in that yard took the number of them or wrote one of them down, and the barrow stood against the wall with the shafts up and nothing on it and the two men were gone with the wet edge of their board and nobody in that yard was going to be told whose thumb had been on the grit.
