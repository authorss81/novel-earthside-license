# Chapter 0613 — One Place On A Sheet And One Column On A Page And The Same Shape

The woman of about twenty-nine who keeps a public register had both hands under the register on the shelf behind that bench and she drew it out with its spine to the wall and set it down on the table under the one window with the face of it squared to the edge of the wood. It was about the eighth hour on the two hundred and ninety-eighth day of the Bare Month, a Friday, in daylight. She has a second slate, and a column on the right of every page she leaves empty, and neither of those two things came off that shelf in her hands.

There is a date in chalk on the outside of the door at the foot of the stair at the back of that room. It is the two hundred and ninety-eighth day of the Bare Month in full, and under it there is nothing at all but a bare strip of door about as wide as a hand. Nine people stood in that room at that hour, and none of them had been sent anywhere to be in it. The man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall was at the far end of that table. He has a board standing face in against his own trestle two stalls along, and a piece of chalk in his own inside breast pocket that he does not use, and neither of those came up that stair with him.

The fourth one-place form lay on that table where it had lain since the day it came up that road, a heading across the top of it and one place ruled under that heading with nothing whatever in that one place, and the bone it was folded around still lying in the near fold of it. Nobody in that room had touched it at any hour since. The heading over that one place is a heading and is not a sentence, and it does not say what that one place is a place of, and nobody in that room can say it either.

---

She turned the book to the head of the column that holds figures and put the flat of her left hand on the open page to keep it open, and then she took her hand off it. Down that page there are three columns. One of them is at the left-hand edge, one is in the middle and one is on the right. Figures fill the middle one from end to end — five of them, entered as they were made, each with its own day beside it, and not one of the five struck out. The column on the right is the same width as the middle one and is bare from the head of that page to the foot of it.

A second slate stands on that shelf up on its edge, written face to the plaster. A day is cut across the head of it and there is nothing at all under that day. It did not move at any hour of that morning, and the only hand in that room which came near that shelf was the keeper's, and that hand came off the spine of the book and did not go back to it. On the wall opposite that bench there is a shelf at the height of a person's shoulder, and standing on it is the first sheet of that same one shape, a heading and one place with a figure of this city in it, read out on the four hundred and fifty-fifth day of this flood and never returned and never withdrawn, and it stood there through the whole of that morning and nobody in that room laid it beside anything.

The man of about thirty-four had not put his hands on the table at any point since he came into that room. He looked at the one place on that sheet, and then he turned about on his heel and looked at the column on the right of that open page, and then he said the thing that is said, to the two of them, and it was about nine words and it took him about four seconds.

"**That place and that column are the same shape, and neither of them has ever had anything in it.**"

Nobody in that room thanked him for saying it. Nobody improved on it, nobody argued with it, nobody said that he was right, and nobody in that room came at him afterwards with a second question about it. About four people in that room had the two of those places in their own heads by that hour and about four had not, and the two of those groups did not say one word to each other about it before the light went off that one window.

---

Nobody in that room said that the two of them were the same thing. Nobody in that room said that they were two different things either. A man at the near end of that bench said the thing that is said, to the open book, and he wanted it settled one way before he went down that stair.

"They cannot both be waiting for the same thing."

The man of about thirty-four did not move from the far end of that table and he said the thing that is said, to the man who had asked it, and it was not quick.

"**They can both be empty and only one of them has anybody in this room who has ever been told what it is for, and the other one is on a table with nothing in it, and this is not a question I can settle for you and it is not one I have been asked.**"

Nobody in that room improved on that and nobody thanked him for saying it. A woman standing at the one window said the thing that is said, to the sheet on the table, and she wanted to know whether the two empty things were a mistake somewhere.

"Somebody has left both of them out."

The man of about thirty-four said the thing that is said, to her, and he said it to the sheet on that table and not to the woman.

"**Nobody in this room has been told that either of them was left out, and the one place on that sheet came up that road with nothing in it, and that is what it came up with and not a thing that was dropped in a room.**"

Nobody in that room said that she was right and nobody improved on what he said and nobody asked him how he knew what that sheet had come up with. The man of about thirty-four put his hands into his pockets at that point and left them there and did not say one more word about either of those two places for the rest of that morning.

---

Seven documents have come up that road into this city or gone out of it in this flood. Three of the seven carry four places apiece. The other four carry one place each, and two of those four are in this room: one on the table under the window with nothing in it and one on the shelf opposite the bench with a figure in it. A third of that shape left this city on the five hundred and forty-fifth and is not coming back. The fourth of them arrived with a number already in its place, was written in a second time on the five hundred and eighty-eighth day with a different number, and went back up the road on the round on the five hundred and ninety-fifth with a line of words under that number. Those words are outside this city, they came back with nothing, and nothing in this flood can lift a line of words off a page and leave a bare figure where it stood.

The register went back on its shelf behind that bench at about the eleventh hour with its spine to the wall. Beside it on that shelf the second slate was standing on its edge where it had been standing all morning, and it was still there at the eleventh hour, and the only hand in that room which came near that shelf all morning was the keeper's.

The one lying on that table and the one standing on the shelf opposite it were in that room together until the light went off that one window, and nobody in that room ever put them near each other, and the shape they have in common is a place about as wide as a hand, and at about the eleventh hour the keeper's own hands went to the spine of the book and stopped there and did not go to the column on the right of that page.
