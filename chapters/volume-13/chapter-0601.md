# Chapter 0601 — Three Sets Of Figures On Two Nails And A Satchel Going By Behind Them

Adrian Vale had both hands under the near edge of that board and he lifted it off its two nails and set it down on the flags with its face up. It was about the fourth hour on the two hundred and eighty-sixth day of the Bare Month, a Sunday. The trough stood against the wall with the water in it up to about two hands and the bucket stood on the flags at the foot of the step with a rag on the stone lip beside it. That board carries the account of the two hundred and twenty-fourth day of this flood and two figures of this city's own that do not agree with each other. None of the three has been rubbed out and not one of the three carries a line of words under it. The stone above the top edge of that board goes on up in the colour it keeps.

The man of about twenty-seven who washes that board came along the row at that hour with his bucket in his right hand and a rag over his shoulder, and he has a wall he washed once in this flood and has not washed since, and that wall is the one above the top edge of that board. He set the bucket down at the foot of the step, picked the rag up off the stone lip, and stood a moment looking at the water already down the length of that board where it lay on the flags. He said nothing about it. He put the rag in his own water, wrung it, and ran it down the board from the near end to the far one, and then down again from the same end.

Adrian Vale took the rag off him at the far end and ran it down the length of that board a third time with the flat of his hand on the wood, and the light came off the flags while the rag was still in his hand. He wrung the rag out over the trough, put it back on the stone lip, and set the board up on its two nails with the figures on it the way up they had been. He did not turn it over and he did not stand there afterwards looking at it. The water he put down that row went along the flags in front of three stalls and took the front boards of the stall two stalls along, where three trays were standing on a trestle with their rims level. A woman there lifted them off one at a time and set them on the back boards, and the underside of the third was wet where it had been standing, and she put it upside down on the back boards to dry and did not say anything about it to anybody.

---

At about the seventh hour that row was filling. About four people were at that end of the market in the ordinary course and about four more were not there, and the three sets of figures on that board were the same three that had been on it at the fourth hour. Nobody in that row washed the stone above the top edge of that board that day, and nobody had washed it on any other day of this flood except one.

A woman with a tray cover folded back off her head said the thing that is said, to the board on that wall, and it was short.

"Does that get read or not."

"**That board has been washed every morning since the seventh week of this flood, it has not been rubbed out once, and nobody in this row is going to say which of the three on it is the one to read.**"

Nobody in that row improved on what he said and nobody thanked him for it and nobody said that he was right. He put the rag over his shoulder again, picked his bucket up off the flags, and stood with it at the foot of the step, and Adrian Vale was four steps further along the row with his hands in his pockets, and nobody in that row spoke to him and nobody in that row had been waiting for him.

A man going past with a hand-basket on his head said the thing that is said, to the stone above the top edge of that board, and it was short.

"You could get a bucket up there if you wanted it."

"**There is a wall above that board, I washed it once in this flood, and I am not going to wash it again.**"

Nobody in that row argued with him about that wall and nobody improved on what he said and nobody thanked him for it. He said one more thing about that board and he said it to the woman with the tray cover and not to anybody else, and he said it without stopping.

"**About four people in this row can tell you what is on it and about four cannot, and neither group gets settled today.**"

The woman with the tray cover wanted the water kept off her own boards and not for the three on that wall to be settled, and she said so.

"It is not settling anything. It is the wet."

"**The wet is at the fourth hour and this is the seventh, and the man who washes that board does not know which group he is standing in front of.**"

Nobody in that row put those two things together out loud and nobody chose between them. He took his bucket down the row to the channel and came back with it full. About four people went past the end of that row before the light went and did not stop at that wall, and about four did and stood in front of it for about as long as it takes a man to decide he has read something. The trough stayed against that wall and the rag stayed on the stone lip and the board stayed on its two nails with the same three sets of figures in the same order and nothing under any of the three.

---

In the last hour of the afternoon a man came up that road with a satchel with a flap across his body and the strap over one shoulder, and he went along behind that row, across the front of the store, and up on to the road beyond. He was going the road in the ordinary course of the quarterly round with the flap of that satchel down over whatever was in it, and the flap was still down when he was out of sight of that market. About four people in that row saw a man with a satchel go past the end of it. Not one of them looked inside it, and not one of them looked at it a second time, and nobody in that row said one word to him about it.

He is the man of about thirty-one who carries things for a living, and he has a satchel with a flap, and he has never opened anything he has carried in this flood. He carried a sheet up that road on the five hundred and ninety-fifth day of this flood with a line of words under a figure on it, and nobody in this city has ever asked him what was in it on any of those days. He is the fourth man who has carried a sheet of that one shape up that road, and the three before him are not behind him now, and two of those three are not in this city at all and are not coming back.

The last thing that happened at that end of that market on the two hundred and eighty-sixth day of the Bare Month was not a figure and was not a sheet. A man walked past the end of a row of stalls with a bag on his shoulder, and four people in that row saw him go, and not one of them turned round afterwards to say to anybody else what they thought he was carrying, and by the time anybody in that row had a question about it the man was past the store and out of sight, and there was nothing on the wall behind him to look at.
