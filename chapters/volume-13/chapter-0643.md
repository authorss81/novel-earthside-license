# Chapter 0643 — A Man Walks Out Past The Last Named House With A Board Under His Arm And Nobody Asks Him About Either Of The Two Things He Is Carrying

The man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall had a board under his own left arm with the palm of his own right hand flat on the face of it, and he walked out of that market row at about the sixth hour and along the top of the flats and out past the last house that has a name on it. It was the three hundred and twenty-eighth day of the Bare Month, a Sunday, in weather that had not decided anything, and nothing in that row asked him where he was going.

That board of his own stands face in against his own trestle two stalls along with the bare back of it out in the row, and he had it under his arm and not against the trestle. The piece of chalk in his own inside breast pocket came out of that pocket on the six hundred and thirty-eighth day of this flood and went back into it the same afternoon, and it has not been out of that pocket since that day, and the full form of the day it went in is the two hundred and ninety-eighth day of this flood.

The man of about thirty-eight at the salt wharf came along that row at about the seventh hour with his own barrow behind him on its shafts and nothing on it and his own tally-board under his other arm. He did not ask the man of about thirty-four where he was going and the man of about thirty-four did not ask him anything either, and about four people along that row saw two men pass each other going opposite ways and about four did not, and nobody took the number of either of them.

---

A fence stands along the top of a road that is shut, out past that last named house. It is sixteen willow posts with eleven withies bound on it with the same cord. Nobody in this city has measured that fence and nobody has moved a post in it, and the man of about thirty-four stood at the near end of it for a while with his own hand on the top of the near post and did not put a cord on it and did not take one off it.

The road under that fence goes out of this city and it is four hundred miles long. Three barrows stand on it with nothing on any of them and a party of four is four, three of them went and one of them said no before anybody was asked, and nobody in this city can be shown one person standing on that road. No chapter of this flood prints how far along that road anybody is, and nobody in this city is going to be told.

He put one thing out loud at the near end of that fence, at the near post and not at the man of about thirty-eight who had gone on up the row, and nobody there had asked him for it.

"**Sixteen of them and eleven of the other and nobody has ever put a hand on the top of this one, and I am not the man who starts it, and I am going back down that row the same way I came out.**"

Nobody standing along that row made a better thing of it and nobody thanked him for saying it, and he took his own hand off the top of that post and went back the way he had come.

---

He was back at his own trestle by about the ninth hour and stood behind it with the board still under his own arm. The store's board was on its two nails at one end of that market with three sets of figures on it, none of them rubbed out, and not one of the three carrying a line of words under it, and the top edge of it stood level with the sill of that wall where a thin wedge of wood had been driven under the head of the near nail. The wall above that top edge was not washed on that day and by no second person.

A woman at the front of the stall next along wanted to know where he had been, in the ordinary way, and he said it to the front of his own trestle and not to her.

"I went out past the last house and I stood at a fence and I came back, and I am not going to stand at a trestle with a board under my arm and be asked what a fence is for, and you have been at that front since before the light like everybody else in this row."

Nothing in that row made a better thing of that and nobody there thanked him for it. She went on to the front beyond his own and did not come back to that end of the row that day, and he did not know that and was not told.

A man with a board under his own arm stopped at that end of the row at about the tenth hour and asked him, without any preamble, whether anybody had ever told anybody what the column on the right of a page of the keeper's book was for. He has nineteen rates on his own board in three columns and at the foot of the column between the other two there is a space about as wide as two of his fingers with nothing whatever in it, and that space is about as wide now as it was the day before the water came.

The man of about thirty-four said it to the ground between his own two feet, and he did not turn about to say it, and it was the last time he said anything at all that day.

"**I have never asked her what that column is for and I am not going to ask her on a Sunday morning, and a man who has not asked her is not a man anybody has to be told anything about, and that is me, and it has been me since that column got a word in it.**"

Nobody in that row made a better thing of that either and nobody thanked him for it, and nobody asked the man with the board what was in that space at the foot of the column between the other two, and the man with the board went on along that row and did not ask him a second thing.

A man at a front two along said, across that row and to nobody in particular, that a person who carried a board under his own arm and would not say where he had been with it was doing something in that row that morning that about four people had noticed.

"Two stalls along and an hour out of the front, and the board never went on the trestle."

Nobody in that row answered him and nobody told him to mind his own business and nobody thanked him for saying it.

---

There are two pieces of chalk in this flood and they are not one chalk, and no hour of that day put both of them in one hand. One of them is the store's own, in a heap on the flags at the end of that market row, with about a third of it gone into the channel, and its box is on the step above those flags with the lid of it lying down on the rim of it, and that lid has never shut.

The other one is in that man's own inside breast pocket, and it came out of that pocket once, on the six hundred and thirty-eighth day of this flood, and went back the same afternoon, and the man of about thirty-four put the flat of his own hand over that pocket at about the tenth hour and at about the eleventh and did not open it.

Over the back of a chair at the far end of that bench in the room over that market there is a length of new rope, and it is as good as the day it came into this city. Nobody in that room looked at it once on that day and nobody in this city has ever asked the man it belongs to one single thing about it.

---

At about the eleventh hour that room over that market had about four people left in it. The register stood on its shelf behind that bench with its spine to the wall, five figures down the column that holds them with a day written in beside each of the five and none struck out, and the column on the right of that page carrying one thing near the head of it and nothing anywhere else on that page.

The second slate stood up on its edge on the same shelf with its written face to the plaster and a day cut across the head of it and nothing whatever under that day.

The man of about thirty-four went back down that stair at about the eleventh hour with the board under his own arm and the piece of chalk in his own pocket where it had been since the two hundred and ninety-eighth day of this flood. This was the last morning on which anybody in that room could have asked him about that board or about that pocket, and nobody asked, and nobody in that row is going to say why.
