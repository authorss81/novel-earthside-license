# Chapter 0603 — Two Sheets Of One Shape In One Room And A Number Said Twice

The woman of about twenty-nine who keeps a public register had the flat of her left hand on the folded sheet lying on the table under the one window and her right hand flat on the wood beside it, and she set the sheet down on the near edge of that table and squared it with her fingertips and took both hands off it. It was about the seventh hour on the two hundred and eighty-eighth day of the Bare Month, a Tuesday. She has a second slate, and a column on the right of every page she leaves empty.

The date on that morning was in chalk on the outside of the door at the foot of the stair at the back of that room, and the two hundred and eighty-eighth day of the Bare Month was written in full on it, and there is no name under it. About nine people were in that room at that hour and not one of them had been sent for it. The man of about thirty-one who carries things for a living was at the one window with the strap of his satchel across his chest and the flap of it down, and he has one thing he knows what is in.

She took that sheet off that table at about the seventh hour, unfolded it flat with the bone still lying in the near fold of it, looked at the heading across the top of it and at the one place ruled under that heading with nothing in it, and put it back where it had been with the same two folds in the same places.

The register is on its shelf behind that bench with its spine to the wall, and that page is ruled into three columns, the one at the left-hand edge of it, the one in the middle, and the one on the right. The middle of those three is full from its head to its foot with five figures, one under another, in the order they were made in, with a day written in beside each, and not one of the five struck. The column on the right of that page is the same width as the one beside it and is empty the whole way down. A second slate stands on that shelf up on its edge with its written face turned to the plaster, a day cut across the head of it and nothing whatever under that day, and it did not move at any hour of that morning and the only hand in that room which came near that shelf was the keeper's.

The man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall got down on the floor at about the eighth hour and looked at that shelf from underneath it. He has a board standing face in against his own trestle two stalls along, and a piece of chalk in his own inside breast pocket that he does not use, and neither of those two things came up that stair with him. He did not put a hand on it and he did not put a hand on the register and he did not put a hand on the second slate, and he stayed on the floor looking up at the underside of that shelf for about as long as it takes a man to decide that there is nothing under a shelf, and then he got up off the floor and said nothing whatever about it to anybody.

---

On the wall opposite that bench there is a shelf at the height of a person's shoulder, and standing on that shelf on its edge is a sheet with a heading across the top of it and one place ruled under that heading with a figure of this city in it. That sheet was read out on the four hundred and fifty-fifth day of this flood and it was never returned and it has never been withdrawn, and it is in this city, and it has been standing on that shelf through every day since. It is the first of the four of that shape there have been in this flood.

There are seven documents in this flood. Three of them have four places on them in the order they are printed: a return with a district, a population, a rota and a share, which went out of this city on the three hundred and ninety-third day; a schedule with a date, a gate, a place for whoever keeps that gate and the days, which stands where it stands; and a sheet with a place, a line, a day and a person, which went out of this city on the four hundred and thirty-eighth day. Four of them have one place, and the fourth of those four is on the table under the one window, and the first of them is standing on the shelf opposite the bench, and the second of them was filled on the five hundred and thirty-eighth day and went out of this city in a satchel with a flap on the five hundred and forty-fifth, and the third of them came up that road with a figure already in its one place, that one place was written in a second time on the five hundred and eighty-eighth day with a figure that was not the first one, and it went back up that road on the round on the five hundred and ninety-fifth with a line of words under the figure in it.

Those two sheets of one shape were in that room on that morning, on a table on one side of it and on a shelf on the other, and nobody in that room laid one of them beside the other, nobody in that room had both of them in one hand, and nobody in that room said one word out loud comparing one of them to the other.

A man at the near end of that bench said the thing that is said, to the woman who was counting, and what he wanted out of that morning was a different thing.

"Count the shelf and not the table."

"**I am counting what has come up that road and gone out of it, and a shelf is not a number, and nobody in this room is going to make it one this morning.**"

Nobody in that room argued with him about that shelf and nobody improved on what he said and nobody told either of the two of them that they were right, and the man who had asked for it stayed at the near end of that bench for the rest of that morning with his hands flat on the wood in front of him and without once saying another word about it to anybody.

The woman of about twenty-nine who keeps that register said the thing that is said, to the table, and she was counting on the fingers of her left hand.

"**Everything that has come up that road into this room or gone out of it in this flood, counted on one hand, and I make it six.**"

About four people in that room had those six in their own heads when she said it and about four did not. Nobody in that room said that she was right and nobody argued with her and nobody thanked her for it.

She said the thing that is said, to the same table, a quarter of an hour later, and she was counting on the fingers of her right hand.

"**Everything again, counted the other way round, and I make it seven, and I am not going to take either of the two back.**"

Nobody in that room improved on that and nobody said which of the two was the count and nobody said that she was wrong about the first one. The man of about thirty-one who carries things for a living said the thing that is said, to the one window, and it was short.

"There is one of those sheets on this table and one of them on that shelf, and I have carried two more of that shape on that road, and I am not going to be the one who counts anything in this room."

Nobody in that room asked him to count anything, and nobody in that room told him he was wrong to keep out of it, and he went on standing at that window with the flap of that satchel down over what was in it and did not say a second thing at any hour of the rest of that morning.

---

The man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall, who had been on the floor, went out of that room about the ninth hour and went down the stair at the back of it and along the row. Two people in that room said one word each to each other about the two numbers, and neither of those two words was about which of the two was right. The man of about thirty-one went down that stair at about the tenth hour with the flap of the satchel down over what was in it and nothing in that satchel except the strap, and nobody in that room went after him and nobody asked him what was in it.

The sheet on the table was not moved again that day. The first sheet of that shape was not moved off that shelf at any hour, and the figure in its one place was the figure that was in it before this flood, and nobody in that room had said one word about what that figure was a figure of and nobody in this city has ever been shown. The register went back on its shelf behind that bench with its spine to the wall and the column on the right of that page empty from its head to its foot, and the second slate stood on that shelf up on its edge with a day cut across the head of it and nothing under that day, and the only hand that came near that shelf all morning was the keeper's.

Six and seven had both been said out loud in that room that morning in front of about nine people, and neither of the two had been taken back, and the two of them went out of that room still not agreeing with each other. The woman of about twenty-nine who said the six and then the seven is the person who keeps the book where the five figures in it are written down with a day beside each of them, and a number is going to have to come off one of those two in the ordinary course, and about four people in that city know which of the two she is going to use and about four do not, and neither of those groups is going to say so to her.
