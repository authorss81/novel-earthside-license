# Chapter 0636 — A Plank Laid Across A Wet Sill And A Man With A Handcart Who Did Not Know Whose Hands Laid It

Adrian Vale had that plank on his shoulder when he came out along the second row and he went down on one knee at the near end of that wet sill and set the near end of it into the silt and then got the far end across and let it lie. It was about the sixth hour on the three hundred and twenty-first day of the Bare Month, a Sunday, in a second row of flats, in weather, with a channel of wet silt running along the bottom row and the water moving with every step a man took on that ground. He did not stand on it and try it. He put a knee on the middle of it and rocked it once with his own weight, and it did not move, and what he had come out to find out was not whether the plank was sound but whether anything on that row was going to take it off him in the night.

The sill he laid it across is about a foot above the water in that channel and there is standing water in the shadow of the top row on both sides of where that plank went down. He took the end of a rope off his own belt that has been round a gatepost and a post and a barrow and a great deal else in this flood, and it is not new, and he put one turn of it round the middle of that plank and one round the sill end nearest the top row and knotted it, and the knot held when he put his weight on it again.

The trough, the bucket and the rag are one of each in this city and none of the three of them was on that row. The trough was standing against the foot of a wall at the market end of those flats with the bucket in his own right hand, and the rag was on the stone lip of a step at the end of that market row, where it is put down every morning and left, and it did not come out along that row at all.

---

The man of about thirty-eight at the salt wharf came along that second row at about the seventh hour with his own barrow behind him on its shafts and nothing on it, and his own tally-board under his other arm with the side of it that the sun gets to turned in against his ribs, and a knife in the belt of his coat. Nobody in this city has ever asked him one thing about that board or about the figure on it and nobody in this city has ever been shown where that figure came from.

That board is not turned over on its face on any day of this flood, and it was not turned over that day, because a board turned over carries what is lying on it to the other side and leaves it lying there.

He wanted to get his barrow along that row and there was a gap in front of him where that plank was not yet, and he said it to the plank and not to the man who had laid it.

"**A thing a person has laid across a wet sill is a thing a person walks on and not a thing a barrow goes over, and I am not asking you who laid it and I am not going to be told.**"

Nothing on that row made a better thing of what he had said, and nobody on it thanked him for saying it. He took his barrow round the far end of that gap along the top row where the ground is higher and came back into the second row on the other side of it, and he did not put his hand on the plank and did not look at the rope on it.

He said one more thing at about the eighth hour, to the plank again, and nobody had asked him for it.

"**I have a figure cut into a board of my own that nobody has checked and nobody is going to check and I am not going to stand at a plank and be asked about a rope.**"

Nobody on that row improved on that either and nobody thanked him for saying it and nobody in this city has ever asked him about either of those two things.

---

Two people on that row wanted that plank to be somewhere else, and neither of them got what it was.

A woman at the near end of that row wanted that plank taken up off that sill and laid at a sill further along where four people instead of two cross it every morning, and she said so once, standing at the near end of it.

"Lay it further along. There are twice the people across at that one."

A man at the far end of that row wanted it taken up off that sill altogether, on the grounds that a thing laid across a wet sill in this flood is a thing that is not there in the morning, and he said that once and did not say who he thought took it.

"Take it up. It will not be there at the light and you know it as well as I do."

Adrian Vale did neither of those two things. He got the silt out from under that sill with the flat of his own hand and put it in the water on the bottom row where the water took it, and he pulled the one turn of that rope tighter where it had worked loose against the wood, and the plank stayed where he had put it.

Nobody on that row said thank you to him for any part of it.

---

About the eighth hour a man came up that row with a handcart of his own and went along it as far as a man goes to fetch a thing and turned about and came back down it with that handcart and something in it. There is no barrow in this city with anything on it on any day of this flood and a handcart is not a barrow and joins nothing to it.

He stopped on the way back at the place where that plank was and stood a moment on the near end of it and went on down that row with what he had gone up it for.

He did not know whose hands had laid that plank there and nobody told him and he did not ask. Adrian Vale was two rows off at that hour with the bucket in his right hand and had not been told that anybody had gone up that row and come back down it.

The trough and the rag were at the market end of those eleven miles and the bucket was in Adrian Vale's own right hand. A rope that has been used holds a plank across a wet sill in weather that a length of new rope would not, and there is more than one rope in this flood and this one is the one that was on his own belt that morning, and none of the others was in this row at any hour of that day.

---

By the ninth hour that plank was still lying across that wet sill with a turn of an old rope round the middle of it, and the near end of it was about a foot higher than the stone because the stone had gone down under it. A person could walk on it. A man with a handcart had walked on it and had not known whose hands laid it there and had not stopped.

That was the whole of what he had come out to do and he had done it, and it was the first thing in this flood he had laid down and found still there in the morning, and there was nobody on that row at any hour of that day who knew it had been in doubt that morning or was told at any hour of that day that it had been in doubt. Nobody on that row was thanked for it and nobody was told what it was for, and Adrian Vale carried the bucket back up the top row towards that market with his own hands empty. He had not expected anybody to say anything about it and he was not going to be told.
