# Chapter 0648 — A Man Asks One Woman One Thing About A Room And She Does Not Answer It And The Room Did Not Hear It

The man of about thirty-one who carries things for a living had both hands in the two pockets of his own coat at the near end of that bench at about the tenth hour, and he took them out of them and put them flat on the top of that bench on either side of where he was standing, and he was not in a hurry about it. It was about the tenth hour on the three hundred and thirty-third day of the Bare Month, a Friday, in the room over a market, in daylight, with about nine people in that room and not one of them sent for anything.

On the outside of the door at the foot of the stair at the back of that room there is a date in chalk, and the three hundred and thirty-third day of the Bare Month is on it in full, and there is no name under it, and the wood below that date is bare for the width of a hand.

That book stood on its shelf behind that bench with its spine to the wall, and the page of it that is open is ruled into three, and the middle one of the three holds five figures put in one under another in the order they were made, with a day written in beside each of them. Not one of the five is struck out and no sixth has been entered and none has been taken out.

The column on the right of that page is the same width as the one beside it and carries one word near the head of it and nothing anywhere else on that page. Behind that book on the same shelf, standing up on its edge with its written face to the plaster, is a second slate, and a day is cut across the head of it and there is nothing whatever under that day. It did not move at any hour of that morning and the only hand which came near that shelf was the keeper's.

The satchel with a flap was behind that bench with the flap down over it, and nothing went out of that room on that day.

---

A word in a place on a page is a kind of thing, and this city has two of them. One is at the head of the column on the right of that page in a hand that is not the keeper's, and one of them went up a road that is out of this city on a sheet with a heading and one place ruled under that heading, and neither of the two has been read out in a room, and nobody in this city is going to be told what either of them says.

Three of a party of four went down a road that goes out of this city, and one of that four said no before anybody was asked, and a party of four is four. Three barrows stand on that road and nobody in this city can be shown one person standing on it, and nobody in that room said one word about that road at any hour of that day.

At about the half hour after the tenth hour he asked it. He said it to the woman of about twenty-nine who keeps that book's own second slate, at the near end of that bench, in her own ear and at the speed a man says a thing he has been carrying up a road.

"**What is this room called, in a column, on a page, when the only thing anybody will use the name for is to be able to find the door.**"

She did not answer it. The room did not hear it.

She stood at the near end of that bench with her own hands flat on the wood in front of her and she looked at the shelf behind her for a moment and then at the table under the one window, and she said nothing at all, and nobody at that bench or at any other place in that room heard one word of it.

---

Two people in that room wanted two different things out of the rest of that morning and neither of them got what it was.

A man at the step wanted that room settled before the light went off the one window, on whatever it was that room had been doing for a week, and he put it to the middle of that room in the ordinary way at about the eleventh hour. About four people in that room told him that nobody in that room had been told what to settle, and about four told him nothing at all, and nobody in that room settled anything.

"Then what is it we are all standing in this room for."

A woman at the one window wanted the man of about thirty-one to say out loud what he had been asked for by the woman at the near end of that bench, and she said so to him and not to the room. He said it to the top of that bench and not to her, and it was the shortest thing he said that morning.

"**Nobody in this room asked me anything and I answered nobody, and if you want to know what a person says at a bench you have to be standing at the bench, and you were at a window.**"

Nobody in that room said that she was right and nobody improved on it, and she went back to that window and said nothing further about it before the light went.

---

Seven notices have gone out of this city in this flood and every one of them was written by somebody in it. There is no eighth and there is not going to be one, and nobody in that room wrote one, and nobody in that room said on which day the next one goes out. Four of the seven sheets of that shape in this flood carry one place each, and the second of them went out of this city and is not in it, and the third of them is outside this city and is not coming back, and the fourth of them went up that road and is not coming back either.

A woman at the far end of that bench wanted the man with the satchel to stay in that room until the light went, on the grounds that a man who carries things for a living ought to be in a room on the morning a thing goes out of it, and she said it once and he sat down on the end of that bench and did not answer her and got up again about a quarter of an hour later.

"Then you are no use in here this morning."

About four people in that room were near enough to that near end of that bench to have taken in the sound of a man asking a question and not one word of what it was, and about four were not, and neither of those two groups said one word to the other about it at any hour of that day.

A question put twice in daylight, in a room, with a date in chalk on the outside of the door, is a thing this city does not get away with once, and this one went to one woman and not to a room. She did not answer it, nobody in that room heard it, and nobody in that room is going to be told what it was.

---

By the twelfth hour that room had thinned to about four, and a man who had carried a sheet the length of a road had gone back down that stair without being answered, and a woman at the near end of that bench was standing with her own hands flat on the wood in front of her.

No two people in that room knew that either of those two things had happened at the same time, and the second slate stood on its shelf behind that bench with its written face to the plaster and a day cut across the head of it and nothing whatever under that day.
