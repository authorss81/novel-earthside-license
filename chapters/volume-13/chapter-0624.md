# Chapter 0624 — A Woman Alone With Three Things She Keeps From The Sixth Hour To The Tenth

The woman of about twenty-nine who keeps a public register had both hands on the near end of that bench and she drew it out from the wall and turned it about and set it down clear of the wall with the shelf behind it open from the floor to the height of her own shoulder. It was about the sixth hour on the three hundred and ninth day of the Bare Month, a Tuesday, in that room, and the room was empty and the date in chalk on the outside of the door at the foot of the stair at the back of it is the three hundred and ninth day of the Bare Month in full with nothing under it and a bare piece of door about as wide as a hand below that date.

She keeps a register and she keeps a second slate, and the register is on its shelf behind that bench with its spine to the wall and the second slate is up on its edge on the same shelf with its written face turned to the plaster, a day cut across the head of it and nothing whatever under that day. The page of that register that is open is ruled into three columns down it, and the one in the middle of them is the one that holds figures: five of them, one under another, entered in the order they were made and each with its own day written in beside it, and not one of the five has been struck out of anything. The column on the right of that page is the same width as the one beside it and has nothing whatever in it at all, from the head of that page to the foot of it. She looked at that column for about as long as it takes a person to look at one thing, and she did not write in it.

---

A man came up that stair at about the seventh hour with a slate of his own under his arm and he said the thing that is said, to her, in the ordinary way, and what he wanted was a line.

"A line. Any line. I do not care which page and I do not care where on it."

The woman of about twenty-nine said the thing that is said, to him, and she did not raise her voice to do it and she did not put her hands in front of the book to do it either.

"**That book does not take a line off anybody this morning and I am not going to ask you which end of your own slate you would want it at, because if I asked you that it would be me taking it.**"

That man said the thing that is said, to her, and he was not going to be put off by a person who was holding a book.

"It is a line about a thing of my own. It is not a figure and it is not anybody's name."

The woman of about twenty-nine said the thing that is said, to him a second time, and she said it the way she had said the first one.

"**Then it is a line you have somewhere to put that is not a page in this city, and I have not been asked what that column on the right of that page is for, and I am not going to be the one who opens it up for a line of yours that you did bring up that stair with the intention of opening it.**"

Nobody in that room argued with her about it. That man stood in the middle of that room for about as long as it takes a person to decide that a woman holding a book is not going to be moved by a man with a slate, and he did not move her, and she did not say a third thing about it, and he went back down that stair with his own slate under his arm and it did not have a line on it, and nobody in that market was going to be told what he had come up that stair for. The woman who keeps that city's only public book has never been asked by anybody in this city what the column on the right of that page is for, and that man came up that stair this morning to find out and went back down it without finding out, and neither of those two facts was put in front of the other one at any hour of that day.

---

She drew the register out from under the second slate with her own two hands and set it down on the bench she had cleared and opened it at the head of the middle column and put the flat of her left hand on the open page to keep it open, and she took her hand off it. There is a length of new rope over the back of the chair at that far end of that bench. It is as good as the day it came into this city. Nobody in that room looked at it once, and no hand in that room picked it up or cut it or took it up or lifted it off that chair, and the man it belongs to was not in that room and has not been asked one single thing about it by anybody in this city at any hour of this flood.

She got down on the floor of that room and looked along the underside of that shelf with her head turned sideways, and she did not put a hand on the shelf and she did not put a hand on the second slate. That slate has been standing on that shelf since before the water came, with a day cut across the head of it and nothing under that day, and nobody in this city has ever been told what is written on it, and it was not picked up and not turned over and not written on at any hour of that morning, and the only hand in that room which came near that shelf was hers and it came off the spine of the book and did not go back to it.

She put the register back on that shelf with its spine to the wall and shut it, and nothing went into the column on the right of that page at any hour of that morning, and the five figures in the column beside it were the same five at the tenth hour that they had been at the sixth hour, and nobody in that room counted them.

---

Three of a party of four went down the road that goes out of this city and the road is shut and it is four hundred miles long, and one of that four said no before anybody was asked, and a party of four is four. Three barrows stand on that road with nothing on any of them and nobody in this city can be shown one person standing on it. Along the top of that road there is a fence of sixteen willow posts with eleven withies bound on it with the same cord, and nobody in this city has measured that fence and nobody in this city has moved a post in it, and whether it stands between the right two things is not a thing anybody has settled. About nine years ago a man took a name off a board he carries under his arm, in his own hand, and he has not said so to anybody, and at the foot of the column between the other two on that board there is a space about as wide as two of his fingers with nothing in it. None of that was in that room at any hour of the three hundred and ninth day of the Bare Month, and the woman who keeps that city's only public book was not asked about one item of it, and she did not raise any of it.

---

At about the tenth hour she went out of that room and down nine steps and out of the door at the foot of that stair, and she stood on the step and looked at the outside of that door from there. The date was in chalk on it in full, and under that date there was nothing at all but a bare piece of door about as wide as a hand, and she gave that bare piece of door about as long as a person gives a thing that is only going to be there tomorrow, and she did not put her hand on it and she did not put a name under a date, and she went back up those nine steps into her own room and left the door where it stood, and the three things she keeps were where they had been at the sixth hour, and the column on the right of that page was bare from the head of it to the foot of it, and nobody in this city has ever been asked what it is for and nobody in this city is going to be told.
