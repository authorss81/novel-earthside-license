# Chapter 0622 — Nine People Asked One Question In Ordinary Conversation Over About An Hour

The man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board had the string at the top of that board over his left shoulder and the flat of his right hand on the top rail of that bench, and he was not trading. It was about the tenth hour on the three hundred and seventh day of the Bare Month, a Sunday, in the room over a market, in daylight, with about nine people in it. He has a board under his arm with nineteen rates on it in three columns, and at the foot of the column between the other two there is a space about as wide as two of his fingers with nothing whatever in it, and he did not put a finger in it and he said nothing about it at any hour of that morning.

The date on the outside of the door at the foot of the stair at the back of that room is in chalk and the three hundred and seventh day of the Bare Month is written on it in full, and there is no name under that date and there is a bare piece of door about as wide as a hand below it. On the table under the one window of that room there is a sheet with a heading across the top of it and one place ruled under that heading with nothing whatever in that one place, and the bone it was folded around is lying in the near fold of it, and nobody in that room had put a hand on that sheet at any hour of that morning.

The register is where it is kept, on that shelf behind that bench, with its spine to the wall. The page of it that is open is ruled into three, one of them at the left-hand edge, one in the middle and one on the right, and the middle one holds five figures put in one under another in the order they were made with a day written in beside each of them and not one of the five struck out, and the column on the right of that page is the same width as the one beside it and is bare from the head of that page to the foot of it. Behind the register, up on its edge on the same shelf with its written face turned to the plaster, a second slate stands with a day cut across the head of it and nothing whatever under that day. It was not picked up and it was not turned over and it was not written on at any hour of that morning, and the only hand in that room which came near that shelf was the keeper's.

---

Somebody in that room said the thing that is said, to the next person along, in the ordinary way, and it was short, and it is the oldest question anybody asks in a room like that one.

"Did anybody ever tell you what that column is for."

That question went round that room in the ordinary course of a morning for about an hour. It was asked of a woman at the near end of the bench, and of a man who was standing at the one window with his hat in his hands, and of a man who had come up that stair at about the tenth hour with nothing in particular in his hands, and of a man who had been in that room since the light, and of others. About four of the people in that room said out loud, in the ordinary way, that they did not know. About four of the people in that room said nothing at all, and said nothing in the ordinary way, and one of the four went on standing where she was standing for about as long as it takes a person to decide that a question like that is not one to be answered in a room.

The woman of about twenty-nine who keeps a public register was at the far end of that bench with the flat of her own left hand on the wood in front of her, and nobody in that room asked her that question at any hour of that morning. She heard it asked four times. She answered a different question three times in that hour, twice for a figure of this city's own with its day beside it and once for a thing she went to the shelf for, and nobody in that room put the two of those together out loud, and this is not the place to say why she was not asked.

---

The man of about thirty-nine took his hand off the top rail of that bench and put it flat on the face of his own board where it stood against the bench, and he said the thing that is said, to the middle of that room, and it took him about four seconds and it was the whole of what that room had.

"**If one of us had been told what that column is for, one of us would have said so in this room by now, and nobody in this city can be shown one person who was ever told.**"

Nobody in that room said that any of it was right. Nobody in that room improved on it, nobody argued with it, nobody thanked him for it, and nobody in that room came at him afterwards with a second question about it. About four people in that room had that in their own heads by that hour and about four did not, and the two of those groups did not say one word to each other about it at any hour before the light went off that one window.

A woman near the step said the thing that is said, to him, and she wanted to be sure of it before the light went.

"You are sure. You have thought about it."

The man of about thirty-nine did not take his hand off the face of his own board, and he said the thing that is said, to her, and it was the third time he had answered the same question in an hour and he answered it the same way all three times.

"**I am as sure as I can be standing up, and I have not been told either, and I would rather this room went on not knowing than had one of us guess at it out loud and have that be the thing that is remembered.**"

Nobody in that room said that she was right and nobody in that room said that he was right, and the woman near the step stood at that step for about as long as it takes a person to be told no twice and then sat down on the end of that bench and said nothing else about it at all. The space at the foot of the column between the other two on that board was the same width it had been at the tenth hour and nothing went into the column on the right of that page at any hour of that morning.

A man who had been in that room since the light came said the thing that is said, to him, across the top of that bench, and it was the third time that morning he had been asked the same thing by a different person.

"You have never asked her, have you."

The man of about thirty-nine said the thing that is said, to that man, and he said it to the board he was holding rather than to the man who had asked it, and it was the shortest thing he said in that hour.

"**I have never asked her one thing about that column, and I am not going to start at the eleventh hour of a Sunday with nine people in this room, and I would take it back tomorrow if somebody in this city did ask her.**"

Nobody in that room thanked him for that and nobody improved on it, and about four people in that room had worked out by then that the two people at the two ends of that bench were the only two in that room who could have said what that column was for, and about four had not, and the two of those groups did not exchange one word about it before the light went off that one window.

---

Two people in that room wanted two different things out of that hour and neither of them got it. A man with his hat in his hands wanted somebody at the far end of that bench to say one sentence out loud about what that column was for, so that he could stop carrying the question about with him, and he asked her for it twice in that hour in the ordinary way, and she said the same short thing both times, which was that she had not been asked and was not going to be asked, and that a person who does not know what a column is for does not write in it. Nobody in that room improved on that and nobody in that room argued with her about it.

The other of the two was a woman who wanted nobody in that room to say anything at all about that column, on the grounds that the moment one of them said a thing about it out loud, that thing would be the thing the room had decided, and there would be a word in the air of that room that four of the nine of them had agreed to and five had not. She said as much to the man with the hat in his hands, once, and he said the opposite of it back to her, and they went on like that for about as long as it takes two people to get to the end of a thing, and neither of them gave the other one word, and about four people in that room heard the whole of both sides of it and about four heard one of them.

A boy of that market came up that stair at about the eleventh hour with a message from a man at the end of the row below, and asked what that room had come to, and a woman told him, in the ordinary way, that nine people were asking each other what a column was for. He went back down with that, and the man at the end of the row below took his cloth in rather than leave it out on the front of his stall, and did not go up that stair at all that day, and nobody in that room knew any of that and nobody in that row was going to be told.

---

At about the near end of eleven miles of flats there is a yard with a stack of boards in it and a board lying face up on the top of that stack with a line of grey grit across the figure on it, and about four people a day walk past the open end of that yard and not one of them stops, and that figure is on no page in this city. There are words that go in places on pages, and a word in a place can be read by anybody four hundred miles off and checked by nobody four hundred miles off, and there is one place in this city with a rule round it and nothing in it, and the same hour in that room established nobody's business at either end of four hundred miles.

That room asked one question in ordinary conversation over about an hour on the three hundred and seventh day of the Bare Month, and about four of the people in it answered it and about four said nothing, and the one person in that room who could have answered it better than anybody else was not asked, and nobody in that room wrote a word in the column on the right of that page, and the one place on that sheet was empty at the tenth hour and empty at the light going off the one window, and a boy went back down that stair that afternoon with nine people asking each other what a column was for in his head, and the man at the end of the row below took his cloth in and has not put it out on the front of that stall since.
