# Chapter 0635 — Eleven Miles Of Flats And A Person With A Slate Under Her Arm Who Was Not Asked Why She Stopped

The man of about twenty-seven who washes that board had both hands on the handle of his own bucket and he walked the top row out along eleven miles of flats with the water in it going from side to side with every step he took. It was about the fifth hour on the three hundred and twentieth day of the Bare Month, a Saturday, in weather that had not decided anything, and the light was flat and there was about a hand's width of standing water in the shadow of the top row and nothing on that ground dried from one hour to the next.

What he carries is a bucket, a rag, and a trough that goes out along that row and comes back on it. The trough and the rag are at the market end of those eleven miles and not on this ground at all. The rag is on the stone lip of a step at the end of that market row where it is put down every morning and left, and the trough was standing against the foot of a wall at the end of that row when he came out of that market before the light.

A channel of wet silt runs along the bottom row of those flats with a bank of it on either side, and the bank on the near side has cut back into the stone of a sill since the water came and has not cut back again since.

---

A person came along that bottom row at about the sixth hour going the other way from him, with a slate under one arm and a tin held in both hands in front of her, and she stopped where the bank of that channel has cut into the stone of a sill.

She stood there a while. She did not put the tin down and she did not open it and she did not take the slate out from under her arm and she did not look along the top row at the man with the bucket.

Nobody on that ground asked her one thing about any part of it. She was not asked why she had stopped and she was not asked what was in the tin and she was not asked what was on the slate under her arm, and when she started walking again she went the same way she had been going, and nobody on that ground thanked her for stopping where she stopped.

---

A man came out along that bottom row at about the seventh hour with a barrow of his own and nothing on it, and he wanted the channel opened at one place so that a barrow could get across it.

"Open that bank and I can get a barrow across it."

The man of about twenty-seven put the flat of his own hand on the bank of that channel and said it to the bank and not to the man with the barrow.

"**I have walked this ground every morning in this flood and I have taken nothing off it and I am not going to open a bank for a barrow with nothing in it at the seventh hour on a Saturday.**"

Nobody on that ground improved on it and nobody thanked him for it. The man with the barrow stood at that bank a while and then went back the way he had come with nothing on it.

A woman who keeps a stall at the near end of that row came out along the top row at about the eighth hour and wanted the bank pulled down at the same place, on the grounds that the water had come over that sill twice since the water came and would come over it again.

"Take that bank down. It has come over twice and it will come over a third."

He said it to her without stopping his walk and it was not a short sentence.

"**Take that bank down and this water has somewhere to go that it has not got at the moment, and the next place it has is a doorway a good way back along this row, and I am not moving it for you and I am not asking you to hold it.**"

Nobody on that ground said that she was right and nobody argued with him about it. She hung nothing on that ground and she went back along the top row and she was not out on that ground again that day, and the bank was standing where it had been at the fifth hour when the light went off it.

---

One kind of thing in this flood was counted on foot out along those flats on four mornings, and those four mornings are the five hundred and eleventh day of this flood and the five hundred and fifteenth and the five hundred and seventeenth and the five hundred and twenty-first. All four of those figures went into a book in a room over a market with a day written in beside each of them, and the four do not agree with one another and were not permitted to agree.

The three hundred and twentieth day of the Bare Month was not one of those four mornings. Nothing came off that ground in the course of it, nothing was written down, and there is no fifth morning of that count in this city and nowhere to put one.

At about the ninth hour the man of about twenty-seven said one more thing out loud on that ground, and he said it to the channel and not to anybody.

"**Four people walked this ground in this flood on four mornings and every one of them went back the same way and I have met them coming the other way and not one of them ever stopped where I am standing now.**"

Nobody on that ground agreed with him out loud and nobody asked him how many mornings he had come out here, and nobody told him what came off those four mornings.

---

Out past the last of that row the room stands over a market with a stair at the foot of it, and on the outside of the door at the bottom of that stair there is a date in chalk. The three hundred and twentieth day of the Bare Month is on that door in full, and there is no name under it, and a bare piece of door about as wide as a hand is below that date.

About the ninth hour the man of about twenty-seven filled his bucket at that channel and carried it back up the top row towards that market. The person with the slate under her arm and the tin in her two hands had gone on along that bottom row before that and she was not on that ground at that hour, and she was not asked anything on it while she was, and nobody on that ground is going to be told where she stopped or what was in the tin or what was on the slate.
