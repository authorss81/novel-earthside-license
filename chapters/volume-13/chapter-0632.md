# Chapter 0632 — A Trough The Length Of A Row And A Rag Put Back On A Stone Lip Before The Light Came Off The Flags

Adrian Vale had both hands on the near end of that trough and he carried it the length of that row of flags with the bucket hanging out of his right hand and a rag over his own shoulder, and he set it down square at the far end of that row against the foot of the wall that the store's board is nailed to. It was about the fourth hour on the three hundred and seventeenth day of the Bare Month, a Wednesday, at one end of that market, in weather, and the light had not come off those flags yet.

He had taken that rag off the stone lip of the step at the end of that market row when he came through it, and that rag is put down on that stone lip every morning and left there, and it went out along that row in his own hand and it was not inside that trough at any hour of that day. He took his own hand off the trough, wiped the outside of it down the length of it with that rag, and put the rag back on the stone lip of that step and left it lying the way he had found it.

The trough, the bucket and that rag are one of each in this city and there is no second set of any of the three in it. The bucket was in his own right hand the whole time and about half of what was in it went across those flags and off the far end of that row into the channel, and the other half stood in the bottom of the trough about a finger's width deep while he had the rag to it.

The stone above that board's top edge keeps the colour it has always kept. It was washed once in the whole of this flood, on a day that is not one of this run of days, by the man who washes that board every morning, and by no second person since. Adrian Vale put the flat of his own hand against the stone at the foot of that wall to hold that trough where he had set it, and his hand did not go above the top edge of that board, and no rag went to that stone, and nothing was washed.

Two kinds of chalk are in this flood, they are not the same chalk, and no hour of that morning put both of them in one hand. The store's own is in a heap on the flags at the end of that row with about a third of it gone into the channel, and its box is on the step above them with the lid of it lying down on the rim and that lid has never shut and there is a thumb's width of grit under the near side of that rim.

Two stalls along, the man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall has his own piece in his own inside breast pocket, and that piece has not been out of that pocket since the two hundred and ninety-eighth day of this flood. Nobody in that market had asked him about it at any hour of any day and nobody asked him about it that morning.

---

About half past the fifth hour a woman came to the front of that store with a hand-basket and wanted a piece of the store's own chalk out of that heap on those flags, and the man at the front of that store told her to go down and take what she wanted out of the heap because the box on that step was empty.

She went down into that heap with the flat of her own right hand, and what came off the edge of it that morning went over the flags in about a hand's width of water and into the channel, and what was left in her hand went back into that box off her own fingers and she put the lid of that box down on the rim of it and it did not shut. Nobody in that row said a word to her about the heap being smaller than it had been and nobody in that row said a word about the lid.

The man of about twenty-seven who washes that board came round the end of that row at about the seventh hour and found that rag on the stone lip of that step where he had put it himself, and he picked it up and went on up the row to that board and put a wet arm across it and did not look for it anywhere else and did not ask anybody where it had been.

---

About the ninth hour the man of about thirty-eight at the salt wharf came up that row from the far end of it with a barrow of his own behind him and its shafts on the ground, his own tally-board turned in against his own hip, and his own knife in the belt of his coat. Nothing was on that barrow and there is no barrow in this city with anything on it on any day of this flood.

He wanted to get that barrow along that row to the step at the end of it, and the trough Adrian Vale had set down at the far end of those flags was square across the only dry ground left there, and Adrian Vale was still at that trough with the bucket in his right hand.

He said it to the trough and not to the man who had put it there, and nobody in that row asked him one thing about his own board.

"**A thing on the ground that a person needs on the ground is a thing a person walks round, and I have never asked anybody in this city where their barrow is to stand and I am not going to start with this trough.**"

Nothing in that row made a better thing of what he had said, nobody there thanked him for it, and nobody said he was right. He took that barrow round the far end of the trough and down the wet edge of those flags to the step and set it down there and came back up the row, and he did not look at the top of the store's board on his way past it, and he did not look at his own tally-board at all.

Nobody has ever been shown where the figure cut into the side of that tally-board came from and nobody in this city has ever asked him about it. Nobody in that row turned that board over on its face, because a board turned over carries what is lying on it to the other side and leaves it lying there, and it was not turned over and it cannot be.

Adrian Vale lifted that trough out of the only dry ground and stood it on its end against the wall to drip, and the man of about thirty-eight did not wait for him and did not thank him and did not know he had done it until he came back up the row and found the way open.

The woman at the front of that store came out of that doorway as he came past and asked him whether he was going up past the wharf that morning, because she had a crate of her own she wanted carried to the top of that row.

He stopped with a hand on the shaft of his own barrow and said it to the flags and not to her.

"**I go up past that wharf and I do not come down this row with other people's crates on my own barrow, and there is a man at the far end of it who will carry a crate for you and he is not worth having at this hour.**"

Nothing on that row made a better thing of it and nobody there thanked him for saying it. She went back into that doorway and shut it behind her and carried nothing up that row, and he went on up it with nothing on his barrow, which is the ordinary condition of every barrow in this city.

A man at the front next along came out and wanted that trough moved six feet off the line of flags, because the water coming off it was running under the boards of his own front.

"That trough is standing where the low ground is. Move it and it will be under my boards."

The woman at the store came out again and wanted it left where it was, and she said it to that trough and not to that man.

"That trough is the only thing on this row holding the water off my step, and if it moves six feet my step is under by supper."

The man of about thirty-eight said it to that trough and to neither of them, and he had a hand on the shaft of his own barrow while he said it.

"**Two of you want that trough moved and one of you wants it where it is, and I have said all morning that I am not going to decide where a thing stands on this row, so put it to whoever set it there.**"

No one on that row made a better thing of that either and nobody there thanked him. The trough stayed on the low ground until Adrian Vale lifted it out of it and stood it on its end against the wall to drip, and neither of those two people said thank you to him for that either, and they did not know whose hands had put it there and were not going to be told.

---

Out at the back of that market the room stands over the row with a stair at the foot of it, and on the outside of the door at the bottom of that stair there is a date in chalk. The three hundred and seventeenth day of the Bare Month is on that door in full, and there is no name under it, and a bare piece of door about as wide as a hand is below that date.

That room had nine or so in it by the ninth hour and not one of them had come for anything. The register stood on its shelf behind that bench with its spine to the wall, and there were five figures in the column that holds them with a day written in beside each, and the column on the right of that page bare from the head of it to its foot. The second slate stood on that same shelf up on its edge with its written face to the plaster, and the only hand in that room which came near that shelf all morning was the keeper's.

A day is cut across the head of that slate and there is nothing whatever under that day.

The heap on the flags at the end of that row was short of what it had been at the fourth hour by about as much as would cover a coin. The rag was on the stone lip of that step at the fourth hour and at the seventh and at the ninth, and it was not inside that trough at any hour of that day, and the man who washes that board found it where he had left it and did not look for it anywhere else and was not told by anybody on that ground whose hands had carried it out and put it back.
