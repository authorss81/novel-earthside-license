# Chapter 0619 — A Hurdle Made And A Bar Put In And Nobody In That Room Asked Him A Thing

The man of about fifty-nine had the near end of that hurdle under his left arm and a bar of wood in his right hand, and he stood it up in the walk at the back of the room over a market and looked at the place where it had been standing. It was about the ninth hour on the three hundred and fourth day of the Bare Month, a Thursday, in that room, in daylight, with about nine people in it. He made that hurdle himself in this flood and he put a new bar in it himself a fortnight ago, and nobody in that room had asked him about either of those two things and nobody in this city has asked him anything at any hour of this flood.

On the outside of the door at the foot of the stair at the back of that room there is a date in chalk, in full, and it is the three hundred and fourth day of the Bare Month, and the space under it is blank the way the space under every date on that door has been. The register stood on its shelf behind that bench with its spine to the wall and the column on the right of that page had nothing whatever in it from the head of that page to the foot of it, and behind the bench, up on its edge on the same shelf with its written face to the plaster, a second slate stands with a day cut across the head of it and nothing whatever under that day, and the only hand in that room which came near that shelf all morning was the keeper's.

---

The man of about fifty-nine said the thing that is said, to that hurdle, and it was about as long as it takes a man to get a hurdle off the ground and no longer.

"**I made that and I put a new bar in it, and the new bar is not the same length as the one that was in it, and nobody in this room is going to ask me which of the two was the right one.**"

Nobody in that room thanked him for saying that and nobody improved on it and nobody argued with it and nobody said that he was right. A woman near the step said the thing that is said, to that hurdle, and she wanted the walk at the back clear for the people coming up and down that stair.

The man of about fifty-nine said the thing that is said, to her, and he lifted the hurdle off the flags as he said it.

"**It was standing in that walk before you came up that stair this morning and nobody sent me to it and nobody asked me to be in this room, and I am taking it out because you have said so and not because anybody put me here.**"

Nobody in that room improved on that and nobody thanked him for it. He got that hurdle up off the flags of that walk again and carried it out through the door at the foot of that stair and stood it against the outside wall of that room, up on its end, and the bar of wood he was carrying went in the top of it, and that walk was clear and the light on that stair was worse than it had been.

---

Nobody in that room asked him where he had come from and nobody in that room asked him whether he had been on the road. Four hundred miles of road go out of this city and three barrows stand on it, one to a place, and there is nothing on any of them and there is not going to be. Three of a party of four went down that road. A party of four is four and one of that four said no before anybody was asked, and nobody in this city has counted anything about that road at any hour of any day, and nobody in that room asked the man standing at the back of it about the three who went down it.

Out past the last named house a man of about sixty has a handcart with a coil of rope in it and a hazel mallet lying across that rope with its head bound in cord. That cart is on that road and it is not one of the three and nobody in this city is going to ask the man of about sixty where his cart has been or who it belongs to when it is not at his own gate. The coil of rope in that cart is a rope that has been used and the cord round the head of that mallet is a cord that has been used, and neither of those is new, and neither of them is on a chair in this city.

A man at the near end of that bench said the thing that is said, to the man with the bar of wood, and he wanted him to stay.

"You are four hundred miles from where you started."

The man of about fifty-nine turned the bar of wood over in his hands and said the thing that is said, to the man who had asked it, and he said it the way he says the rest of it.

"I am not going to say how far I have come and you are not going to ask me, and a bar of wood is a bar of wood and a hurdle is a hurdle and neither of them is on that road and neither of them is on any barrow."

Nobody in that room improved on that and nobody thanked him for it and nobody asked him a second thing. He said one more thing at about the eleventh hour and it was the only thing anybody in that room did not ask him for.

"**I put a bar in that hurdle that is longer than the one that came out of it, and the middle of it is wider now than it was, and it is not going to fail, and nobody in this city is ever going to know that and neither am I going to come back and find out.**"

The hurdle was back against the outside wall of that room by the time the light was off it, and a bar of wood was in the way at the foot of a stair again, and the man of about fifty-nine had gone back down that road he came in on, and the date on the outside of that door was still in chalk with no name under it, and nobody in that room asked him one single thing about the three who went down that road, and he did not say one word about them.
