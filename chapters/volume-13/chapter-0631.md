# Chapter 0631 — A Board Carried Up A Stair And Back Down It And Nobody At Either End Asked Him A Thing

The man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board was on the stair at the back of that room with the string at the top of that board over his left shoulder and the palm of his right hand on the face of it, which kept the face of it turned in against his own ribs. He has nineteen rates on that board in three columns, and at the foot of the column between the other two there is a space about as wide as two of his fingers with nothing whatever in it. He carried that board up that stair the way a man carries a thing he has decided not to put down.

It was about the eighth hour on the three hundred and sixteenth day of the Bare Month, a Tuesday, in the room over a market, in daylight. What is on the outside of the door at the foot of that stair is a date in chalk, and the three hundred and sixteenth day of the Bare Month is on it in full. There is no name under that date and there never has been one, and the door below it is bare for the width of a hand.

Nine or so were in that room at that hour, and every one of them was in it before anything was said in it. The register stood where it always stands, on its shelf behind that bench, with its spine to the wall. Five figures stand in the column that holds them, one under another, with a day written in beside each of the five, and not one of the five is struck out. The column on the right of that page is the same width as the one beside it and is bare from the head of it to its foot.

Behind the register on that same shelf there is a second slate standing on its edge with the written face of it against the plaster. There is a day cut across the head of it and nothing whatever stands under that day. Nothing in that room lifted it, turned it over or wrote on it at any hour of that morning, and the one hand that came near that shelf was the keeper's.

A sheet with a heading across the top of it and one place ruled under that heading lies flat on the table under the one window, and the one place under that heading has nothing whatever in it. The bone that sheet was folded around is lying in the near fold of it, and nobody had put a hand on it since it came up that road. A word in a place on a page is a kind of thing, and the heading over the one place on that sheet is a heading and is not a sentence.

---

A woman with a hand-basket was at the near end of that bench before he had the string off his shoulder, and she put the flat of her own hand on the face of that board below the column between the other two and asked him for one of his rates.

"That one, from the top."

He took his own hand off the face of that board and said it to the board and not to her, and it took him about four seconds.

"**Nineteen of these have got a day standing against them somewhere else in this city and one of them has not, and the one that has not is the one I would have to answer for, and nobody has asked me.**"

Nobody improved on that and nobody thanked him for it and nobody asked him which of the nineteen it was. She read the line off the face of that board from the end where the writing starts and got a rate, and she paid what it was and she went back down that stair, and nobody in that room said that any of it was right.

The one name that is not on that board is not on any page in this city. It is a name he took off that board in his own hand and has not said to anybody, and it was not said out loud in that room that morning and nobody asked after it.

---

Two of the people in that room wanted two different things out of the rest of that morning and neither of the two of them got the thing each of them wanted.

A man at the far end of that bench wanted that board set down flat on the top of the bench, because half the people at that end of the room could not get to the near end of it, and he said so to the middle of that room in the ordinary way.

"Set it down there and half of this room can read it."

The man of about thirty-nine took the string off his shoulder again and gave it back over the same shoulder.

"**I do not set it down in a room, and I have never set it down in a room, and there is nobody in this room that it is for.**"

The woman at the one window wanted him to take that board out of the room and down into the market and stand at the front of the stall she keeps for four hours, because she had already lost one morning at that window and was not going to lose another. She said it to him and she said it twice, once at about the ninth hour and once at about the tenth, and both times she said it without raising her voice.

"I need that at the front of my stall and not up here."

He answered her the second time without moving his hand off the face of his own board, and it was shorter than the first answer had been.

"**I have been up this stair once this week and I go back down it the way I came up it, and you should go down and trade and not stand at a window.**"

Nobody in that room said that she was right and not one person there improved on what he had said to her. She went on standing at that window until the light had come off it and then she went down that stair herself, and she did not thank him and he did not tell her she was welcome to take it.

A boy from that market came up that stair at about the eleventh hour with word from a man at the far end of the row below and asked that room what it had come to, and about four people in it told him nothing and about four told him that a man was sitting at the near end of a bench with a board against his chest doing nothing anybody could see.

---

About nine people were in that room at the ninth hour and about nine were in it at the twelfth, and none of them had been sent for anything. Nobody in that room said out loud what the column on the right of that page is for, and nobody said out loud that they did not know either. The register stayed on that shelf with its spine to the wall and the five figures in the column that holds them were the same five at the thirteenth hour that they had been at the eighth.

This flood has two pieces of chalk in it and they are not one chalk. The heap on the flags at the end of that row is the store's own chalk, with about a third of it gone into the channel. The box it belongs to stands on the step above those flags and its lid lies down on the rim of that box, and that lid has never shut on any day of this flood.

Two stalls along that row the man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall keeps a piece of chalk shut inside the front of his own coat, and that piece went into that pocket on the two hundred and ninety-eighth day of this flood and has stayed in it every hour since. It was there at the eighth hour of that morning and it was there at the thirteenth hour of it, and not one person in that market has ever asked him a single thing about it.

---

About the twelfth hour he went back down that stair with the string over his shoulder and the face of that board turned in against his own ribs. He went along the row at the far end of that city and not through this market, and he did not say where he was going and nobody asked him.

Through the one window of that room you could see the front of that market and the flags below it and the front of the stall next along his own. The woman who keeps that front had a person at it from about the eighth hour until about the half hour after the ninth, and then nobody at it until the light went.

The man who would have been standing at her front in the ordinary course of that row was standing at the near end of a bench two floors above her with a board against his own chest. She took her own morning off that front after the light went and she did not carry her basket back up that stair to ask anybody where he had gone, and nobody was going to tell her.
