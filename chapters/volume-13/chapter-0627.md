# Chapter 0627 — A Hand Goes Into A Pocket And Comes Out Again With Nothing In It

The man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall had the flat of his right hand over his own inside breast pocket, and he turned about on his own heel at the near end of that row and went two steps and turned about again, and his hand was on that pocket the whole of that movement.

It was about the seventh hour on the three hundred and twelfth day of the Bare Month, a Friday, in that market, in ordinary weather. He has a board standing face in against his own trestle with the bare back of it out in the row, and a piece of chalk in that pocket that he does not use. That piece of chalk has not been out of that pocket since the two hundred and ninety-eighth day of this flood, and on the six hundred and twenty-seventh day of this flood that is three hundred and twenty-nine days.

At the end of that row is a step with the store's own box of chalk on it. The lid of that box is lying down on the rim of it and has never shut, and there is a thumb's width of grit under the near side of that rim. The store's own chalk is in a heap on the flags at the end of that row, with about a third of it gone into the channel with the grit on the eighth day of this flood, and there is no second box behind that step.

Those two pieces of chalk are two kinds of chalk. They are not in one hand at any hour of that day, and they were not in one room until the seventh hour of it.

---

A man came along that row at about the eighth hour with a board under his own arm the whole length of that row and a length of cord round it. He carried it to the step at the end of the row and set it down against the wall there, and he asked for a piece of chalk.

There was a woman standing at the heap on the flags at that hour. She went down into that heap with the flat of her right hand to pick up what she wanted and came up out of it with her own hand shut, and when she opened it there was chalk on the thumb of it. She did not look at it, and nobody in that row looked at it either.

She put about as much of that chalk as would cover a coin back into that box on that step and put the lid of that box down on the rim of it, and the lid did not shut, and it has not shut on any other day of this flood either. She went back down that row with her hand in her pocket and nobody in that row said one word to her about the thumb and she said nothing about it to anybody, and what is on that thumb is not on any page in this city.

A woman who had been at the front of that man's own stall at about the eighth hour wanted to know, across the row, whether the box on that step was anybody's. What she wanted it for was not said.

He had his own hand on his own pocket while he answered her.

"**That box has been at the end of this row since before the water came and it belongs to the store and not to me, and if a person in this row wants a thing out of it she can go and ask the man who owns it, and I am two stalls along and I keep a stall.**"

Nobody in that row improved on that and nobody thanked him for it, and the woman went down the row to the store and came back past him with a piece of the store's chalk in her own hand and put it into that box herself, and the lid of that box did not shut when she had done it, and nobody in that row said a word to her about it.

The man with the board said it across the row to the man of about thirty-four, two stalls along. It was short, and it was about that box.

"Get a piece of that and be done with it."

He took his hand off his own pocket to say it to that row, and then put it back on the pocket again.

"**There is a heap of it on those flags and a box of it on that step and neither of them is mine, and I have a piece of my own in that pocket and it has been in there since the two hundred and ninety-eighth day of this flood, and I am not going to take it out this morning because a man with a board wants a line.**"

Nobody in that row improved on that and nobody thanked him for saying it, and the man with the board said nothing further about it and carried his board and his cord on down the row and did not write anything anywhere with anything.

---

Nobody in that row asked him about that pocket at any hour of that day.

He put his hand into it at about the ninth hour, in the ordinary way, the way a man checks for a thing he has not been looking for, and he took it out again with nothing in it. Then he said one thing about having done it, out loud, to the front of his own trestle and to nobody.

"**I put my hand in there and there was nothing to take out, and that is the second time this week, and nobody in this row is going to ask me what I was looking for, and I am not going to be the one who says it.**"

Nobody in that row said a word about it and nobody asked him a second thing and nobody thanked him.

The man of about thirty-eight at the salt wharf was not in that row at any hour of that day, and nobody in that row has ever asked him anything about a figure of this city's own that is cut into the side of a tally-board lying face up on a stack of boards in a yard at the near end of eleven miles of flats, under a line of grey grit that nobody wiped. Nobody can be shown where that figure came from, and about four people a day go past the open end of that yard and not one of them stops at it.

Six forms have been in this city in this flood, and there is a seventh of them on a table under one window with one place ruled under a heading and nothing whatever in that one place, and the seventh is the one that has never once had a figure put in it. A word goes in that one place and not a figure, and it goes up a road that is four hundred miles long to somebody who will read it and cannot check it.

The store's chalk is in a heap on the flags at the end of this row and in a box on the step above it, and in neither of those is a piece that anybody in this row will use to put a line of words under a figure. There has not been one of those on any surface in this city since a sheet went up that road with a line of words under a figure on it.

---

A woman came to the front of that man's stall at about the seventh hour and he had his own hand on his own pocket, and he did not serve her. She went to the stall next along, got what she had come for there, and did not come back.

She told nobody in that row where the man two stalls along was standing, and nobody in that row said anything to him about it. He did not put the flat of his hand over that pocket again until about the eleventh hour, and the two pieces of chalk in this flood are not in the same hand and were not in the same place for one hour of that day.

That row was back to the length it had been at the seventh hour by the tenth. The box on that step was where it had always been with the same lid lying on the same rim, and the heap on those flags was short of what it had been at the seventh hour by about as much as would cover a coin.

The man who had carried a board the length of that row with a cord round it had gone on down the row and had not come back up it. The man of about thirty-four was behind his own trestle with his hand flat over his own pocket, and the piece of chalk in that pocket where it has been since the two hundred and ninety-eighth day of this flood.

Those two pieces of chalk were not in the same hand at any hour of that day, and have not been in the same hand on any day of this flood.
