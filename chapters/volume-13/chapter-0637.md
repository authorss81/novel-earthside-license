# Chapter 0637 — A Board Down At The Fourth Hour And Up Again By The Tenth And A Bench Cleared For A Sheet Nobody Turned

The man of about twenty-seven who washes that board had both hands under the near end of it and he lifted it off the two nails it hangs on and stood it on its lower edge against the foot of that wall so that its face was turned in toward the stone. It was about the fourth hour on the three hundred and twenty-second day of the Bare Month, a Monday, at one end of that market, and the light had not come good on the front of that store.

The rag was on the stone lip of that step where he had put it down himself the evening before, and he took it off that lip and carried it to that board with it. The trough was standing against the foot of that wall with about a foot of water in it from the night before, and the bucket was beside that trough on the flags, and none of the three of them was in the trough at any hour of that day.

That board carries three sets of figures of this city's own, none of the three rubbed out, and not one of the three carrying a line of words under it. From the day after a sheet went up that road carrying a line of words set under a figure there has been no surface in this city with a line of words under a figure on it at all.

The stone above the top edge of that board went on up in the colour it keeps and nothing was washed on it that morning. It was washed once in the whole of this flood by the man who has that board in his own hands every morning of it, and no second person has washed it since and nobody has ever put a hand on that stone, and it was not washed again on that day by anybody at all.

---

A man came to the foot of that wall at about the fifth hour to copy one of the figures off it and he had to stand there until that board went back up on its two nails, and he did not say anything about the waiting and he did not say thank you when it did.

"I cannot get at that board with it down."

A woman came at about the seventh hour for the same reason and had to wait longer, and she said the same thing in the same words at the foot of that wall, and she copied the figure she came for and put the slate she had copied it onto under her arm and went on down that row.

The man of about twenty-seven said one thing out loud to that board while he had it down flat on the flags in front of him with the rag in his own hand, and nobody in that row had asked him for it.

"**Three sets of figures on that board and not one of them has been rubbed out, and nobody at this end of this market has ever said which of the three is the one to read.**"

Nobody in that row improved on it and nobody thanked him for it and nobody told him which of the three was the one they read.

---

He washed that board at the fourth hour and once more at the ninth and put it back up on the same two nails at about the half hour after the ninth. The near one of those two nails had been worked up out of that wall by the fall of that board on a great many mornings until the top of it stood about a finger's width proud of the stone.

He had a thin wedge of wood in the heel of his own hand. He knocked that nail back and drove the wedge under the head of it and the top edge of that board came level with the sill of that wall for the first time in this flood, and he hung the whole weight of the board on the two nails and stepped back and looked at it.

Nobody in that row noticed it happen and nobody has noticed it since and no person in that market has ever said anything to him about the level of the top edge of that board.

A woman at the stall next along called across that row to ask him whether he was going to be at the front of it that morning, and he said it to that board and not to her.

"**I wash a board and I go back to my own front and I am not standing here for anybody to look at me while I do it, and you have been at that front since before the light like everybody else in this row.**"

Nothing in that row made a better thing of it and nobody there thanked him for saying it. She went along that row to the one beyond and did not come back to that end of it that day, and he did not know that and was not told.

He said one more thing at about the tenth hour with his own hand flat on the face of that board, and nobody in this city has ever asked him for it.

"**That board has been washed every morning in this flood and the stone above it has been washed once, and I have never worked out which of the two of those is the thing people mean when they say somebody keeps a place clean.**"

Nobody in that row agreed with him out loud and nobody asked him which of the two he had meant.

---

At about the seventh hour the woman who keeps that city's only public register drew a bench out from under a shelf in the room over that market and set it down clear of the wall, and she cleared the top of that bench with the flat of her own hand.

She carried that sheet to that bench and laid it down in the position it was already in, with the heading at the top and the one place ruled under that heading empty, and the bone still lying in the near fold of it. Nobody turned that sheet over at any hour of that afternoon and nobody put a weight on it and nobody read a word off it, because there is nothing in its one place to read.

What is on the outside of the door at the foot of the stair at the back of that room is a date in chalk. The three hundred and twenty-second day of the Bare Month is on that door in full, and there is no name under it, and the door below that date is bare for the width of a hand.

The register went back on its shelf behind that bench at about the eighth hour with its spine to the wall. Five figures stand in the column that holds them with a day written in beside each of them and none of the five struck out.

The column on the right of that page is bare from the head of it to its foot. On the same shelf behind it a second slate stands on its edge with its written face to the plaster. There is a day cut across its head and nothing whatever stands under that day. It did not move at any hour of that afternoon and the only hand in that room which came near that shelf was the keeper's.

A word in a place on a page is a kind of thing and there is one of them lying on that bench with nothing in it. Nothing went into that one place at any hour of that day and nothing went into the column on the right of that page at any hour of it, and nothing went up the four-hundred-mile road out of this city on that day, and nothing has gone up it at any hour since the sheet that carries the line of words went.

---

The man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board went up that row about the half hour after the ninth with his own string over his own shoulder and his own board under his arm with nineteen rates on it in three columns, and he was stopped at two places in that row and set that board down on the flags both times and picked it up again both times, and he did not go into the room over that market at any hour of that day.

About the tenth hour that board was back on its two nails and the top edge of it was level with the sill of that wall for the first time in this flood, and there was a thin wedge of wood under the head of the near nail that nobody in that row had watched go in.

In the room over that market a bench had been cleared at about the seventh hour and a sheet had been laid on it in the position it was already in and nobody had turned it, and a man came up that stair at about the ninth hour to fetch a thing he had left on a chair, saw that bench cleared and that sheet lying on it, said nothing whatever about either of them, and went back down again with the thing he had come for.
