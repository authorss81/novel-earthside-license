# Chapter 0633 — A Woman Names Out Loud What A Word In That Column Is Going To Cost And It Is Not Her Own Cost

The woman of about twenty-nine who keeps a public register stood at the near end of that bench with both her hands flat on the wood and her fingers spread on it, and she was not behind it. It was about the seventh hour on the three hundred and eighteenth day of the Bare Month, a Thursday, in the room over a market, in daylight, and about nine people were in that room and not one of them had been sent for anything.

What is on the outside of the door at the foot of the stair at the back of that room is a date in chalk, and the three hundred and eighteenth day of the Bare Month is on it in full. Nothing is under that date and the door below it is bare for the width of a hand.

That register was lying on its shelf behind that bench with its spine to the wall, and that page of it is ruled into three. Down the middle one there are five figures, entered in the order they were made and each with its own day written in beside it. Not one of the five has been struck out of anything.

The one on the right of that page is the same width as the one beside it and has nothing whatever in it at all, from the head of that page to its foot. Behind the register on that same shelf a second slate stands up on its edge with its written face to the plaster. A day is cut across the head of it and there is nothing whatever under that day, and it was not picked up and not turned over and not written on at any hour of that morning.

A sheet with a heading across the top of it and one place ruled under that heading lies flat on the table under the one window and there is nothing whatever in that one place. The bone that sheet was folded around is lying in the near fold of it. A word in a place on a page is a kind of thing and this city has two of them standing in this room, and neither one of them had been read out in that room at any hour.

---

A man came up that stair at about the seventh hour with a lump of the store's own chalk in his own hand, and he put it on the top of that bench and then went back down again and left it there.

The man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall took that lump up off the bench, wrote on a slate of his own, and put the slate down on that bench and the chalk lump beside it. Adrian Vale had the slate in his own hands and the chalk lump was on the bench next to his own knee and he was not asked to read that slate and he did not ask to read it.

He looked at what was on it. There was one thing written on the face of that slate and it was written in a hand that nobody at that end of the market can read, and it stayed that way for the rest of that morning and for the rest of that day. A word in a place is read by anybody four hundred miles off and checked by nobody four hundred miles off, and this one could not be read by a person standing in that room either.

---

At about the eighth hour the woman of about twenty-nine said it to that room, in the local language, and she said it to the middle of it and not to the man of about thirty-four.

"**That word is his to put in and not mine, and in about a year nobody in this city will be able to show that a person chose to leave that column alone.**"

Nobody improved on it and nobody argued with it and nobody said that she was right. Nobody in that room thanked her for saying it, and the man it was about did not thank her for saying it either, and he did not say one word back.

A man at the far end of that bench asked her, in the ordinary way, whether anybody in that room thought that was a thing to say out loud in front of nine people. She gave him the same answer in the same words and a little more slowly than the first time, and he did not ask her a third time.

---

Two of the people in that room wanted that morning to come out in two different ways and it came out in neither of them.

A man standing at the step wanted that slate read out in that room so that the nine people in it would have the word in their own heads before the light went off the one window.

"Read it out in this room. Once, out loud."

He asked for that twice and he asked the keeper the second time and not the man who had written on the slate, and nobody read it out either of the two times he asked.

A woman at the one window wanted that slate turned face down and put back on the shelf where it came from, on the grounds that a thing lying face up in a room for a whole morning is a thing that will be copied out of by somebody before the light goes.

"Turn it over and put it back where it came from."

She said that to the man of about thirty-four and not to the keeper, and she said it once.

He answered her without picking the slate up off that bench.

"**It lies there because I wrote on it and I am not turning it over and I am not reading it out, and you should go and do the thing you came up that stair for.**"

Nobody said she was right and nobody made a better thing of it. The slate lay on that bench face up until the light went off the one window and nobody in that room turned it over and nobody copied anything off it.

There are two kinds of chalk in this flood, they are not one chalk, and they were never in one hand at any hour of that day. The store's own had come up that stair in a man's own hand and was on the top of that bench, and the man of about thirty-four's own piece was in his own inside breast pocket at the seventh hour of that morning and at the tenth hour of it. It has not been out of that pocket since the two hundred and ninety-eighth day of this flood and nobody in that room asked him one thing about it.

---

The road that page came up is four hundred miles long and it comes on the quarterly round, and nobody in that room went into it and nobody in that room came back from it. Seven documents have come up that road into this city or gone out of it in this flood, and four of the seven carry one place each, and the fourth of those four is on that table under that window with nothing whatever in its one place, and there is no page in this city with a word in one of those four places in it.

About four people in that room had worked out before she opened her mouth what the cost of a word in that column was going to be, and about four had not, and nobody told the four who had not what she had said, and they went on with the morning they had been having.

About the tenth hour that room had about four people left in it, and the light had come off the one window onto the bare column and onto the slate lying face up beside it.

The man of about thirty-four went back down that stair with his own two hands empty. The woman who sells at the front of two stalls along his own took that morning at that front with nobody standing at it, and she did not carry her basket up that stair to ask anybody where he had gone. Adrian Vale carried the lump of chalk that was not his down that stair in his own closed hand, and nobody had asked him to read a word on that slate and he had not offered to.
