# Chapter 0615 — Four Hundred Yards Of Flags And Water Going Down Twice

Adrian Vale had both hands on the near end of that trough and he set it down at the foot of the poles and the water in it went over the lip and along the ground at the foot of them, and he went back up the row with the bucket in his right hand and the rag over his shoulder. It was about the fourth hour on the three hundredth day of the Bare Month, a Sunday, at one end of that market, in weather, and the row of flags runs about four hundred yards from the near gate to the last pole and the light had not come off it yet.

Four days of weather went through that row. Every one of those lines of flags is doubled back on itself where the poles lean, and the two sides of every one of them are wet to the top, and the whole length of that row is standing in about a hand's width of water with a slow movement in it. He wanted them dry by the fourth hour and the fourth hour went past them with them wet, and there is nothing in this city that dries cloth except a wind that has not come.

The rag is on the stone lip of the step at the end of that market where it is put down every morning, and it was not in that trough and it was not in that bucket and it did not come out along that row at all. The store's own chalk was in its box on that step with the lid of that box lying down on the rim of it, and a thumb's width of grit is under the near side of that rim. Two stalls along the man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall had the flat of his right hand over his own inside breast pocket, and that pocket has a piece of chalk in it that has not been out of it since the two hundred and ninety-eighth day of this flood, and it was in that pocket and the store's chalk was in that box and no hand on that row had both of them in it at any hour of that day.

---

The man of about twenty-seven who washes that board came along that row before Adrian Vale was on it. He has a bucket and a rag and a wall he washed once in this flood and has not washed since, and that wall is the stone above the top edge of the board at the end of that market and he did not put a hand on that stone at any hour of that day. He went the length of that row with his bucket and looked at the flags and did not use them, and he did not take one of those lines down off its poles and he did not hang anything of his own on any of them, and he said the thing that is said, to the flags, at the near end of them, and it took him about as long as it takes a man to lift a bucket.

"**I have walked this row every morning in this flood and I have never once got anything off it, and there is no wind coming, and I am not going to stand in the wet here and take somebody else's flags down to prove that I can.**"

Nobody on that row improved on what he said and nobody thanked him for it and nobody asked him what he thought would dry them. He went back down the row with his bucket to the near gate and did not say one word about it again that day, and nobody in that row said to him that he had been there first.

A woman at the pole next along the near gate said the thing that is said, to the trough at the foot of the poles, and she wanted the ground back.

"That is where I stand."

The man of about twenty-seven said the thing that is said, to her, without stopping in his walk, and it was not a short sentence.

"**That trough is holding the water off the bottom of these poles and if it is not standing here it is standing in the front of somebody's boards further down, and I am not going to carry it back to the store with me and I am not asking you to hold it.**"

Nobody on that row said that she was right and nobody argued with him about it and nobody improved on it. She stood there about as long as it takes a person to decide not to be the one who moves, and then she hung her own washing on the line next along and got her own two buckets in underneath it and went off along the row, and the trough stayed at the foot of the poles and her buckets were not under her own line and she was not in that row again that day.

---

The water at the near gate went down once about the fifth hour and Adrian Vale was at the far end of that row when it did, and it went down a second time about the seventh hour and he was at the near end when that happened, and both of those times it went down without anything in this city touching it. There is a length of cord at that near gate, tied off round the near gatepost and running out to a post further down, and it has been there since before this flood, and nobody has ever taken it up to say what it holds, and no hand on that row lifted it at any hour of that day and nobody cut it and nobody took it up.

What came off that row went down it. He carried the trough the length of those four hundred yards twice with the water in it and put it out at the far end each time, and the second lot of it came back under the boards of a stall at the far end of that market and stood about a foot deep in the doorway of it before the light was good. A man came out of that doorway and stood in his own doorway and then went for a board and put it down across the front of it and came back in, and he said nothing about it to anybody, and nobody in that row said a word to him about where the water came from.

Adrian Vale carried the trough back up the row with the flag lines dripping off it on both sides. Nobody on that row thanked him for that and nobody told him the row was any better for it and nobody in that row was waiting for him to get anything dry, and the man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall was two stalls along with his hand over his own pocket and said nothing to him, and the man of about twenty-seven was at the near gate with his bucket and did not look up the row again.

---

Somebody counted one kind of thing on foot out along those flats on four mornings of this flood — the five hundred and eleventh day of it, and the fifteenth, and the seventeenth, and the twenty-first — and all four of those went into a book in a room over a market, and the four do not agree with one another and were not allowed to. This morning was not one of the four and nothing came off that ground, and nothing came off those flags either, and there is nothing in this city that could have taken a figure off either.

The board a man at the far end of that row had laid across his own doorway was still holding at the eighth hour and the man behind it had not come out again, and the four days of weather that went through that row are not going to be argued with by anybody standing at either end of it, and there has not been a wind in this city that ever came when it was wanted.
