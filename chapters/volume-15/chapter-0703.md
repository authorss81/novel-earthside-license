# Chapter 0703 — A Length Of Cord On A Gate-Peg At A Near Gate That Was There The Day Before, A Trough Put Down Where A Trough Is Not, And A Barrow That Went Round By The Low Side For Nothing

Adrian Vale had both hands under the near end of a trough and he set it down on the high ground at that near gate and took his own two hands out from under it, and the bucket came along in his own right hand and the rag was over his own shoulder and it was not inside that trough at any hour of that day.

It was about the fourth hour on the three hundred and eighty-eighth day of the Bare Month, a Thursday, in weather, out along eleven miles of flats with a channel of wet silt running along the bottom row of them, and the light had not come good along the tops of that row of flats, and nothing on that ground dried from one hour to the next.

At that near gate there is a peg driven into the gate post, and a length of cord is coiled on that peg, and the loop of it goes over the top of the peg and under itself. He put his own fingers under the top of that loop and lifted. The cord came up off the wood as far as the thickness of two of his fingers and then stopped dead, and the reason it stopped is that it had gone flat and had gone round that peg twice underneath itself and had not been lifted off it by anybody in this flood. He wound it back down again in the same order it had been in, over the top of the peg and under itself, and it was on that peg at the fourth hour of that day and it was on that peg at the fourth hour of the day before.

He wanted that cord off that peg and coiled on the step where a person could find it. It did not come off. He did not say why, and nobody at that gate said a word about it, and the gate he wanted it on the step of is not latched to anything and stands open against its own post the whole of this flood.

---

A trough, a bucket and a rag are one of each in this city and there is no second set of any of them in it, and he had carried the one trough and the one bucket up that row of flats from where the water was standing. He set the trough down against the wall on the high side of that near gate, above the place where the low ground of that row stands under, and he set the bucket down on the high ground beside the trough, and he put the rag down on the stone lip of the step at that gate and not inside the trough. The lip of that step is a stone lip and the rag lay along it, and the water that came out of the trough when it was set down ran in a long run down the high side of that ground and away off the flags at the edge of it.

While he was standing there he put his own left hand flat on the face of that gate post and pushed the peg in it round with his own thumb, and the peg was loose in the post and it turned a quarter of a turn under his hand, and the gate, which is fastened to nothing and has stood open against that post since the water came, came across of its own accord and lay against the rail at the side of that opening. He took his hand off the post and the gate stayed where it had come to.

The man of about thirty-eight at the salt wharf came down that row of flats at about the seventh hour with his own barrow empty and his own knife in his belt and got as far as that near gate, and the gate was against that rail, and he stopped in front of it, and he looked at the rail and then at the post and then at the length of cord on the peg in the post. He did not touch the gate and he did not touch the cord. He turned about and went back along that row of flats and round the low side of that ground, and came up to that near gate from the other way with his barrow going round a place where it could go.

"**That gate has been standing open against that post since the water came and somebody has shut it this morning, and I have gone round by the low side for nothing with a barrow that will not go through there, and I am not going to stand here and ask anybody whose two hands did it.**"

Nobody answered him. Nobody thanked him. Nobody told him anything about that peg, and nobody in that city ever told him anything about that peg, and Adrian Vale was at the far end of that row of flats with his own two hands in his own pockets and did not hear one word of that and was not told one word of it and did not know it had been said.

---

That man came back to that near gate with his barrow when he had been round the low side, and the trough was standing against the wall above the low ground where a trough is not, and the bucket was standing on the high ground beside it. He put his own right hand on the rim of that bucket and turned it over on its side, and then he put it back down and dropped it into the trough and let go of it, and it went down into the water and stood there with a hand's width of the top of it out. He worked the handle up and down until it came up full and the water came over the rim of it and ran down the outside of it onto the flags, and then he took that bucket by the handle and carried it down that row of flats to his own yard at the near end, and the barrow went back with it, and he did not look at the cord on that peg again and he did not look at the rag on the stone lip.

The only thing that had come off that row of flats that morning was a full bucket, and the bucket went down to a yard at the near end and the trough stayed against that wall with nothing beside it. The cord was on the peg in that post with the loop of it over the top and under itself in the same order, and the gate was lying against that rail where Adrian Vale's own hand had left it earlier that morning, and Adrian Vale was at the far end of that row of flats at that hour with his own two hands in his own pockets and has not known to this hour that that gate came across at all, and nobody at that gate had seen a hand go near that post at any hour of that day.
