# Chapter 0738 — A Room Over A Market On A Thursday Morning With About Four People In It Wanting To Be Able To Say Who Had Been In That Room On A Morning And There Being Nothing In That Room That Will Do It, The Ground At The Foot Of The Stair And Seven Sheets This City Has Had For Carrying A Thing Out Of A Room And There Not Going To Be An Eighth

The man of about thirty-four had both hands flat on the sill of the one window of that room with his two thumbs hooked over the edge of it, and he was looking down through that glass at the ground at the foot of the stair at the back of that building, and there was nothing on that ground that a person standing in that room could have named.

It was about the fourth hour on the four hundred and twenty-third day of the Bare Month, a Thursday, in that room over that market, and about nine people were in it over the course of that morning, and nothing has ever been put on the wall along the far side of that room and there is nothing on it and nobody has ever put anything on it.

The ground at the foot of that stair is a strip of ground running from the bottom step of it to wherever the paving gives out, and about four people a day walk on it in the ordinary course and about four of them are getting a thing off the thing they are walking past and about four of them are going over it with their hands empty, and nobody has ever kept that number and it is not the same twice and there is no surface in this city that would take it.

This city has had seven sheets for carrying a thing out of a room and not one of them of that kind is in this city now, and all seven of them went out of it on seven different mornings, and there is not going to be an eighth one. The oldest instrument of that kind — a thing arrives and the person who reads it out says out loud what it is for before anybody has asked him — was not used at any hour of this flood, and there is no morning before one to put it in.

---

At about the fifth hour the woman of about twenty-nine who keeps a public register put both hands flat on the wood of that bench and said out loud that there was no way in that room to find out who had been in it.

"**There is no way in this room to find out who has been in it on any morning since the day before, and there was one before the day before, and nobody in this room is going to find that out and nobody outside it is going to be asked.**"

Nobody thanked her for saying that and nobody improved on one word of it and nobody asked her how she knew what there had been before the day before.

At about the sixth hour the man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall came off that sill and said the same thing out loud to the middle of that floor.

"**I am the one who did that. I did it in one morning and I did not know I was doing it, and what I have taken off is not the people who walk on that ground. It is the only way anybody ever had of telling afterwards who went up this stair.**"

Nobody in that room asked him one word about that and nobody thanked him for it and nobody improved on one word of it and nobody said he was right and nobody said he was wrong.

At about the seventh hour the man of about thirty-eight at the salt wharf said that every one of those seven sheets had gone up a road a long way out of this city and that none of them would be coming back down it, and the man of about thirty-one who carries things for a living came up that stair at about the eighth hour and stood at the far end of that bench and did not say one word at any hour of that morning, and the woman of about fifty-two told him the one sentence she had been given and he did not repeat it to anybody. He put his own two hands flat on the wood of that bench at the near end of it and kept them there while he counted something under his breath that nobody standing near enough to hear it heard, and then he took them off it again and put them flat on his own knees, and the wood of that bench at that end had nothing on it and had had nothing on it all morning.

---

At about the ninth hour that man of about thirty-four put his own two hands flat on the sill of that window one more time and looked down through that glass at that ground until the light had come over the roof above it and came across it from the step outwards. He took his hands off that sill and came away from that window, and nobody in that room went to that sill after him and looked down out of it, and he did not look down out of it again at any hour of that day.
