# Chapter 0314 — The Column That Is Not There

On the fifty-ninth day of the Short Month, a Sunday, a room over a market had about nine people in it and a date on its door, and the light came in on the north side and went off the low ground early and came back up the far wall, and the cold was in the shutters and in the wax of a slate, and what was under about four pairs of hands that morning was a register with nine years in it, a second slate, a box with a deed in it and a sheet pinned to the corner of the deed, and a waxed slate in a case.

---

The register was on the table open at a page about forty days back, and the woman of about twenty-nine who keeps a public register had it open at that page on purpose and had said so at the seventh hour in about nine words.

Three columns. What was asked, what came back, and the day. She has kept it that way nine years and has never once wanted a fourth, and she wanted a fourth on Tuesday, and she asked a room of about nine people to tell her what a register is for before she put one in.

Hettis Roke came in about the eighth hour and put a waxed slate on the table face up, which is the first thing anybody in that room has ever seen him do with it, and then turned it face down, which is the second.

"**You have three columns and the third one is the day the room met, and the day a sheet went out is not in any of them.**" He put two fingers on the case. "That is why you cannot check it, and I have known that for nineteen years, and I have never once wanted to be able to."

"Have you got a register."

"**I have got a roll and I am not going to show it to anybody in this room, and I would like it said that I said that.**" A gate, a keeper, a set of days, and nothing else, and no column in it for a person. It has been kept in that shape for longer than the oldest thing any of the four men who keeps it has. He said the rest of it to the table and did not improve on it, and about four people in that room have worked out since that a man who refuses a room a roll and then says the shape of it out loud is a man who wants a room to know what it is not being shown, and that those are not the same thing and only one of them is a courtesy.

---

The man of about forty-three read the register from the top of the page down and got to the bottom and read the last two rows again.

He keeps nine boxes of other people's papers for eleven years and he keeps a list of what is in each box in his own hand, and the list has four columns, and one of them is a number and one of them is a room and one of them is a person and one of them is a day. So he has the column she is asking for, in a book, on a shelf, in a room he does not let anybody into, and he has never once read it back against a day and could not tell her the order two of the boxes went in.

"**You could read it back.**"

"I could read it back in about a quarter of an hour and I have not done it in eleven years. If I read it back I will find nine mistakes, and then I will have to decide what to do about nine mistakes, and I have never in my life wanted to decide what to do about anything on a Sunday."

Nobody laughed, and about four people in that room have worked out since that a man who keeps nine boxes and has not read his own index back in eleven years is not careless and is not afraid, and that the reason is the third one, and that the third one is in the room.

Adrian read the register over the woman of about twenty-nine's shoulder, both columns, and read the last two rows twice as well, and about four people in that room have worked out since that a man who reads the same two rows twice and says nothing about them has understood something the woman keeping the book has not said out loud, and that the woman of about twenty-nine said his name once and then nothing else about him, and that nobody in that room has asked him one question about the boundary or about the book.

---

The finding came out of a mouth at about the third hour of the afternoon and it came out of the man of about forty-three and not out of anybody else, and about four people in that room have worked out since that the thing a room can be given by a person in it is not usually the thing it is looking for.

"**You are not missing a column for the day the sheet went out. You are missing it because of the four days.**" He put his hand flat on the table about a foot from the register and left it there. "You have been running a register for nine years that cannot tell a question that took four days from a question that took nine. Every answer in it is attached to the day you heard it and not the day you asked."

"Nobody in four hundred miles can check your nine years."

"**And I could not check it either, and I have wanted for about four of my eleven years to have somebody ask me whether one word in one of those nine boxes is true, and there is not one column in this book that would let anybody do that, and that is not your fault and it is not mine. It is the shape it is because nobody ever wrote it down.**"

Nobody answered him for about as long as it takes to fill a pail and the woman of about twenty-nine put her hand flat on the second slate and did not write anything on it.

"I am not going to put a fourth column in a register I have kept for nine years on a Sunday, and I am not going to refuse one either, and I would like both of those said out loud in a room with a date on the door, because about four of you have watched me do this before."

---

At about the fifth hour Roke picked the waxed slate up off the table, turned it over once in his hand and put it back in its case, and about four people in that room have worked out since that a man who has just refused a room a roll and turned a thing face down and then said nothing for seven hours has not got a reason in him that he cannot say.

There are eight people of his own on no page in four hundred miles. He has wanted a page for them for nine years. He did not ask for one this afternoon and he did not give a reason, and about four people in that room have worked out that the not-giving is the reason and that the woman of about twenty-nine, who has refused three things out loud and named the cost of each one before she said it, looked at him for about four seconds and then wrote the fifty-ninth on the second slate and nothing under it.

"Nineteen stones go into a cell in nine words and a single number goes at the bottom of it." He said it to the table, not to anybody. "**A number at the bottom of a thing is the only part of it anybody ever sends for, and I am not putting eight names under a number on anybody's wall in this city.**"

The day after the fifty-ninth is the last day anybody in this city can write in a form that exists, and nobody in that room said so out loud, and the date on the door was still the fifty-ninth at the fifth hour, and about four people in four hundred miles have worked out since that a room which has found out that its own calendar has run out and has agreed a day to do something about it is carrying two dates at once, and that the one in chalk is the one anybody can check, and that there is one of them left and she is the one who has to write it.
