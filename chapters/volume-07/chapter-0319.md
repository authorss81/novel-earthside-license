# Chapter 0319 — A Gate, And A Boy Who Says It Back

Four streets came together at a gate where the ground had been worn down about two inches in about a hundred years of people going over it, and the light came up the middle of the four of them at about the seventh hour on the fourth day of that month, a Friday, and stopped about nine feet short of the gate, and the cold was out of all four streets at once and in the wet of a yoke and in the iron of a bar stood against a post, and what was under about four pairs of hands that morning was a bar, a barrow with a spade lying across its handles, a wooden yoke with a strap, and two pails.

---

The man of about thirty-six had been at that gate since before the light and about four people in four hundred miles have worked out since that he keeps nothing, that he holds gates on a turn, and that a man who holds gates on a turn learns four gates in about four months and one gate in about nineteen years, and that this is not the same job four times.

The pails went through at the seventh hour and came back at the fourth and the boy of about eleven who carried them did not stop at the gate either way and had not stopped at the gate since he was about eight, and about four people in that market have worked out since that a boy who does not stop at a gate is a boy who has decided the gate is not his, and that the gatekeeper has decided the same about the boy, and that between the two of them a gate on a road where four streets come together is the only place in four hundred miles where two people are obliged to stand still and neither of them is.

The bar was against the post and the bootful was emptied at the near end of the lane before he said anything, and about four people on that road have worked out since that the gate is the only trade anybody in this flood has where the man is better than the page, and that emptying a bootful at a gate is not courtesy, it is a man putting the day somewhere it cannot be put on paper.

Then he said the day out loud to the boy's back, and about four people in four hundred miles have worked out since that he said it the way a man says a thing he has said before, and that a man of thirty-six who has held gates on a turn for nineteen years had never once said a day out loud at one of them, and that he said it to a boy carrying two pails because the boy was the only person on that road who could not be asked to keep a page about it.

"The fourth day of the Bare Month."

The boy of about eleven put the yoke up on his shoulder inside the gate and turned round, and about four people in that market have worked out since that a boy who turns round at a gate when a man has spoken to him has worked out inside about four seconds that a sentence with a number in it is worth a sentence without one, and that he has learned it from a man who carries things and is about eleven.

"**The fourth day of the Bare Month.** My mother has not got one of them for the last two days and she has been doing it out loud at the door."

The man of about thirty-six looked at the bar and did not turn round to the road.

"**That is right, and I will tell you why she is doing it at the door, and it is because there is nothing to write it on, and I am saying it out loud at a gate because there is nothing to write it on either, and that is the entire reason and I am not going to say it twice.**"

---

He did not say it again that day and about four people on that road have worked out since that a man who says a thing out loud once at a gate has not made a habit of it and has also not stopped, and that a man who holds a gate on a turn is a man whose whole trade is turning up at an hour somebody else has chosen, and that the fourth day of a month with a word on it and no number under the word is going to be a Saturday tomorrow and a Sunday the day after, and that a man at a gate is going to have to find out what tomorrow is from somebody, and that the only person on that road who will have it is a boy of about eleven with a yoke.

The pails went out at the fourth hour and the water went into them and about four people in that market have worked out since that a man who holds a gate at the end of a turn spends about as long as it takes to fill a pail in the middle of it doing nothing, and that the doing of nothing at a gate is the only part of the job that is not a trade, and that it is the part he has just put a sentence into.

Then a woman of about twenty-eight came over the worn ground and stopped at the gate, which she has not done since the days went back, and about four people in four hundred miles have worked out since that a person who stops at a gate in this flood is a person who has come to be asked something, and that the man of about thirty-six emptied his bootful at the near end of the lane before he said anything, and that the woman did not ask him anything, and that he did not ask her anything, and that the two of them stood at a bar with nothing said in it, and that the woman went through.

"You did not ask her. She did not ask you either."

Nobody had said it. It came out of the bar's own length of oak, which is a thing a man has said at four gates in nineteen years and which about four people in that market have worked out since is the only sentence in this flood that has never once been entered on anything, and that a man who says it to a bar is a man who has worked out that the bar is the only party in four hundred miles that cannot be put in a column.

"**That is the trade. Nineteen years and the whole of it is that, and nobody has ever put it on a wall, and I have said it to a piece of oak twice in one afternoon and I would like the oak to have it.**" He put his hand on the bar and did not lift it.

---

The boy came back at the fourth hour and the man of about thirty-six was still there, and the bar was in the same bracket and the bootful was empty and the two pails had the ground colour of the four streets in them, and the light had gone off the worn ground by then and come back up the four streets one at a time.

He said the day out loud a second time and the boy said it back the second time, and neither of them asked the other anything, and about four people in four hundred miles have worked out since that a gatekeeper on a turn at the end of four streets has begun saying what day it is to a child who carries water twice a day, and that no form exists for it, and that no page exists for it, and that it is the only instrument in this city that is cheaper than a chalk mark, and that a woman of about fifty at a shut gate on another road has been writing a date on a post every morning for nine years and has been asked nothing and has said nothing to anybody about it, and that the two of them have never met and neither of them has ever been asked.
