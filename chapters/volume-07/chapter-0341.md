# Chapter 0341 — A Date On The Post, And A Lane Nobody Goes Up

A gate on a lane past two gates had the twenty-sixth day of that month in chalk on the post, a Saturday, and the light came over the wall from the west about an hour later than it had come for a week and stopped at the bar instead of going past it, and the cold was out of the ash and in the crease of an apron a woman of about fifty had put both hands into before the seventh hour, and what was under about four pairs of hands that morning was a bar of ash in two brackets, a wooden hook over its handle, a piece of chalk, a barrow of boards against the wall, and a brass key on a tarred cord in the pocket of it.

---

She put the twenty-sixth on the post at about the seventh hour and she did not rub anything out, and the twenty-fifth was above it and the twenty-fourth above that, and about four people who live on that lane have worked out since that a keeper who writes a date on a post every morning and has never rubbed one out is doing the one thing on that road a person can do again tomorrow, and that it is also the one thing on that road that is going to run out first.

The key was in the apron pocket on its cord and it had been in the apron pocket every morning for nine years, and she did not take it out and look at it, and a key nobody looks at is a key that is only ever going to be looked at by a hand that has learned where it is.

The boards were still against the wall.

They had gone there on about the forty-first day of the Short Month counting the day they arrived, and the arrangement for taking them up was for the forty-ninth, which was a Thursday, and about four people on that lane have worked out since that a barrow of boards can stand against a wall through a whole month and through the end of that month and into the month after it without anybody in four hundred miles deciding anything about it, and that the deciding is what has not happened, and that a thing nobody decides is not the same as a thing nobody minds.

---

Nobody asked her anything.

That is the whole of that morning and it is the condition of that lane and it is not an accident, and about four people on that lane have worked out since that a woman who keeps a gate and has been asked nothing by anybody since before the water got that by refusing one person once and saying why, and that the why was hers and has never been improved on by anybody, and that the cost of the why has not been counted by anybody either, least of all by her.

The man of about thirty-one who carries things for a living came up the lane at about the eighth hour with nothing in his hands, and he went past her and he did not stop, and he did not slow down at the gate, and about four people on that lane have worked out since that a man who has carried five sheets in this flood and has not opened one of them knows that a lane with a shut gate on it is a lane where a man who stops gets asked what he has come for, and that he came up that lane with nothing in his hands so that if anybody did ask him he would be able to say nothing at all, and that he was not asked, and that he had not needed to be careful.

She did not ask him either, and that was the arrangement and both of them knew it and neither of them had ever said it.

"I am not going to be asked when those boards come off that wall."

She said it to his back and she said it at the volume he was walking away at, and he did not turn round.

"**Then nobody is going to be asked, and I have been up this lane twice this month and nobody has asked me anything, and I would like it noticed that the not-asking is not a courtesy and that it is costing somebody.**"

"You may notice it. You may not come back for it."

---

The man of about thirty-eight came up the lane at about the ninth hour with the barrow empty and the rope hooked back over the handle where he had put it, and he did not stop at the gate.

He went past her and up the road about nine hundred paces, and then he put the barrow down in the middle of the road, and then he stood with his forearms on the top of the handles and looked at nothing in particular to his left, and about four people who have been on that lane since the water have worked out since that a man who puts a barrow down in the middle of a road and looks at one side of it for a while is a man who has been told about that side of it, and that nobody on that lane has told anybody about anything, and that the two of those are the whole of what that lane knows.

He stood there a while. He did not go in. There is a row of trees at the near end of that ground and a stone at the far end of it that nobody put there, and about nine hundred paces is the distance between the gate and the row, and he looked at the row and he did not take one step off the road, and then he took his hands off the handles and picked the barrow up and went back down the lane and did not stop at the gate going out.

She watched him do all of it from the post with her hand flat on the ash, and she said nothing, and he said nothing, and about four people on that lane have worked out since that there are two people in this city who know what is in that orchard, and that one of them is the woman at the gate and the other one is the man with the barrow, and that they have not spoken about it and are not going to, and that nobody standing on that lane this morning would be able to tell you why the man stopped.

---

A room over a shop on the low ground had the twenty-sixth day of that month in chalk on its door and about nine people in it at about the fourth hour of the afternoon, and the light came in on the north side and had gone off the top of the low ground before it was across the table, and the cold was out of the glass and in the lay of a rope over a chair back, and what was under about four pairs of hands that afternoon was a deed of about forty years ago with a sheet pinned to the corner of it, a second slate, a length of new rope nobody had used, and a barrow nobody had brought in past the step.

The man of about thirty-eight came up the stair at about the fourth hour and stood at the back of it with his hands in his pockets and did not say anything for a while, and about four people in that room have worked out since that a man who has been in about nine rooms in this city in a fortnight and has not been asked a question in any of them came into this one and waited to be asked, and that the waiting is the cost of a man who has something and cannot put it on a page, and that a thing a man cannot put on a page is not a thing the room can work with.

Nobody asked him anything.

That is the rule that room arrived at by itself about a fortnight ago and the man of about thirty-eight came into it and was not touched by it, and about four people in that room have worked out since that a rule which protects a man who is in the room and does nothing at all in a room in which a man who has said something out loud is put in a column is not the same rule, and that nobody in that room can say which of the two it is for.

The man of about fifty-seven had the deed out and the sheet pinned to the corner of it out before the man with the barrow was through the door, and the man of about forty-three told him to put it away and he put it away.

"The sheets do not agree."

"The sheets have not agreed for about a hundred and forty years, and the copy in your hand is a copy of an entry nobody in this city can go and look at, and the one on the back of the stone is wrong, and I am told both of those are on a page and neither of them has been read out in a room in this city except the copy."

"The copy has been read out."

"Then the stone has not, and the stone is nine hundred paces off a shut gate and a man in this city has stood at the end of it, and the two of those are the same problem and they are not the same document."

The woman of about thirty-three who keeps that room had the bread board at the end of the sill and had not put it down, and she said the one thing she said that afternoon, and she said it to the man of about forty-three and not to the room.

"**You have had that deed out and that sheet off it in front of about nine people three times in about a fortnight, and each time you have been right about wanting it and wrong about why, and I have not said so and I am not going to be asked, and a woman who keeps a room is allowed one thing in this city and I am spending it on that.**"

Nobody improved on it, and about four people in that room have worked out since that a woman who keeps a room has used the whole of what she is allowed on a sentence about a man putting two papers on a table, and that about nine people in this city could have said it and that nobody has said it in about a fortnight.

---

"Are those boards yours."

"No."

"Whose are they."

"**They were against a wall on the forty-first day of the Short Month counting the day they went there, and they were to go up on the forty-ninth, and I was to be the one to take them.** I have not been up this lane since the thirty-ninth of that month. That is the whole of what I know about them, and I would like it said in this room that nobody has asked me one question about any of it."

The man of about fifty-seven put his hand flat on the table and left it there, and about four people in that room have worked out since that a man has said out loud that he is not going to be asked anything, and that saying that out loud is the only way anybody in this city can get a thing said without being asked for it, and that four people have worked that out in about a fortnight and about nine have not.

"You have not been up this lane since the thirty-ninth of that month."

"No."

"And you have just come up it."

"I came up it at about the ninth hour and I stopped at the boards and I am going to say the rest of it because nobody is going to ask me for it. I went up past the gate about nine hundred paces and I put the barrow down and I stood there and I did not go in, and that is all I did."

The room had gone very quiet in the way a room goes quiet when a man has been about to say the rest of a thing and has not said it, and about four people in it worked out at the same moment that the rest of it was a thing he was not going to say here, and that about nine people in this city who have been in nine rooms have each got one of those, and that this is the first one anybody in four hundred miles has heard the end of.

**I am not going to tell the room what I stood there for and the room is not going to ask me.**

The barrow was still at the bottom of the stair where he had left it and had not been brought in past the step, and about four people in that room worked out at the same moment that a man who has said out loud that he is not going to tell a room a thing has not touched the thing, and that those two took about four seconds each and neither of them is the other.

"Did anybody see you."

"**Nine households live on that lane and about four of them were out of their doors when I came past, and one of them saw me stop, and none of them said anything and I did not say anything, and that is the whole of what happened on that lane this morning.**"

"Somebody will have said something by the fourth hour."

"**Somebody will have said something by the fourth hour and it will be about the boards, because that is what I did, and if any person in this city says a different thing about that lane then they have not been on it, and I am not going to be asked about it.**"

The man of about forty-three shut the deed and did not put it under his arm.

"You have told this room that you went about nine hundred paces and stood there and did not go in."

"I have told this room a number and two verbs and I have not told it a thing, and you keep nine boxes of other people's papers and you have not asked me for the thing, and that is the correct use of a room and I want it noticed that I noticed it."

"I have not asked you for the thing and I am not going to, and there is a line in a field about nine hundred paces off a shut gate and a man in this city read a name off an index in about four seconds on a page in this room and put the book back on a shelf, and you are not the man who did that and neither am I, and there is nobody left in this room to do it."

"Then we will get on with the Monday." and that he has not said what the thing is, and that the not-saying is the most expensive thing anybody in this room has done today, and that the man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall would be the only person in four hundred miles who could put a word on it and he is not in this room and is not going to be.

---

Then the man of about fifty-seven asked the one question anybody in that room asked anybody all afternoon, and it was not about the line and it was not about the word and it was not about the deed, and it was the question a man asks in a room when he has run out of the questions he came with.

"Who is going up that lane."

The room did not have it, and about four people in that room have worked out since that a man who has bought half a holding about forty years ago asked a room in this city who is going up a lane on his own ground, and that there is no form in four hundred miles for a question about a lane, and that the answer to a question like that is a name, and that a name is a thing that goes in a column.

The man of about thirty-eight did not answer it. He was not asked it either, because the man of about fifty-seven had put it to the room and not to him, and about four people in that room have worked out since that that was the first question anybody in this city has asked in a room with a date on the door since the ninth of that month and that it went to the walls, and that the walls are a thing this city has no word for and is not going to get one.

The man of about fifty-seven put his hand flat on the table and left it there.

"I have asked four doors in a row where their own ground stops and not one of them knows, and there is a word on a slate in this city for a thing in an orchard that nobody can find, and I cannot show that slate to anybody, and now I am asking a room who is going up my lane." He took his hand off. "**Nobody is going to tell me and I am not going to make anybody tell me, and I would like it written on that door that I did not ask twice.**"

Nobody wrote it on the door.

"It is not going on that door."

"I know it is not. I have been in this room six times and I know what a room does with a thing that wants writing down, and I am asking for it because I want to see a room turn one down."

He put the deed in front of the man of about forty-three.

"Then I would like it refused in a room and not on a door, and I would like it refused by somebody who keeps a book and not by somebody who keeps boxes."

"There is no form in four hundred miles for a note on a door, and I have kept this book nine years and I have never put anything on a door and I am not starting on a Saturday."

"I know that. I asked for it to see a room turn one down, and it has, and I have been in this room six times and I have got what I came for, and it is nothing."

About four people in that room have worked out since that a man who keeps a stall is not the only person in four hundred miles who has put a word on a thing in public and then had to say out loud that he had, and that nobody in this room is going to be told about the ninth of that month until somebody decides to tell it, and that a word that comes off a slate is a different kind of thing from a word that comes off a wall, and that the man of about thirty-four knows the difference and is not in this room and is not going to be asked about it.

---

And the cost of all of it was not hers.

The woman of about fifty was at a shut gate some streets off and she had not been asked anything since the day before the Short Month ended and had not been sent anybody, and about four people in four hundred miles have worked out since that the cheapest thing anybody has done in this flood is the not-asking, and that it has been done about four hundred times, and that every one of the four hundred cost somebody else, and that the woman it was done for is the only one of the four hundred who has not been charged for it.

The room over a shop on the low ground had a word on a slate in it for a straight mark in the ground that no person in four hundred miles has stood at the end of, and the only two people in this city who know where that mark is have not told the room and are not going to and cannot be asked, and the word on the slate has sixteen days left on it, and about four people in that room have worked out since that a word on a page is a thing that points, and that this one has started pointing at about the rate a man walks, and that nobody in the room can tell which man.

The rope went back over the chair back and the man with the barrow went down the stair without being asked anything and without having said anything, and the man of about fifty-seven sat on with the deed in front of him and did not open it, and about four people in that room have worked out since that a man who has spent a fortnight refusing pages and a man who has spent a fortnight refusing to be asked have arrived at the same room from opposite ends and neither of them can use it, and that the room is the only thing in this city that has cost nobody anything and is worth nothing, and that a man is going up a lane on Saturday with a barrow and nobody in four hundred miles is going to be told.
