# Chapter 0345 — A Second Bar, And A Line Nobody Can Administer

Four streets come together at a gate and the ground under them has been worn down about two inches in about a hundred years, and the light came up the middle of the four of them on the thirtieth day of that month and stopped about nine feet short of the bar the way it always does, and the wind was up the fourth street where it always is and had not stopped, and the cold was out of all four streets at once and out of a boot that had been standing in wet since before the light, and what was under about four pairs of hands that morning was a bar against a post, a barrow with a spade lying across its handles, a length of rope hooked over a gate post, and a second bar in a bracket of its own that nobody four hundred miles off has ever been told about.

---

The near sill had gone soft on the low side again in the last week and he had had the spade out of it before anybody knew he was there, and the heap of wet ground was about nine feet off the sill and had no date on it, and about four people on that road have worked out since that a heap of wet ground nine feet off a gate is the most ordinary object in four hundred miles and the only one anybody has ever had to dig twice.

The bootful came out of his left boot at about the seventh hour while he stood with the bar in his hands, and about four people on that road have worked out since that a man who holds gates on a turn empties a boot before he says anything on a morning, and not after, and that the reason is not cleanliness and that a man standing in a wet boot is a man who has not decided to answer yet.

He put the bar back in its drop and it went in the way it goes in, and the second bar stayed where it was in a bracket of his own making at the height of his own choosing, and about four people on that road have worked out since that a gate with two bars is not a gate with a fault in it and is not a gate that has been mended, and that it is one gate and two objects, and that the two objects are the same length because he cut the second one out of the first one's pattern and that is the whole of what the two of them have in common.

---

The man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall came up the fourth street at about the eighth hour with a barrow of pears and stopped at the bar the way everybody stops at a bar.

He has a piece of chalk in his coat and does not use it, and about four people on that road have worked out since that a man who keeps a stall and keeps a chalk and does not use the chalk is a man who has been in a room in this city on Sunday where a word came off a slate in a cloth, and that a man who has watched that happen does not go home and put his own writing down, and that the not-putting is the whole of what he has done about it.

A barrow of pears on a Wednesday morning is the most ordinary thing on a road in this flood and it is the reason the two of them were standing at the same gate, and about four people on that road have worked out since that about nine men in this city push a barrow of something up this street every week and that the man of about thirty-six has never once looked at what is in one, and that on the thirtieth of that month he looked at this one for about four seconds and then looked at the second bar.

"You have two."

"I have two."

"Nobody sent for the second one."

"Nobody sent for the second one."

That is the whole of the exchange and about four people on that road have worked out since that two men in this city have now said the same pair of sentences at two different gates about the same object and that neither of them has ever seen the other before, and that a man who keeps a stall has just been told a true thing about a piece of iron by a man who puts iron in brackets.

---

Then the man of about thirty-four asked the question, and he asked it because he had been in a room on Sunday, and about four people on that road have worked out since that the question a man asks at a gate is the question he has been carrying since the last room he was in, and that this one had been in a room with a slate in it.

"How does a gate with two bars go on a roll."

The man of about thirty-six emptied the other boot before he answered, and about four people on that road have worked out since that a man who empties a boot before a hard answer is a man who has decided the answer is going to be long enough to want both feet dry for.

"The roll has a gate on it and a keeper on it and a set of days, and that is the whole of what is on it, and it has been that for longer than the four of us have been anything." He put the bar in again. "**Two bars is one gate. It goes on the roll as one gate and the second one is not on the roll and the first one is not either, and no clerk four hundred miles off is going to come up this road and count them, and that is the arrangement and I put the second one in myself on a Wednesday night because the first one had begun to lift out of its drop and a gate that does not shut is not a gate.**"

"Nobody is going to come up this road and count them."

"Nobody is going to come up this road and count them, and if they did, the first thing they would find is two bars and the second thing they would find is that a gate is a gate, and the two of those cancel, and that is the cheapest thing in four hundred miles and I am the only one in this city who has it."

---

Nobody said what it was worth.

About four people on that road have worked out since that a man who keeps a stall stood at a gate and heard about a second bar in a bracket and did not once ask what it was worth, and that the same man was in a room on Sunday where a word came off a slate in a cloth, and that he writes a line on a wall nine miles up the road in a harder piece of chalk so that nobody can rub it out, and that nobody has ever offered to take it down, and that a man standing at a gate with both of those in the same week has not worked out which of them he would rather have and about four people who have watched him not work it out have.

"I am not going to say what that is worth, and it is not because I do not know."

"**Then that is two men in this city who are not going to say what it is worth, and there is no form in four hundred miles for what a man is worth, and that is the whole of what I have come up this street to say and I have said it at a gate and not in a room, and I am not going to be thanked for it and I would like it noticed in advance that I am not going to be thanked for it.**"

Nobody thanked him.

About four people on that road have worked out since that a man who has asked in advance not to be thanked has asked for the one thing in four hundred miles that costs nothing to give away and that nobody has ever given him, and that a man of about thirty-four who writes a ninth line on a wall in a harder piece of chalk so that the word is the last thing anybody can rub out does not want to be thanked for a sentence at a gate, and that the two of those are the same man and that nobody on that road has worked out how to hold both of them at once and about four of them have stopped trying.

---

The woman of about thirty-three who keeps a room over a shop on the low ground came up the fourth street at about the ninth hour with a basket on her arm and she asked the man of about thirty-four about the wall.

Nobody on that road has asked anybody anything all morning. About four people who were watching from the four doorways have worked out since that a woman who keeps a room in this city has been in about nine of them in a fortnight and has been asked nothing in any, and that she came up this street with a basket at the ninth hour and asked a man with a barrow of pears a question about a wall, and that she is the third person in four hundred miles to be refused a thing by a man with a piece of chalk he does not use.

The ninth line is on a wall nine miles up the road in a harder piece of chalk and it is the only line anybody can read from the bottom of a yard, and about four people on that road have worked out since that a man who asked about it was asking a man who has been asked about it in kitchens and yards about four hundred days and that this is the first time he has been asked about it by anybody who had read a page that morning.

He did not answer.

He did not say whether it works, he did not say it does not work, he did not move it, he did not date it, and he did not put his hand in his coat and take the chalk out, and about four people on that road have worked out since that a man who has been asked five times in about four hundred days has now been asked a sixth time on a road in this city by a stranger, and that he has said no, and that there is no page in four hundred miles on which anybody keeps a count of the number of times he has said it, and that a city which has spent a fortnight refusing to write down what its rooms do is not going to start now with a man and a wall.

"Do not write it down," he said. "**Not because of me. Because a line with a count on it is a line somebody can be asked to produce, and the day after that there is a column, and I have been in a room this city where a man said that to a page and I am not going to improve on him and I would like it noticed that I have not improved on him.**"

Nobody improved on him, and about four people on that road have worked out since that a man who writes on walls has just told a man who keeps gates that he is not going to improve on a man in a room, and that the man in the room has said that sentence about four times this month to four different people, and that none of the four knew about the other three, and that this is the first time one of them has been in a room with another one and neither of them mentioned it.

---

The pears went up the fourth street and the bar stayed where it was and about four people on that road have worked out since that a barrow of pears that was stopped at a gate for about a quarter of an hour came out of it with nothing written on it and no name at the top of it, and that the man who pushed it had not gone up the fourth street to trade and had not said that he had, and that about nine men in this city push a barrow up that street every week and that the man of about thirty-six has never once asked one of them what is in it and never will, and that a second bar in a bracket at a gate on a road where four streets come together is the only thing in four hundred miles anybody has put up this week that cannot be taken down and cannot be written down, and that he put it in himself on a Wednesday night, and that nobody sent for it, and that nobody four hundred miles off will ever be told.
