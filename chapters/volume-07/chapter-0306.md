# Chapter 0306 — A Ditch, A Channel, And Two Pair Of Boots

A ditch on eleven miles of flats had to go somewhere before the season turned and the only somewhere was a channel that had had no water in it since the hundred and ninety-sixth day of this flood, and the light on the fifty-first day of the Short Month came up flat out of the east with no hill anywhere in front of it and lay along the ground like water off a floor, and the cold was out of the north across about eleven miles of open ground and not off a shoulder of anything, and what was under about four pairs of hands that morning was a spade, a line of willow, a bucket, a barrow, and about nine feet of wet silt.

---

A man of about sixty-three had been in that channel since the light came up, and he had a rack standing in it at the low end with a foot of its leg in the silt, and a rack that has not been worked does not come off, and there is no argument in four hundred miles that will make one, and that sentence is on a page in this city and he has never once said it out loud and about four people on those flats have worked out that he does not have to.

The silt was nine feet deep and wet to the knee and it had been settling for a hundred and ten days and a channel that takes its water from a gate and does not get any is a hole in the ground, and a hole in the ground is where everything in a flood goes, and about four people on eleven miles of flats have worked out since that the boundary question and the water question have always been the same question on those flats and that anybody who thinks there are two of them is a person who has never stood in a dry channel.

He had the willow line out and a spade and a bucket, and he dug the spadeful and put it in the bucket and went up the side on a board he had laid in himself, and he tipped it, and he came back down, and the whole of the method is about nine seconds and he had been doing it since the fifth hour.

The heap was not beside the ditch.

---

The man of about sixty-four was about nine hundred paces off with his own spade and his own heap and a bucket, and there are about nine of his on those flats and they are all older than the spades they are using, and he is a neighbour of the man of about sixty-three and he is a year younger or a year older depending on which of them you ask, and the two of them have never been in the same hole.

Four people on eleven miles of flats can tell them apart with their eyes shut, and the reason is not the age and the reason is not the face, it is that one of them has a bucket and a heap and the other one has a bucket and a line of willow laid out ahead of him at the far end of what he has done.

"Are you going to that one?"

"That one is not going anywhere. I have taken the head off it and I have got the water off the top and it is not yours."

"It is not mine and I am not asking."

"**You are not asking and I have noticed, and I would like it noticed that I am digging a ditch that is not mine, on ground that is not mine, for a house that is not on this row, and I have not been asked to and I have not been paid, and nobody on these eleven miles is going to hear me say so from me.**" He went down into his own spadeful. "Take your own water off. That is all anybody wants and it is the whole of what I have found out about it in about nine days."

---

The man of about sixty-three worked from the fifth hour to the seventh hour of the evening and got nine feet of silt out of a channel and put it in a barrow and the barrow went up the side and the silt went down at the head of the channel, and the head is not beside the ditch, and the head is the far end of the channel where the water comes in from the gate, and about four people on those flats have worked out since that a man who puts nine feet of somebody's ground back in at the head of a dry channel has not dumped anything and has decided something, and that what he has decided is where the top of that channel is, and that the top of that channel is on a household's side of about a foot of ground.

They asked him about it in the middle of the afternoon and he answered in four sentences and none of them was about the split.

**"The ditch wants the water and the water has not come, and the water comes from that gate, and the gate has been shut, and a ditch that has not got water in it before the season turns is a ditch that takes the weather in, and the weather in this flood goes to the lowest place there is, and this is the lowest place on eleven miles.** I am not saying it goes on your half. I am saying it has got to go somewhere and I have got a barrow and I am putting it at the head of a channel, and if anybody else on this row has a better place for it than the head of a channel, they can have the first bucket off me and say so out loud."

Nobody said so out loud, and about four people on those flats have worked out since that a man will offer a bucket to nine neighbours and that none of the nine will take one, and that the reason is that taking a bucket is agreeing about the head of the channel, and that a man who offers has not noticed that he has just made the head of a channel into a thing that has to be agreed on.

At about the second hour of the afternoon the light went off the flat and the cold came up out of the hole they were standing in and not off anything, and about four people on those flats have worked out since that on eleven miles of flats the only place a man can get cold in the middle of a day is out of the ground he is standing in, and that the man of about sixty-three did not get out of the hole when that happened and that the man of about sixty-four did, and that this is the whole of the difference between two men a year apart.

---

The ditch went nine feet down and about as far again as a man can throw a stone and then it stopped, and it stopped because the ground went firm, and the man of about sixty-three put the spade down on the firm ground and stood in the bottom of his own ditch and said a thing about the ground out loud to nobody in particular, and the man of about sixty-four, who was about nine hundred paces off with his own spade, heard it and did not answer it.

**"The ground on this row is soft all the way down and it would take a ditch the length of the field, and I have had both halves of this holding in my own hands and it is not the same ground.**"

He got down into it again on his knees and put the back of the spade flat against the face of the cut, which is what a man does when he is arguing with dirt.

"**It went firm at about twenty paces. It is not stone and it is not clay and it is not anything you can put a name on in a kitchen, and it is firm, and I did not put it there, and nobody told me where it would be.**"

He got out of the ditch and stood on the edge of it and looked down its length at the twenty paces of it, and about four people on eleven miles of flats have worked out since that this is the first time in about a month that anybody has said anything about the ground being different in two places on one holding, and that the man who said it was standing in a hole he had dug to take water away from a rack, and that nobody has asked him whether the difference matters and he has not said that it does.

"Do you think that is where the line is?"

"I think that is where the ground is firm and I have said that and I am not going to say it again today, and I am going to take my barrow up and tip it at the head of the channel, and if you want to ask me that again you can ask me on a day that is not the fifty-first of a month that has nine days left in it."

---

The silt went up the side in the barrow and went down at the head of the channel all afternoon, and the heap at the head of that channel was not beside the ditch and was not beside anything, and about four people on eleven miles of flats have worked out since that a man who puts nine feet of somebody else's ground in the wrong place is a man who has an opinion about where a line runs, and that the man who put it there has not said so and would not have said so if he had been asked, and that there is another person in four hundred miles with an opinion about the same line who has paid money for it, and that the two of them have not met and are not going to meet this month, and that the man of about sixty-four dug his ditch for nothing and told nobody and put his heap where his own feet were standing, which is the one place on those flats where nobody has ever put anything down, and that is the whole of what a man who is not asked does with a day.
