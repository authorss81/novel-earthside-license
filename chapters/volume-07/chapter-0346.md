# Chapter 0346 — A Line In A Register, And Nothing Under The Day

A room over a market had the thirty-first day of that month in chalk on its door, a Thursday, and about nine people in it, and the light came in over a step at the back with no name on it and reached the table before anybody came up the stair, and the cold was out of the flagstones and in the seam of a tin somebody had carried a mile in both hands, and what was under about four pairs of hands that morning was a register open at a ruled line, a pen, a piece of chalk, a sheet of paper thirty years old lying face up on the table where it had lain since the fifth of that month, and about nine paces of floor nobody was standing on.

---

The woman of about twenty-nine who keeps a public register had ruled a line at about the seventh hour and had not written anything on it, and about four people in that room have worked out since that a woman who keeps a register for a living has ruled a line and then gone and stood at a window, and that this is the ordinary condition of a person about to write the correct amount.

The form has three columns and it has had three columns for nine years, and the first of them is a day, and the second of them is a thing, and the third of them is where a person goes, and about four people in that room have worked out since that nine years of entries in this book have somebody's name in the third column, and that the third column is the only reason anybody has ever been afraid of this book.

There is a form with a notary's name at the top of it that has never been used because there was no form for a day, and there is a page with a line ruled at the top of it that has been filled in once in four hundred miles, and about four people in that room have worked out since that this morning is going to be the first time anybody in this city has entered a thing and left the third column empty on purpose, and that the person who rules the line decides the columns and that the person who has been arguing about the columns for about a fortnight has not been asked.

She came back to the table and picked up the pen and put the cap off and did not put it down, and about four people in that room have worked out since that a woman who keeps a register keeps the cap in her left hand, and that she has done it about four hundred times, and that a room of about nine people has watched her do it and has not once mentioned it.

---

Then she said what was going in it, out loud, in daylight, with a date on the door, because that is what she does.

"The thirty-first day of that month at the top. Underneath it, that a straight mark in a field may not be dug." She put the cap back on. "**There is no name. There is no word for the mark, and the word was taken off a slate in this room on Sunday and it is not coming back, and I am not going to write down what it was even though I wrote it down once. And the third column stays empty, and nobody is to ask me to fill it in, and if anybody asks me to fill it in I will leave the book open on the table and go out of the room.**"

Nobody asked her to fill it in.

About four people in that room have worked out since that a woman who keeps a public register for nine years has just told a room of about nine people that she will walk out of it rather than put a person against a line in a field, and that the woman of about fifty at a shut gate on a road some streets off refused her once for a reason that is hers, and that the two of them have never met and are not going to and that the two refusals are the same shape and that neither of them knows it.

She wrote it. It took her a moment longer than an entry usually takes, and about four people in that room have worked out since that a woman who has written out about four hundred things in nine years in about nine different hands took longer over one line with nothing in the third column than over anything she has put in that book, and that the reason is that she is leaving a space on purpose, and that a space left on purpose in a register is the only kind of space anybody in four hundred miles has ever been able to keep.

---

The man of about fifty-seven was in the room and she told him, and she told him plainly and in the order of the facts.

"You have a line you cannot have shown anybody. You have had that line for about forty years and it has not moved and nobody has measured it and nobody is going to. You have a winter you cannot get back. It went on the ninth of that month, in this room, when it was decided that nobody goes, and there is no instrument in four hundred miles that gets a winter back, and there never was one, and the only page in four hundred miles that would have shown anybody what you paid for is one you refused on the nineteenth in this room and were right to refuse." She put her hand flat on the open book. "**And as of the seventh hour this morning you have a third thing, which is that the mark may not be dug, and that is not a right and it is not yours, and it is on a page in this city with a day on it, and nobody four hundred miles off can send for it because a thing that may not be dug is a refusal and a refusal is not a return.**"

He said what it cost out loud.

"**A winter and a certainty and an afternoon, and now a line in a book that says a thing I own may not be dug, and I cannot have shown anybody where it is and I cannot show anybody that, and a man is going to be able to say that the ground is not to be touched and he is going to be able to say it about ground he has not found.**"

Nobody in that room told him he was wrong.

About four people in that room have worked out since that a man who has been in this room six times in a month and has said out loud that he is not going to be told he is wrong has now been told, in a room with a date on the door, exactly what he has, and that the telling cost the room the only thing it had left to give anybody, and that about nine people in that room have worked out since that nobody in it is wrong, and that the man of about fifty-seven is not a villain in this and has not been one, and that he is right about most of what he says, and that being right is what has cost him the season and the winter and the afternoon and that a man being right and a man being told he is right are two different rooms and this city has only got one of them.

He did not leave. He put the length of new rope nobody had used over his arm and he sat down again, and about four people in that room have worked out since that a man who has been told what he has has sat down, and that sitting down is not happiness and is not defeat and is what a man of about fifty-seven does when there is nothing else in a room he has been in six times.

---

The woman of about sixty-nine came up from the end of the lane at about the eighth hour and nobody had sent for her.

She had the tin in both hands and it was empty and she had carried it a mile, and about four people in that room have worked out since that a woman of about sixty-nine brought an empty tin to a room where a register is open on the table because she has been in this room once before and knows what happens in it, and that nobody sent for her and that a person who is not sent for is a person who came, and that the two of those are the only two kinds of coming there is.

The sheet of paper thirty years old was lying on the table where it had lain since the fifth of that month, face up, with her own hand on it and nothing at the top of it and no day on it and no name on it, and it had been on that table since the fifth of that month and had not been moved and had not been copied and had not gone out of the room.

"**I know what is on the table and I know what is in the book and I want both of those said out loud together, and then I want to go down the lane, and I have brought the tin for nothing and I am not going to leave it.**"

The register-keeper said both of them out loud. About four people in that room have worked out since that a woman who keeps a register for a living can say what a book has in it in front of a person who has brought an empty tin, and that she did it in the order of the facts and that the order was the sheet first and the book second, and that about four people in that room worked out at the time that this was not an accident and that it was the tin that put the sheet first.

The sheet is not in the book. It is not copied and it is not going to be copied and it is not leaving the room, and about four people in that room have worked out since that a woman of about sixty-nine said a reason for that in this room on the fifth of that month and it was hers, and that nobody has asked her for it since and nobody is going to, and that the condition she attached to it is hers and that nobody in four hundred miles can take a condition off a person, and that a person who has said no once in a room with a date on the door is owed the whole of that no forever, and that this is the most expensive thing anybody in four hundred miles owns and that she owns it for nothing.

She picked the tin up and put it in her apron and did not look at the sheet again, and about four people in that room have worked out since that a woman of about sixty-nine did not look at a page of her own hand for the second time since the fifth of that month and that the not-looking is the whole of what she has got out of this room, and that a woman in a room who has been in nine of them and asked nothing has just watched a person take a thing back without taking it.

---

Adrian read the entry.

He read it from the top down, both of the columns that had anything in them, and then he read the third one, which was empty, and he put a finger about a finger's width from the edge of it and did not touch it, and about four people in that room have worked out since that a man who has been in about two hundred rooms in this city and has been asked nothing in about nine of them read an entry this morning in a book and read the empty column as well, and that he has been reading empty columns in this city for about a fortnight, and that nobody in this room has ever asked him why and that nobody is going to.

Then the woman of about sixty-nine asked the thing, and she asked it to the room and not to anybody, and about four people in that room have worked out since that it is the fourth time in about a month that somebody in this city has asked a question out loud that nobody is going to answer and that every one of the four has been asked by a person who had already decided.

"What is a person for, on a page, when the only thing anybody needs them for is a journey."

Nobody answered it.

About four people in that room have worked out since that a room of about nine people has just heard a woman of about sixty-nine ask what a person is for on a page and that four of them have an answer and that four of them have said nothing, and that the answer four of them have is a column, and that a woman who keeps that book has three columns and has just left the third one empty on purpose, and that the two facts have been in the same room since about the seventh hour and have not met.

Nobody was asked whether any of it would go. The man of about fifty-seven was not asked. The man of about forty-three was not asked, and the book was shut at about the fourth hour of the afternoon and did not go out of the building, and the sheet of paper thirty years old was still on the table, and about four people in that room have worked out since that this is the fourth room in a month where nobody has been asked anything and that the one thing every one of those four rooms has on a page is a thing that nobody four hundred miles off can ask to have produced, and that a refusal is not a return, and that nobody in that room said it twice.
