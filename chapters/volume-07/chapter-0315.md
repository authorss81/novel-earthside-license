# Chapter 0315 — A Date On A Plain Slate, Read Out Loud

A room over a market had a date in chalk on its door on the sixtieth day of the Short Month, a Monday, and the sixtieth was the last day of that month and the room did not know how to say so without sounding like a sentence about something, and about nine people were in it, and the light came in over a step at the back with no name on it and did not reach the far wall, and what was under about two pairs of hands that morning was a plain slate, a lump of chalk, a cloth, and about nine sheets of paper with nothing at the top of any of them.

---

She had been copying since before the light and she was the only person in that room who was on no roll of anything and had never been asked a question in it, and about four people in that room have worked out since that a girl of seventeen who has copied every asking anybody has written in this flood and has signed none of them is the only person in this city who can hold the whole of it and is the only one nobody can pay.

She wrote her name at the top of the slate before she wrote anything else on it, in the ordinary way, the way a person writes their name on a thing they have been handed.

"**Is that necessary.**" The woman of about twenty-nine who keeps a public register did not move her hand away from the second slate. "**You are the only thing in this room with your name at the top of it, and I would like to know whether you have put it there because of the form or because of the habit.**"

"**Because of the habit.** I have been on about nine boards since I was fourteen and I have never once not done it, and I know that is a stupid reason and I have never found a better one." She dusted the slate with the cloth and left the name on it. "**And it is my slate, not yours, and you have never once asked me for it, and that is the only reason it is still mine.**"

The woman of about twenty-nine looked at the name while the light was still on the sill behind her, and about four people in that room have worked out since that a keeper of a public register who has not put a name at the top of a sheet for four years looked at a name at the top of a slate and did not ask for it to be taken off, and that the not-asking was a decision and she made it while looking at it and she has never once said so out loud to anybody.

---

The last asking went onto the slate in nine lines and it was the asking that had come back with a question on the back of it out of a kitchen four hundred paces off, and the girl read it out in the local language because that is what a sheet gets read for, and then she wiped the slate and copied it again, because a thing that is going to be read out in a room has to be read off a copy and not off the paper it came on, and she has never once explained that to anybody and nobody has ever asked her.

Then the woman of about twenty-nine wrote the sixtieth on the second slate in chalk and put nothing under it, and the room did what it does, and the light came off the step at the back and went along the floor and stopped about a foot short of the second slate.

**"Read that out."**

The girl looked at the second slate and then at the name at the top of her own slate and then at the second slate again, and about four people in that room have worked out since that a girl of seventeen who is not asked anything all day looked at a slate for the length of four words deciding whether reading it counted as being asked, and decided that it did not, because reading is not answering and she has been on the wrong side of that line in about nine kitchens.

"The sixtieth day of the Short Month," she said. "And that is the last one of those, and there is a Monday on the end of it and the next one is a Tuesday."

"**That is right.**" The register-keeper put the chalk down on the table and did not pick it up again. "**Say the second part again and slower. I want the whole room to have it and not just the two of us.**"

"The next one is a Tuesday."

Nobody explained it to her and nobody told her what was on the day after the sixtieth of the Short Month, and about four people in that room have worked out since that the second part of what a girl of seventeen said out loud in a room in daylight with a date on the door was the whole of what any of them knew, and that it was the first time in about a month that anybody in this city had said a weekday out loud in a room where a page was being kept, and that the reason it went in a mouth is that a girl of seventeen was in the room and a girl of seventeen asks.

---

The man of about forty-three was at the left of the table with a box under his arm and a deed in it and a sheet pinned to the corner of the deed, and he was the only person in that room who could have said something about a name at the top of a page, because he has wanted for about four of eleven years to have somebody ask him whether one word in one of his nine boxes is true and because he has read a hundred-and-forty-year-old hand and knows what the top of a document is for.

He said nothing at all, and about four people in that room have worked out since that he has been asked to be asked for eleven years and has never been, and that a room in which a man declines to use the one thing he has is not a room in which anything has been lost, and that the girl of seventeen went on copying with her name at the top of the slate and did not find out any of it and will not find out any of it, and that being on no roll of anything is not a qualification and nothing in this month has turned it into one.

Then she put the cloth over the slate and squared it on the table and looked at the woman of about twenty-nine and asked the only question she asked in that room, and she asked it in the plainest words anybody had used all day.

"**What am I to copy for tomorrow.**"

The room carried on. The woman of about twenty-nine picked the chalk up and put it in her pocket and said nothing, and about four people in that room have worked out since that she is the only person in four hundred miles who could have answered that question on the fifty-ninth and did not, and that she did not say no and did not say she would find out, and that the girl of seventeen will be in this room on the first day of whatever comes next and will put her name at the top of a clean slate and copy nothing, and that nobody will have told her why, and that she is on no roll of anything and there is no address at which a sheet could be put in front of her, and that this has not changed in about a month and is not going to.
