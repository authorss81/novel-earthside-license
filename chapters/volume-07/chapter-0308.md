# Chapter 0308 — Four Hundred And Forty, And The Only Page It Is On

A man of about thirty-eight kept a tally on eleven miles of flats and had put a number in a return four times a year since the winter before the water, and on the fifty-third day of the Short Month, a Monday, he was on the boards of a salt wharf in this city with the tally-board on his knee, and the light was on the water and not on a wall and came up off the river in one long sheet and did not move all morning, and the cold was out of the river and there is no hill within about four miles of that wharf, and what was under about four pairs of hands that day was a tally-board, a knife, a barrow, and about four hundred and forty.

---

The number was about four hundred and forty, and it was not a number anybody could check, and the woman of about thirty-four who kept the stall by that wharf had said it out loud in daylight in a market in this city a month ago to about nine people, and had been saying it out loud at that wharf ever since in about the same words, and it was on no page in this city except the one he was holding, and about four people on those boards have worked out since that a number said out loud on a wharf is a thing a wharf knows and a market knows and a form does not.

The river came up over the wall at about the eighth hour the way it does and went back again a foot from where it had been, and the boards on that wharf are the only flat thing for about four streets.

A man who keeps a tally for a living spends his whole day on a floor that is never still, and that is the reason his figure is in his head and not on a wall.

He cut the salt off a barrow and the woman of about thirty-four put it on the stall with her hands while he talked, and neither of them stopped working.

Two people who will not stop working while they argue about a number have decided the argument is not worth stopping for, and one of them has been getting four parts in five of what she was getting a month ago and the other one has not said so.

"You are the only person in four hundred miles who has ever written that number down."

"**I have put it in a return four times a year since the winter before the water and nobody has ever answered it and nobody has ever come, and I have never known what it is for and nobody has ever asked me, and I have had it in my head for nine years.**"

A gull came down the middle of the boards, took about as long as it takes to fill a pail over the tally, and left, and the man of about thirty-eight did not look up, and about four people on that wharf have worked out since that a man who does not look up when a bird comes over his figures has been doing this for a very long time and has a way of being uninterrupted that other trades do not have.

"Nine years of a number you did not count."

"**Nine years of a number I was given. A man gave it to me and the man is dead or gone and he did not count it either, and the only two ways a man gets a number he did not count are that somebody counted it for him or that somebody told him it, and both of those are about nine years older than me holding it.**"

---

The woman of about thirty-four said the number out loud on the boards at about the eighth hour, in the same words, to nobody, because that is what she does, and about four people on that wharf have worked out since that a woman who says a number out loud every morning is a woman who has decided that if the number is only ever said once then the day it stops being said is the day it is gone, and that nobody on those boards has ever asked her to stop, and that she has been doing it every morning of the spring since the day she said it in a market in this city, and nobody has said thank you and she would not want them to.

"You have added to it."

"**I have added to it. Four times a year for nine years, in my own head, without being asked to, and the figure I send is not the figure I have, and the figure I have has never been on a page in four hundred miles and never will be.**"

The tide came up and stopped and went back about an inch while he thought about it, and the woman of about thirty-four put the salt on the stall in three heaps instead of one, which is what a person does with their hands when a thing has been said that they did not know.

"Why do you send the other one."

"**Because the other one is the one somebody gave me. In about nine years the number has gone up and the number I send has not, and if the two were ever read against each other a man four hundred miles away would be able to work out that about four hundred and forty people do not exist, and some of them have asked me for salt.**" He put the knife down. "**And I have been quietly wrong for nine years and I have known it every day, and the reason I have not corrected it is that a correction is a page, and the return has a heading and four places and no name on it anywhere, and I got the number from a man with no name on him.**"

---

The woman of about twenty-nine came down the boards at about the second hour of the afternoon and asked him one question, and he had not been asked anything by anybody in nine years and he had not been asked anything by a person who kept a register in about nine days, and about four people on that wharf have worked out since that this is the only question anybody has ever asked him and it is not a question about the number.

A man came up the boards with a handcart of nothing and turned round and went back down, and a woman with a basket stood at the end of them for about as long as it takes to fill a pail and then went to the other end, and nobody spoke to anybody.

A wharf is the only place in this city where a conversation of this length can happen in the open air without either party being watched by a person who has an opinion about it.

"What is the return for."

"**That is a question and not a request and I am going to treat it as a question, and I am not going to be able to answer it, and I have known that for nine years.**" He turned the tally-board over and did not give it to anybody. "**I am going to say the only thing I have and then I am going to go back to the tally.**"

She answered him, in her own words, without a slate and without a date, standing on the boards with the light on the water behind her. The return has no name on it. The return has a heading on it. The heading has four places under it. And there is no place on it anywhere for the person the four places are about. She said it once and she did not improve on it, and she said afterwards that she had been keeping a public register for nine years and had never been able to make those four places mean anything on a page, and that the four places are for gates, and that there are about four hundred and forty people on eleven miles of flats and none of them is a gate.

Then she said the other thing, and about four people on that wharf have worked out since that she said it in a voice she had planned.

"**A schedule that nobody in four hundred miles can fill in will be filled in from the returns, and it will not be anybody's doing. The case on that table over a market is the only correct paper this city has, it has four places in it, three of them are empty, and correct is the only thing anybody has ever been able to fill a place in with.** And a schedule filled in out of the returns goes out correct, and it is about the wrong people, and it will be filed."

Nobody on that wharf said anything about that for about as long as it takes to fill a pail, and about four people on those boards have worked out since that this is the first time in about a month that anybody in this city has said out loud what a schedule would get filled in with if it were ever filled in, and that the person who said it was a woman who keeps a register, and that the reason it took a man with a tally-board to bring it out is that the returns are the only paper in this flood that anybody has ever obeyed.

---

"I am not relieved."

"You are not going to be. I did not come down here to relieve you and there is nothing in it that could."

"**I have been the only person who ever wrote that number down for nine years, and this morning is the first time it has been a thing that anybody knew, and I would like to know what it was for, and I am not going to be told.**" He picked the knife up and looked at the barrow and not at her. "**I am going back to the tally on the same day, and the tally has a figure on it that is not the figure I send, and I am going to look at it for a while, and I am not going to rub it out.**"

He said that about four paces off and the boards were loud under him, and about four people on that wharf have worked out since that the sentence which frightens a room is not the one about the number and is the one about the cloth.

The woman of about thirty-four at the stall said the number out loud again at about the seventh hour to nobody at all while the two of them stood at the end of the boards, and about four people on that wharf have worked out since that she has never once said it to a person, and that a number said out loud every morning to nobody is a number a person has decided does not belong to anybody in particular, including the people in it.

"You are not going to tell the four hundred and forty."

"**I am not going to tell four hundred and forty people on eleven miles of flats that a man in this city has been wrong about them for nine years, and I would like it entered in whatever room there is for it that I said no to that as well.**"

The knife went into the salt again and the woman of about thirty-four went on saying the number out loud at the head of the stall every time she came round, and the light on the water was gone by about the sixth hour and the cold came off the river and up through the boards into about four pairs of feet, and about four people on those boards have worked out since that a man who has found out he has been wrong for nine years and a woman who says a number out loud every morning are in the same position, and that neither of them has anybody to hand it to.

He said that in a mouth, and about four people on that wharf have worked out since that the man who keeps the only page that has ever mentioned four hundred and forty people in four hundred miles said no to being relieved and no to telling them in about nine minutes, and that both of those refusals cost him something and neither of them was about himself, and that the woman of about thirty-four at the stall said the number out loud again at about the fourth hour with the same words, and that about nine of the flats are on a rota with no readable name on it and that nobody went there that day and that nobody is going to go this month, and that a number on a wharf and a number in a room are the same number and that only one of them has a person in it and it is not the person the number is about.
