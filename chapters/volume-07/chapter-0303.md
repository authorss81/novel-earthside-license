# Chapter 0303 — The Deed, And A Sheet Attached To It

A man of about forty-three who kept papers for people who could not keep them had a deed in a box under his bed and a sheet pinned to the corner of it, and on the forty-eighth day of the Short Month, a Wednesday, he took the pin out, and the light came in on the north side of two rooms and a stair and went off the low ground by about the second hour of the afternoon, and the cold was in the shutters and out of the wind, and what was under about four pairs of hands that morning was a pin about four inches long, a deed of about forty years ago, and a sheet with a clerk's hand on it that had been copied out of a register in a town four hundred miles inland a hundred and forty years before the deed was written.

---

The pin was older than the pin in any other box he kept and he had never taken the sheet off the deed, and he said, standing on a ladder with the box on his knees, that he had kept it on because the two of them were bought together and you do not unpick a thing you have been keeping for eleven years without a reason, and about four people who have worked with him have said since that a man who keeps other people's papers for a living keeps them the way they arrive, which is not always the way they should be kept, and that this is not carelessness and it is not respect, it is that he has never once been told which of the two it is.

He carried the box down a stair in both arms and did not put it on the arm that the frame wanted it on, and he carried it across a room over a market and put it on a table and took the deed out first and then the sheet, and neither of them was folded and neither of them was rolled, and about nine people in that room have worked out since that a man who takes forty-year-old paper out of a box flat and puts it on a table flat has decided that the paper is the more important of the two things in the room and has not said so.

The date on the door was in chalk and it was the forty-eighth, and the room had about nine people in it and the light was going off the low ground outside by about the second hour and the man of about forty-three put the sheet down on the left of the deed so that the two of them were about a hand's width apart and nobody was going to be able to read one of them without the other being in the corner of their eye.

"I read what is on them and not what I think is on them, and if I stop and cannot read a word I will say that I cannot read the word and we will go on past it, and there are two of them in about nine words and I have never once been able to read either."

"Read them."

---

The deed was four pages and it was about a purchase, and the man of about forty-three read all of it out in the local language without stopping once, and the third page is the one that everybody in that room can remember and it is the page with the boundary of what was bought on it, and it goes from a gate to a hedge to a stone to a ditch and it names the ditch, and it is in the words of a house four hundred miles inland, and it does not say where the line runs so much as it says what a line goes past, which is a different sentence and everybody in that room noticed it and about four of them did not know they had noticed it.

Then he turned his head about a quarter of a circle and read the sheet.

The sheet was a copy. It had a clerk's hand on it that was not the hand of anybody in that room, and it was the copy of an entry in a public register in a town four hundred miles inland, and the entry had been made a hundred and forty years before the deed was written, and the copy had been made about forty years ago by a clerk who was not the clerk who wrote the original, and nobody in that room has ever seen the page it was copied from and nobody in that room can go and see it.

It named nineteen stones. That is what it is on the page for. Nineteen stones, in a list, with what each of them stood next to, and a direction for each of them taken from something on the ground at the time the entry was made, and the direction is a direction from the gate, and the gate is the same gate in both of them.

**"Eight of the nineteen are different."**

"Different how."

"**Different where they are, and different in what order they come in, and they are further down the road than the deed has them, and the deed does not have them at all.** The deed has nine stones and a ditch and a hedge. This has nineteen. This is the older one by a hundred years and the older one is the one with the stones in it and I cannot tell you why the newer one has fewer, and I am not going to stand in this room and guess at it." He put his finger on the list and did not move it. "Every figure on both of these is right. That is not me saying so to be fair to a piece of paper. Both of them were made by people who were being careful about the thing they were making, and neither of them was made to say where the line runs."

---

Nobody in that room touched either piece of paper and about nine people in that room noticed that the man of about forty-three had not once said that they should not, and that a man who does not tell nine people not to touch a deed has decided that nobody in a room is going to be prevented from doing anything by a sentence, and that this is a different thing from guarding and it is not better and it is not worse.

At about the third hour a man who had come in late and had been standing at the back of the room with his hands behind him came forward and read the clerk's hand over the man of about forty-three's shoulder, and he read it twice.

He was not a clerk and had never been a clerk, and he read a hand the way a man reads a barrow, which is by the way the weight of it falls.

He was named once, in a mouth, by a woman at the side of the table who had been keeping count of what was being read out, and she called him Adrian and nothing else, and nobody in that room asked him one question about the boundary, and about four people in that room have worked out since that this is now the second time in about nine days that a man has stood in a room where a page was read and was not asked what he thought of it, and that nobody in that room had decided not to ask him and that is a different thing and it is the thing that happens when a room is working on a paper and a person is not on the paper.

The man of about forty-three felt him come up and did not move the sheet and did not turn his head, and after a while he said, without turning round, that the clerk had used the word *ditch* in two places and had crossed one of them out, and that the crossing out is a hundred and forty years old and the ink is different from the ink either side of it, and that he had never been able to work out whether the clerk had crossed it out before he sealed the book or after, and that either way it is in the copy, because whoever made the copy copied what was in front of him including the crossing out, and that this is the most honest thing on either piece of paper.

---

A woman of about thirty-three in that room had been listening to the list of nineteen stones being read out and had counted them on her fingers, and she asked one question and it was not about the deed and it was not about the split in the ground, and about four people in that room have worked out since that it is the first question anybody has asked about that list in the whole time anybody has had it.

"The stones are in a different order. The deed has the nine in a different order from the nineteen, and the eight that are not in the deed at all are in the list in between the ones that are, not at the end. That is not a person adding four stones to a list of fifteen at the end because they thought of some more. That is a list that was kept in a different order by somebody who knew what they were doing and expected to be asked about the order."

"Yes."

"Whose order."

"I do not know." He took his hand off the list. "**I have had that box eleven years and nobody has ever asked me whether one word in it is true, and I have wanted somebody to ask me that for about four of the eleven.**" He did not move the sheet. "**And I cannot tell you whose order it is. A hundred and forty years ago a clerk in a town four hundred miles off copied down a list in the order it was in the book in front of him, and I have never seen the book.**"

---

The sheet has eight names at the foot of it, below the last line of the list, in the same hand and a smaller one, and the man of about forty-three came to them at about the sixth hour of the evening and stopped about a finger's width above them, and about nine people in that room have worked out since that he stopped in the same place both times, on the first reading and on the second, and that a man who stops in the same place twice has decided something.

"That is the end of the entry and the eight names are under it."

"Read them."

"**I am not going to read them. I want it said in this room, with a date on the door, that I was asked to read them and I did not, and that it is not because of the law.**" He took his hand off the paper. "**It is because I have never been asked one question about whether that paper is true, and a man who has not been asked whether a paper is true is not in any position in four hundred miles to put eight names out loud on a table.**"

Nobody argued with him about it and about four people in that room have worked out since that the reason nobody argued is that everybody in the room had worked out inside about nine seconds that the two things he had said were the same thing said twice, and that a man who keeps other people's papers for eleven years and has never been asked whether they are true has exactly one thing that nobody else in that room has got, and it is the one he has just used, and it is not knowing and saying so.

The names stayed under the last line. The man of about forty-three read everything else on the sheet out loud, twice, and did not touch those eight words with his finger, and the sheet went back on the deed and the pin went back through the corner of both of them, and neither of them was folded.

---

At about the seventh hour a woman of about thirty-three said the thing that the room had been standing in for two days without knowing it, and she said it to the table and not to the man of about forty-three.

She said it to the table because a person who has just decided not to read eight names does not want to be handed a sentence about the sheet by the person who decided that.

"Both of them were right. The deed was right and the nineteen stones were right, and the only thing that has happened in this room since that box came down that stair is that two people decided one of them was a mistake because it did not agree with the other one, and the deed was not made to agree with anything and neither were the nineteen stones."

"That does not tell anybody where the line is."

"**No. And that is the first thing said in this room since Monday that is worth having, because a thing that is right and a thing that is right about a different thing is the whole of what is in that box.**" She put her hand flat on the edge of the two pieces of paper without touching either of them, which took some doing. "**This room has been treating one of them as an error since it walked in, and an error can be corrected, and a thing that was made to do a job four hundred miles ago cannot be corrected by anybody in this city.**"

The room did not settle the boundary and the room did not try to. The man of about forty-three took the box up in both arms and did not put it on the arm the frame wanted it on, and the pin through the corner of the deed and the sheet had been in there longer than the deed, and about four people in four hundred miles have worked out since that a boundary in this world is settled by somebody walking it or by nobody, and that this room has just been handed a hundred-and-forty-year-old account of a walking by a clerk who was not there, and that the difference between those two things is four hundred miles and nobody's life, and that both of the papers on that table were true and about four people who have read them would like it entered that they said so in a room in daylight with a date on the door.
