# Chapter 0338 — A Second Bar, And A Boy With Two Pails

Four streets come together at a gate where the ground has been worn down about two inches in about a hundred years of people going over it, and the light came up the middle of the four of them at about the seventh hour on the twenty-third day of that month, a Wednesday, and stopped about nine feet short of the gate the way it always does, and the wind was up the fourth street where it always is and had not stopped, and the cold was out of all four streets at once and out of the wet of a yoke and into the back of a man's hand that had been in a bootful of water since before the light, and what was under about four pairs of hands that morning was a bar against a post, a barrow with a spade lying across its handles, a wooden yoke with a strap and two pails, and a second bar that had been cut at the near end in the dark by somebody who had a light somewhere else in the yard.

---

The near sill had gone soft on the low side and the bar had begun to lift out of its drop, and that is the whole of the job and about four people on that road have worked out since that a man who holds gates on a turn does not get a new bar, he gets the same one back in the same drop, and that a drop that will not take the bar is a hole that is not a hole yet and a week later it is a gate that does not shut, and that nobody four hundred miles off will ever be told about it because there is no form in four hundred miles for a drop that has gone soft.

He had the spade out of the low side before the light and about four people on that road have worked out since that a man who has been at a gate since before the light has the ground out of it before anybody knows he is there, and that the barrow has been used twice already and the stuff is in a heap about nine feet off the sill, and that a heap of wet ground nine feet off a gate is the most ordinary object in four hundred miles and the only one that has a date on it, and the date is that a man dug it and no page anywhere says so.

The wind came up the fourth street and went through the gap where the four of them meet and did not stop, and about four people on that road have worked out since that a gate is the only place in this flood where wind is a thing that has to be worked around rather than out of, and that a man digging a sill in it has to keep the spade out of his own eyes and not out of the wind, and that the wind on that road has been going through the same gap since long before the water and has never once been written down.

---

The boy of about eleven came through at the seventh hour with the yoke up on his shoulder and two pails swinging off it, and he did not stop, and about four people on that road have worked out since that a boy who does not stop at a gate is a boy who has decided the gate is not his, and that the man at the gate has decided the same about the boy, and that between the two of them a gate on a road where four streets come together is the only place in four hundred miles where two people are obliged to stand still and neither of them is.

The pails went through and came back at the fourth hour with the ground colour of the four streets in them, and about four people on that road have worked out since that a boy who carries water has a better knowledge of which of the four streets is running than any man in this city has, and that a gatekeeper who is not asking the boy anything has been getting the whole of the water news of four streets out of not saying anything for about nineteen days, and that neither of the two of them has ever said so and about four people watching from a heap of wet ground have worked it out on their own.

"The twenty-third day of the Bare Month."

He said it to the boy's back, the way he has said it about nineteen times, and about four people on that road have worked out since that a man who holds gates on a turn began saying the day out loud at this gate on the fourth day of that month and has said it every day since, and that he said the first one to a child carrying water because the child was the only person on that road who could not be asked to keep a page about it.

The boy put the yoke up on his shoulder inside the gate and turned round.

"**The twenty-third day of the Bare Month. And my mother has not had one of them since before the water and she does it out loud at the door, and it is four doors now, and the man with the handcart heard her do it and he does it at the top of his own road and he has not told her that he does it.**"

Nobody said anything and about four people on that road have worked out since that a boy of about eleven has been carrying a piece of news out of a gate every day for about nineteen days and has this morning told the man at the gate something the man did not know and that the man is not going to thank him for, and that a man with a handcart and a length of rope is somewhere in this city saying a date out loud at the top of a road for exactly the reason a woman at a door says one, and that the two of them are four hundred yards apart and neither of them knows what the other is doing.

"It is right. I am not going to say that twice."

He set the spade down and turned the bar over and looked at the drop.

"**Say it at the doors, not at the gate. I have been saying it at a gate for about nineteen days because a gate is a thing you are stood at and a door is a thing you go to, and it took me nineteen days and a boy with two pails to work out which of the two is the one that travels.**"

---

He put the bar back in and it dropped, and then he took the second bar out of the yard and set it in the bracket on the far side, and about four people on that road have worked out since that a gate on a road where four streets come together has had one bar on it since about a hundred years ago and that a second bar is not a repair and is a doubling, and that a man who has held gates on a turn for about nineteen years has added one in about four minutes on a Wednesday because the fourth street has begun to run across the worn ground where it used to stop, and that this is a decision and not a job and nobody asked him for it and nobody four hundred miles off can be shown has heard of it.

The boy came back at the fourth hour and the man said the day a second time and the boy said it back a second time, and about four people on that road have worked out since that this is the nineteenth time and that a thing said nineteen times at a gate is no longer a habit and is a trade, and that a man who has added a second bar to a gate nobody ordered has, in about four minutes this afternoon, done the one thing in this flood that nobody in four hundred miles will be able to ask him about, and that about four of the nine people who have watched a gate in this city would be able to tell you about the second bar and about four of them would not.

Then a woman of about twenty-eight came over the worn ground and stopped at it, and about four people on that road have worked out since that a person who stands still at a gate in this flood has come to it to be asked something, and that the man of about thirty-six put his bootful out at the near end of the lane before she was all the way across, and that neither of them said one word to the other, and that they stood there with two bars between them and nothing at all in it, and that she went through.

"You have added a bar."

"I have added a bar."

"Nobody is going to ask you why and I would like it noticed that I noticed and that I am not going to ask."

She was about nine paces into the fourth street before she said the rest of it, and she said it over her shoulder, which is a thing about four people in that gate have worked out since is a way of saying something to a man you are not going to turn round for.

"**And I have been to about nine doors this week and I have not written a name at the top of anything, and I have now said out loud in a gate that I did not, and that is the first time I have said it anywhere and you are a gate and not a room, which is the reason I said it here.**"

Nobody improved on that, and about four people in that gate have worked out since that a woman who keeps a book in this city has said the thing out loud at a gate and not in the room where the book is, and that a man holding two bars is the only party in four hundred miles a person can say that to and not have it travel, and that this is the second time in about a week that a thing has been said at a gate rather than in a room, and that both of the times were about a name at the top of a sheet and neither of them was in a room with a date on the door.

---

The second bar went in the bracket and the light came back up the four streets one at a time, and about four people on that road have worked out since that this gate is a bar and a keeper and a set of days and that is the whole of what anybody in four hundred miles can say about it, and that there is a different word for that man on a page four hundred miles off and a column he has never been asked to fill in, and that the two words have never been in one room in four hundred miles and are not going to be, and that nobody on this road has ever been asked what the difference between them is worth, and that the man at the gate has said out loud that he is not going to define either of them and would rather the oak had the other one.

He put his hand on the new bar and did not lift it.

"**Nineteen years and the whole of it is that, and there are two bars on it now, and neither of them is on a wall, and about four hundred people in this city can tell you what I did this afternoon and not one of them will be able to tell you who I am.**"

The pails went out at the fourth hour and the boy did not stop, and the second bar stayed where he had put it, and about four people on that road have worked out since that a man who holds a gate on a turn has doubled a gate that nobody sent for and said the day out loud to a child for the nineteenth time and told a woman at a gate that he was not going to be asked why, and that the whole of it is a gate with two bars and a boy who says the date and a heap of wet ground nine feet off a sill, and that it is on no page in four hundred miles and cannot be sent for, and that a woman at a shut gate on another road writes a date on a post every morning with nothing rubbed out and has been asked nothing by anybody in nine years, and that he has not been asked anything by anybody either, and that the two of them have never met and neither of them knows the other's trade.
