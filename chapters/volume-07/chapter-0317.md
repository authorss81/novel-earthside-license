# Chapter 0317 — Two Instruments, And A Sheet With A Notary's Name On It

A room over a market had a date on its door in a word that was a day old and a slate in a woman's hand had the same date on it, and about nine people were in the room on the second day of that month, a Wednesday, and the light came in on the north side and did not go off the low ground until about the second hour of the afternoon, and what was under about four pairs of hands that morning was a register open at a row with nothing in it, a second slate, a sheet with a notary's name at the top of it and five lines under that, and a waxed slate in a case.

---

The sheet had been on the table since about the seventh hour and about nine people had looked at the top of it and about four of them had read the five lines and about four of them had not, and nobody had written anything on it, and about four people in that room have worked out since that a form which has been in a room since the light came up and has nothing on it is the most alarming object anybody has brought into that room in about a month, and that it is alarming because it is the first thing in the room that anybody in four hundred miles could send for.

A woman of about thirty-three who had a question about the order of nineteen stones and had carried it about since the forty-eighth of that month put her hand flat on the table about a foot from it and asked it.

"**There is a name at the top of that and five lines and there is nowhere on it to say what a day was for.**"

"There is no column for it."

"**Then it is not a form for doing anything, it is a form for having a day in, and that is a different thing and nobody in this room has said which one it is.**" She did not touch it. The other five are not like that. She does not know what they were for either, but they were not empty, and this one is a blank page with a name on it.

"**I would like a person to tell me the name is worth it, because the answer to a question I have been carrying for about nine days is on a piece of paper in a town four hundred miles off, and if a form is only a way of having a day in, then nothing anybody writes in this room is a form of anything.**" She took her hand off the table. "**And I have been wrong about this room since the forty-eighth of that month and I would like to be told.**"

---

The man of about forty-three read the five lines out loud twice, both times from the top, and about four people in that room have worked out since that he read them the second time more slowly than the first and that a man who reads a document more slowly the second time has found something in it that is not a mistake.

Five lines and a name and no figure on it, and that is not an omission. A notary of twenty-six years did not leave a number off that by accident, and a man who has spent eleven years with about nine boxes of other people's papers knows what a thing looks like when somebody has decided where a number goes. There is a space under the last line about the width of two fingers and there is nothing in it, and a space is where a number goes when a person has decided not to write one, and he has found four of those in nine boxes and two of them were a hundred and forty years old.

"Two of them in a hundred and forty years."

"**Two in nine boxes. I am not going to make a law of it and I am not going to improve on it either.**"

---

A man who had come in late and had been standing at the back of the room read the five lines over the man of about forty-three's shoulder, and about four people in that room have worked out since that a man who is not a clerk read a notary's hand about as fast as a clerk and stopped at the same place the clerk would have stopped at, and that the woman of about twenty-nine who keeps a public register said Adrian and then said nothing else about him, and that nobody in that room asked him one question about the boundary or about the sheet.

"**That sheet is a page, and I have never seen a notary's five lines before in my life, and I am not going to pretend I read it the way you read it.**" He had his hands in his coat. "I have stood in about nine rooms this month and watched nine things go onto nine tables, and every one of them went onto a table the same morning, and none of them knew in advance what it was for, and about four of the nine were not asked afterwards."

The man of about fifty-seven, who had a length of new rope he had not used over his arm and had not put down since Saturday, made a sound with his mouth that was not a word.

"**Say it again and say it as a sentence,"** the woman of about twenty-nine said, and about four people in that room have worked out since that she asked a man with money in his hand to say a thing she had not asked anybody else to repeat, and that the reason was that she had been in a market on a Sunday and had not asked one question all day and has not made a habit of it.

---

He said it, and it took him four attempts and about four minutes and he did not improve it between them, and about four people in that room have worked out since that a man who has offered a room four men at a rate he named out loud nine days ago and been answered at length and then not answered at all has spent the week learning that a room is not going to help him, and has arrived at the one thing that would.

A page they can ask to have produced. That is the only kind there has ever been that settled anything in his life. He has been in a house four hundred miles off twice in forty years, and both times a man brought out a book with a thing written in it, and both times that was the end of it, and neither time did the book say where the line ran, and both times the man with the book was right and he went home with nothing.

"**And I know what that sounds like in this room, and I am not going to say it a fourth time, because four people have been saying the short shape of it to each other in about nine kitchens this week, and I have been one of the four.**"

"**That is a page that has got a person in it, and a page that has got a person in it is the thing this room has been arguing about for a month, and you have just described it as the only kind worth having, and you are in this room and I am in this room and about four of us have been saying the other thing all month.**" The woman of about twenty-nine put her hand flat on the second slate. "**I am not going to argue with you. I am going to say the other half out loud, because you have said yours out loud in daylight with a date on the door and that is the only standard available.**"

---

She said the other half and it took her about four sentences, and about four people in that room have worked out since that the woman who keeps a public register has not said a sentence in a room this month that she had not already said at a shut gate to a wall, and that this was one of them, and that she knew it while she was saying it.

She has kept a register in this city for nine years and has put a name at the top of nothing for four of those years, and the reason she did it is that a page with a name at the top of it is a page a clerk can send for, and she has been keeping a thing in this city that nobody in four hundred miles can ask to have produced. And as of yesterday morning this city has two instruments instead of one, and a form with a notary's name on it, and a register with nine years in it, and the one nobody can send for is worth nothing at all without days to go in it, and the days ran out on Monday, and yesterday she made an end of that by standing in a room and asking a woman to write her name on a piece of paper.

Nobody in that room said anything, and about four people in four hundred miles have worked out since that two instruments were not better than one, and that the room had got the one that can be asked for before the one that cannot, and that it got it that way because it was the only one anybody could make in a day.

---

Hettis Roke came in about the fourth hour of the afternoon and stood at the back with the waxed slate in its case and did not take it out, and about four people in that room have worked out since that a man who refused a room a roll on Sunday came back on the Wednesday of the same week to be in a room where a page with a notary's name on it was lying on a table with nothing on it, and that he came on the day it was new, and that nobody in that room asked him why.

A gate, a keeper and a set of days. Nineteen years and no column for a person and no column for what the days were for, and he has never once wanted either, and he has not wanted either this morning.

"**And I would like it entered that a man can refuse a page and be in a room the day after a page is made, and that both of those are the same man.**"

Nobody wrote it down and the woman of about twenty-nine did not say she would, and about four people in that room have worked out since that the register has a row for this day with nothing in it, and that a man said four sentences into it, and that this is the second time in about a week that a true thing has been said in that room and gone onto nothing, and that the first time was a man of about fifty-seven and a rope and a fortnight of his life, and that the second was a keeper of nineteen years who has eight people of his own on no page in four hundred miles.
