# Chapter 1001 — The Store's End Of That Row On A Monday

The tradesman came down the face of his own board with the cloth, and the cloth came off that face at the foot of the middle column where it stops every morning of this flood, and he folded it back over his own shoulder and stood at his own trestle with his own two hands on the two ends of that board.

It was Monday, the six hundred and eighty-sixth day of the Bare Month. The night had been hard and clear for the sixteenth morning running, and the sun was up before the light and came over the roof line at the far end of that market about the fourth hour. The air went thin along the ground early, so that the light got in under the awning at the store's end and lay the whole length of the walking spaces of that row before the row had finished waking. Four hundred yards of flags ran from the store's end of that row to where that row gave out into the side of that yard.

There is a stroke at every joint going up that row, and there are more of them than anybody has ever counted. At the store's end of it there are two, about the length of a hand each and a foot apart across the stones. The newer of the two was made before the light on the day before this one, with a stick coming up out of the grit and leaving a ridge standing along the stroke and a hollow at the near end of it, and nothing has walked on any part of that ridge since. The frost came out of the grit at that end of that row in the night and stood white in the bottom of that hollow, and it stood in the hollow because the hollow is the only place at that end of that row where the grit has not been trodden flat.

The trough on the flags at the top of that step had ice in it about the depth of a thumb and nothing had been fetched for it. The thread of water out of the crack in its near end came over the lip of that step and stopped in the joint of the flags a foot short of the walking space, where it has stopped every morning of the week. The rag lay folded on the stone lip of that step and nowhere else. The bar lay along the top step of that stair and not in its two sockets, and the two of them were empty in the far side of the door below.

Two stalls along the stallholder had his own board face in against the wood of his own trestle, with the bare back of it turned out into the row behind his own shoulder. The near corner of the walking space at the foot of that trestle was standing up on its edge about the depth of two fingers with the pale damp packed earth under it, and the foot of that trestle was still gone into the flag it went down into. His own board carries the grain of it and two battens at the back of it and nothing written on it at all, and nobody in that row has ever come and stood in front of it, because it faces the wood of its own trestle and not the row.

---

The woman of about sixty-nine with her tin came along that row at about the seventh hour with the tin under her right arm and her own stick in her own hand.

She went the length of that row and stopped at every joint along it and stood over each of them with the end of the stick held down against the stone and her own thumb on the top of it, and she lifted it and carried on. She stood over the two strokes at the store's end of that row until the shade of that awning had come a hand's width off the stones, and she made none of them.

Then she came back down the walking spaces with the tin still under her arm, went out past the store's end of that row to where the flags give out into the side of that yard, and stood there a while on the ground on the far side of it with the stick held down in front of her. Then she went back up the row and off out of it. She offered nothing to anybody in that row that morning, and she has offered nothing to anybody in that row on any morning before it.

---

Up that stair and into that room about the fifth hour, and the room was as it had been the day before and as it had been the week before that.

The keeper had the far end of that bench with her own register open in front of her and her own hand on the page of it. The second book lay open beside her register with its cover slab drawn back off the leaf under it, and the crack along the whole length of that column had filled and gone hard and taken the colour of the stone round it and stands a hair proud of the stone, and the dry cloth is lying in it.

The six strokes in the column that holds figures have a day standing against each of them and not one of the six struck out. The seventh place at the foot of that column is bare wood of the colour of the six above it. That bench had been moved about a foot along toward that wall and had not been put back, and there are four squares of clean flags about the size of a hand under the place it came off, and the grit in the joints inside them is pressed flat and has not come back up. The wood under the place where that cloth lay on that bench for about half a morning in an earlier week stands a shade paler than the wood either side of it along the whole length of the cloth and about a finger's width wide.

The woman of about fifty-two had carried that stool up that stair in her own two hands and set it down against the wall along the far side of that room, and she stood at the far end of that bench with her own shoulder to the wood behind the far end of it and her own hands at the sides of her dress. The clear floor between that stool and that wall is about the width of a hand the whole length of it, and the grit standing up in the joints of the flags inside that strip has not been walked on at any point of this flood. She has not sat on that stool once since the water came.

The block of writing was on the wall along the far side of that room and the plaster either side of it was the colour it has been since it went on, and nobody has ever said a word about it and nobody went and stood in front of it.

On the outside of that door at the foot of that stair a day stood out in chalk with about a finger's width of that writing gone down into the groove between the two planks. Under that day the bare piece of board stood clear of the near plank by the width of a thumb and carried the one mark that is on it, thick at the top and thin at the bottom, and nothing went under that date on any morning of this flood and nothing scraped it and nothing altered it.

---

About four people a day went along that row on their own business with the light on it.

Two of them looked down at the grit at the store's end of that row as they went past, and could have said afterwards that there were two strokes in the grit at that end of it and that one of them was standing up along its whole length with white standing in the near end of it. Two of them went along that row with their own eyes on the walking spaces of it and could not have said what was in the joints at the store's end of it.

Nobody stopped. A woman with a hoop of something over her own shoulder went up the far side of that row and did not look down at anything. A boy came down the middle of the flags, put his own feet on the walking space and went on, and could not have said afterwards what was in the grit at that end of that row.

---

The tradesman came up that stair at about the fifth hour with his own board under his arm and put it down on the near end of that bench with its face out and its foot toward the door. He came down the face of it with the cloth from the top of the top column, and the cloth came off the face at the foot of the middle column, and he folded it back over his own shoulder. Nineteen rates stand in three columns on the face of that board, and the middle column of the three stands about the depth of a thumb deeper than the two at the ends of it, and there is a space about the width of two fingers at the foot of it.

Then he said one thing out loud, into the middle of that room, about the width his own board takes up on that bench when it is lying on it.

**"This board takes up half of that end of that bench and it has taken up half of that end of that bench every morning since the water came, and there is a foot of that wood at the near end of it that nobody has put anything on," ** said the tradesman, at the near end of that bench with his own two hands on the two ends of his own board.

Nobody in that room said a word back to him about it. The keeper went on down the column that holds figures and came back up it, and the woman of about fifty-two stood at the far end of that bench and did not turn her own head about at it. Nobody thanked him for saying it and nobody told him it was not wanted.

The stallholder had his own trestle in that row all day and did not come up that stair. He got his own board up against the wood of that trestle at the sixth hour with its face in and the bare back of it turned out behind his own shoulder, and at about the tenth hour he took his own hand to the near foot of that trestle and worked at it with the edge of his own board, and about an inch came off the near side of that foot in shavings the length of a finger, and he put them in a heap at the foot of that walking space. The foot of that trestle was still gone into the flag it went down into while he did it. The carrier had the near wall with the satchel held up against his own ribs and the flap of it hanging down away from his own side. The man of about twenty-seven carried the bucket down that stair and up it again with the water standing flat in it.

The man of about fifty-seven was not in that room on that morning, and he has not been in it since the morning he came up that stair with his own coat buttoned and stood about two feet short of the middle of that bench with his own hands hanging at his own sides.

---

About the middle of the afternoon the light came off that awning and went up onto the wall of that store above the doors, and the walking spaces of that row stood pale along their whole length with the joints between them standing black.

At about the seventh hour the man of about twenty-seven came up that stair with the bucket full and the water standing flat in it, and set it down on the flags at the top of that step with its own handle turned to the wall. Where that bucket stood, the stone was wet in a ring about the size of a coin. He picked the bucket up again and went down, and the ring stood on that stone after he had gone off it.
