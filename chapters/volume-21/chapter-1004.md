# Chapter 1004 — The Wear In The Middle Of That Step

Adrian Vale put the flat of his own hand on the stone of a step about the middle of that stair and left it there, and the stone under that hand was cold and wet with the frost that came out of it in the night and gave him nothing at all.

It was Thursday, the six hundred and eighty-ninth day of the Bare Month. The night had been hard and clear and the sun was up before the light and came over the roof line at the far end of that market about the fourth hour. The frost stood white along the whole length of that stair out to about the seventh hour and stood thicker on the top of each tread than in the risers, and it was going off the treads faster than it was going off the risers because the light came in on that stair from the side of that market.

That step is a long way down from the top of that stair and a long way up from the flags, and there were steps above him on that stair and steps under him. He was on the one with the wear in the middle of it. The stone there shows about the size of a hand and a shade paler than the stone either side of it, and the flakes off it are in a heap at the foot of that step about a foot to one side of the middle of it against the riser, and no boot goes near that heap.

---

What Adrian Vale wanted on that stair was a thing with a shape to it that had no instrument in it at all. He wanted to be asked one thing out loud in that room, in daylight, with the date in chalk on the outside of the door behind his own shoulder, so that he could put down in one sentence what he thought that room ought to do about a thing coming back into it.

He knew the sentence. It had been standing ready in him for days and it was four parts long and it would take about as long to say as it takes to read a page of a book twice, and he had never once been asked for it, and there was no way on that stair to make anybody in that room ask him for it.

He had the other thing too and it was the other half of what he wanted, and it is the part he is good at. He knows what settles a room that has lost the ability to tell where a thing came from. Two records of the same morning in two places, entered by two people who have no interest in either of them, and then the two set side by side by somebody who is not either of them. That is the whole instrument. It is not difficult. He could draw the shape of it on a board with a piece of chalk in about the time it takes to eat, and he has drawn the shape of it in his own head on about nine hundred mornings now, and not one of those drawings has ever been in the hands of a person who could carry it out of a room.

The reason there is not one is a single fact and it is the plainest fact he knows. In this world a thing goes on a surface when a person writes it there, and a person writes it there when somebody who matters will read it, and the person who matters in that room has no use for a second column. So the two records he needs would have to be kept by people for whom keeping them is the whole of their day, and there are about four people a day past the foot of that stair, and none of them has ever been asked to keep one, and there is no form to ask them on and no notice to send them.

He went at it on that stair the way he went at every one of these, which is to take the thing apart and see how many pieces it has got. It has got two pieces and no joint in the middle of it. Somebody has to enter a thing twice, and somebody has to compare the two entries, and neither of those two somebodies can be any of the people who said the thing or heard it. He has been the second pair of eyes for about nine hundred mornings. He has never once been the third thing, which is the person that holds the two pages up.

That is the whole of what is wrong with that room and it has been the whole of it since before the water came, and he had four parts of a sentence about what it ought to do about a thing coming back into it, and there was nobody in that room to say it to.

---

He worked the stone while he stood there, because his own hands are what he has got.

He got his own fingers down at the near edge of that worn place and read it along about half of it the way a man reads an edge. The pale of it goes under the pale of the stone either side of it and has no rim and no lip, and a hand that has gone down that stair often enough does that to a stone without ever once thinking about it. He got his own thumb on the riser under the worn place and pressed, and the stone there was harder than the stone either side of it, because the hard of a stone is what is left when the wet has got into it and gone out of it a thousand times.

Then he put his own heel on the tread and gave it his own weight and let the stone take it, and it took it the way it had taken it every morning since the water came, without any account of how many mornings that was.

A hand of a man's size going down that stair twice a day for a flood does that. That is not a mystery and there is nothing to read in it. What he wanted was a morning on that step and one man on it, and a way of telling that morning out of all the other mornings of the same tread, and the stone has no such a way, and neither has the grit in the joints, and neither has the flags at the bottom of it, and neither has anything else in this city that a person can look at twice.

He came to the top of that stair at about the seventh hour, which is when the light got in under that awning and lay the whole length of that bench.

---

That room was carrying on when he came into it and it carried on for the rest of that day.

The keeper had the far end of that bench with her own register open in front of her and her own hand on the page of it, and she went down the column that holds figures and came back up it, and there are six strokes in that column with a day standing against each of them and not one of the six struck out, and the seventh place at the foot of it is bare wood of the colour of the six above it. The second book lay open beside that register with its cover slab drawn back off the leaf under it, and the dry cloth is lying in the crack along the column of that leaf, and no hand picked that book up and no hand turned it over and no hand wrote on it.

The sheet lay on that bench square with the wood, with six lines on it in two blocks of three and the space under the lower block bare from there to the near edge of it. The woman of about fifty-two stood at the far end of that bench with her own shoulder to the wood behind the far end of it and her own hands at the sides of her dress, and that stool stood against the wall along the far side of that room where she had set it down.

The man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board was at the near end of that bench with his own board lying face out on the wood and the cloth over his own shoulder. He came down the face of that board with the cloth and the cloth came off the face at the foot of the middle column, and he folded the cloth back over his own shoulder and carried his own board down that stair to his own trestle two along.

Adrian Vale stood at the near end of that bench for about as long as it takes to say a thing twice, and no mouth in that room put anything to him at any hour of that day, and nobody in that room said his name, and nobody in that room was waiting for him to have a useful thing to say in it. The carrier had the near wall with the satchel up against his own ribs. About four people a day went past the foot of that stair while the light was on the flags.

He did not put the four parts of that sentence into the middle of that morning. He had done that once before, on a morning in an earlier flood, and it went out of that room in his own mouth and nobody answered it and nobody turned about at it, and he was not going to stand in the middle of that room again saying a thing into it that the people in it had not asked him for.

What he did with it instead was to say it to himself on that stair, in his own mouth, quietly, four parts long, standing on that step with his own hand off the stone by then. It took about as long as it takes to read a page twice. It came out of him and it went nowhere, because there was nobody on that stair to hand it to, and it is not on any surface anywhere in this city and no leaf on either of those two books carries a scratch of it.

He is a man who has been drawing that instrument in his own head for about nine hundred mornings and has never once had a room that wanted it.

---

At about the tenth hour he came down that stair to go along that row and out past the last named house, and as he went down past that step he put his own palm flat on the worn place in the middle of it for about as long as it takes to say a thing twice, and the frost on the stone took the shape of his own hand in about the length of a breath and took it back again in about as long.

In the heap of flakes at the foot of that step against the riser, one flake was standing up on its edge where his own heel had been.
