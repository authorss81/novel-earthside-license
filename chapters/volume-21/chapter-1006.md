# Chapter 1006 — Four Hundred Yards Of Flags And Nobody Stopping

The tradesman got that board up on his own trestle at about the fifth hour with its face out into the row, and the wind came down the four hundred yards of that row and got under the foot of it and lifted about the width of a thumbnail, and he put his own heel on the walking space and held it with his own hand there until it settled.

It was Saturday, the six hundred and ninety-first day of the Bare Month. The wind had gone into the north-east in the night and had come down by the light to something that could not lift a corner of a cloth, and the air over eleven miles of flats stood close under that awning at the store's end of that row until about the seventh hour. The sun came over the roof line at the far end of that market at about the fifth hour, and by six it was in under the awning at the store's end and standing along that bench, and it never got past the middle of that wood all morning.

The walking spaces of that row stood pale and hard with the wet standing in the joints between them about the depth of a finger, and that wet stood in the four hundred yards of them from the store's end of that row to where that row gives out into the side of that yard.

The trough on the flags at the top of that step had ice in it about the depth of a thumb. The thread of water out of the crack in its near end came over the lip of that step and stopped in the joint of the flags a foot short of the walking space, where it has stopped every morning of the week. The rag lay folded on the stone lip of that step. The bar lay along the top step of that stair and not in its two sockets.

There is a stroke at every joint going up that row and there are more of them than anybody has ever counted, and the grit stands up in grains between them where nothing has trodden it.

---

That row is a place where about four people a day go along on their own business with the light on it, and the boards go up along it, and nobody stops at one of them.

Two boards go up in it. One of them has its face out into the row on a trestle and nineteen rates standing in three columns on that face, with the middle column standing about the depth of a thumb deeper than the two at the ends of it, and a space about the width of two fingers at the foot of it. The other one has its face in against the wood of its own trestle with the bare back of it turned out into the row behind the man's own shoulder, and that bare back carries the grain of it and two battens and nothing written on it at all.

Neither man has ever had a person stand in front of his board. Not on the morning before, and not on the morning before that, and not on any morning of the flood. The stallholder puts that board up with its face to the wood of his own trestle every morning because he has never known one reason to put it out, and he has said so out loud once in a room over that market and has not said it out loud anywhere else since. In the pocket of the front of his coat there is a piece of chalk, and it stayed in that pocket all morning, and his own hand went flat over the outside of that pocket once at about the ninth hour and came off it again.

---

The man of about thirty-one who carries things for a living came along that row at about the eighth hour with the satchel on his own back and the flap of it hanging down away from his own side.

He came up the walking space on the far side of that row with his own weight on the crust of it and his own eyes on the stones, and he went the whole length of the four hundred yards of them. He did not stop at either of those two boards and did not turn his own head at the face of either of them, and he went past about nine feet off the man of about twenty-seven at the top of that step.

The man of about twenty-seven had that step to himself all morning. He filled the bucket at the low place in the stone and carried it up that stair with the water standing flat in it, and he did not look up at that man as that man went past him.

About nine feet off him, the man of about thirty-one who carries things for a living said a thing out loud to nobody.

He said it to himself, on his own feet, going up the walking space of that row, in the ordinary voice a person uses when nobody is within hearing. What he said was a thing that had been said in the room over that market on an earlier day, in other words than the ones that were used to it, and he did not know it while he was saying it and nobody in that row knew it and nobody in that room knows now.

He did not say it twice. He carried on up the row.

---

About four people a day went along that row with the light on it and nobody stopped at anything in it.

A woman went up the far side of that row with a hoop over her own shoulder and got to the near end of the store's end of it and stood there for the length of about nine feet and then went on up the row. A man going down that row stopped about as long as it takes to read a thing twice and looked up at the face of a board on a trestle and went on down that row. Two boys came down the middle of those flags after one another and put their own feet on the walking space and went on.

Nobody in that row ever had a person stand in front of the face of that board, and nobody in that row ever had a person stand in front of the bare back of the other one either.

At the far end of that market a board stood on the outside wall on its one nail above its step, and the hole of a second nail was empty above it and the wood round the lip of that hole had gone soft a whole finger's width from the hole all the way round, and pale dust lay in the gap behind the bottom edge of that board about the depth of a grain of rice, along the whole length of it.

---

The tradesman came down the face of his own board with the cloth at about the ninth hour, and the cloth came off the face at the foot of the middle column and stopped at the near edge of the space at the foot of it, and he folded the cloth back over his own shoulder. He took that board down off his own trestle and carried it under his own arm and set it down on the flags on its own edge against the foot of his own trestle, and then he took it up again and put it back on his own trestle face out, because that is the only way he has ever had it.

The stallholder worked at the near foot of his own trestle with the edge of his own board at about the tenth hour, and about an inch came off the near side of that foot in shavings the length of a finger, and he put them in a heap at the foot of that walking space. The foot of that trestle was still gone into the flag it went down into.

The woman of about sixty-nine with her tin came along that row at about the seventh hour, went the length of it and stopped at every joint along it, and made none of them.

The man of about thirty-one who carries things for a living went on up the four hundred yards of that row and out past the store's end of it and went on along the walking spaces of the side of that yard and off out of it, with the flap of that satchel hanging down and not turned back.

---

The light came along that row at about the middle of the afternoon and lay on the face of that board on the outside wall at the far end of that market and took the pale dust out of the gap behind the bottom edge of it and made it stand up white along the whole length of that gap, and the empty second hole above that board stood in that light with nothing in it and nothing going into it.
