# Chapter 1002 — Standing Over Every Joint And Making None

The tradesman put that board up on his own trestle at about the fifth hour with its face out into the row and its foot toward the door, and the nineteen rates on the face of it stood pale in the air of that morning, and by the sixth hour two people had gone along that row and read the middle column of it across without stopping.

It was Tuesday, the six hundred and eighty-seventh day of the Bare Month. There was a white closeness over eleven miles of flats that morning that did not thin along the ground the way it had thinned the day before, and it stood under that awning until about the eighth hour and then went off it in about a quarter of an hour. The frost had gone out of the joints of the flags inside that room in the night and had come back into them after the sun was on the walking spaces, and the grit at the store's end of that row stood up in grains again where nothing had trodden it.

The trough on the flags at the top of that step had ice in it about the depth of a thumb. The thread of water out of the crack in its near end came over the lip of that step and stopped in the joint of the flags a foot short of the walking space, where it has stopped every morning of the week, and a small ring had come up out of the grit round the end of it in the night and was standing there while the light came. The bar lay along the top step of that stair and not in its two sockets. The rag lay folded on the stone lip of that step.

---

What the people who went along that row did with the face of that board was about half one thing and about half the other.

About half of them stopped walking for the length of a step, turned their own heads to it, read the middle column of it across from the left of it to the right of it, and went on. They could have said afterwards what stood at the head of that column and how many rates there were in the two columns at the ends of it. About half of them went along that row with their own eyes on the walking spaces of it and never turned their own heads at it at all, and could not have said afterwards what was written on it.

Nobody stopped for longer than that. Nobody stood in front of that board. Nobody put a hand on it and nobody asked the man who kept it a single thing about it, then or on any morning before it, and he was not asked it after the light had gone off that row either.

Two stalls along the stallholder had his own board face in against the wood of his own trestle with the bare back of it turned out into the row behind his own shoulder, and the bare back of it carries the grain of it and two battens and nothing written on it at all. About the same half of the people going along that row looked at the back of that board as went along looking at the front of the other one, and what they got from the back of it was two battens.

---

At about the seventh hour the woman of about sixty-nine with her tin came along that row with the tin under her right arm and her own stick in her own hand.

She came on to that row at the far end of it and walked the length of the walking spaces with her own feet on them and her own eyes on the stones, and she did not turn her own head at the face of any board standing in that row, and she did not stop at one.

She stopped at every joint along it instead. She stood over each of them with the end of the stick held down against the stone and her own thumb on the top of it, and she stood there about as long as it takes to say a thing twice, and then she lifted it and carried on to the next one. There is a stroke at every joint going up that row and there are more of them than anybody has ever counted, and she went the whole length of that row and made none of them.

At the store's end of that row she stood over the two strokes there with the stick held down beside them and not on them, and the white was still standing in the hollow at the near end of the newer one where the frost had been, and the ridge along its whole length stood up as it had stood since the day before. She lifted the stick and carried on out past that end of the row and went along the walking spaces of the four hundred yards of it with the tin still under her arm.

---

The man of about twenty-seven had that step to himself all morning. He filled the bucket at the low place in the stone and carried it up that stair four times with the water standing flat in it, and on the last of those four he stayed on the flags with his own hand on the outside of the rim of that trough and looked at the ice in it. Nothing had been fetched for that trough and nothing had been broken in it.

At about the tenth hour the stallholder got his own hand to the near foot of his own trestle and worked at it with the edge of his own board, and about an inch came off the near side of that foot in shavings the length of a finger, and he put them in a heap at the foot of that walking space. The foot of that trestle was still gone into the flag it went down into and it went on standing in it while he got his own board up off the wood and carried it down the flags.

About four people a day went along that row on their own business with the light on it. Two of them read the middle column of that board across and could have said what stood at the head of it. Two of them went past it and could not have said what was written on the face of it. Nobody stopped.

---

The woman of about sixty-nine came back along that row at about the middle of the afternoon, when the white had gone off the flats and the light had come round onto the walking spaces and was lying along them.

She came back up the far side of that row and stopped at a joint about the middle of the four hundred yards of it, one she had stopped at on the way out. She put the end of the stick down on that stone and held it down there for about as long as it takes to read a thing twice, and it is longer than the length of her stop at any of the others, and she did not draw anything at all and made no stroke in the grit, and the joint was left standing there with nothing in it that had not been standing in it before she came.

Then she lifted the stick and went on up the row with the tin under her arm and off out of it, and she said nothing to anybody in that row on her way out, and she was not said anything to either.

---

There is a stone in the walking space at the store's end of that row with the near corner of it worn round by boots going over it for years, and the polish on the top of it takes the light along the row as she goes, and it does not stop anywhere between the store's end of that row and where she went out of it.
