# Chapter 1003 — She Goes Down The Column And Back Up It

The keeper went down the column that holds figures with her own forefinger beside it and came back up it, and at the foot of it her own hand stopped in the air a finger's width above the bare place and went up the column again.

It was Wednesday, the six hundred and eighty-eighth day of the Bare Month. It had rained in the night and stopped before the light, and the crust of that ground out past the last named house had gone soft on the top of it again in about an hour. Four hundred yards of flags ran from the store's end of that row to where that row gave out into the side of that yard, and the walking spaces of them stood pale and hard with the wet standing in the joints between them about the depth of a finger. The light came in under the awning at the store's end about the sixth hour and lay along that bench as far as the middle of the wood and no further.

The trough on the flags at the top of that step had ice in it about the depth of a thumb and nothing had been fetched for it. The thread of water out of the crack in its near end came over the lip of that step and stopped in the joint of the flags a foot short of the walking space, and it has stopped in that same joint every morning of the week, and a small ring had come up out of the grit round the end of it. The bar lay along the top step of that stair and not in its two sockets. The rag lay folded on the stone lip of that step.

---

The woman of about fifty-two came up that stair at about the fifth hour with that stool in her own two hands, one hand at each side of the seat of it, and she set it down against the wall along the far side of that room in the place she sets it down, and she did not sit on it. She has not sat on that stool once since the water came, and she carried it up that stair and down it again and up it again that morning and did not sit on it any of those times, and at the going of the light she stood at the far end of that bench with her own shoulder to the wood behind the far end of it and her own hands at the sides of her dress.

She went down that stair at about the tenth hour with that stool in her own two hands and set it down on the flags at the bottom of it, and she stood at the bottom while the man of about twenty-seven went past her up that stair and down it again, and she carried it back up at about the eleventh hour and set it down in the same place against that wall.

---

The keeper went down that column and came back up it. There are six strokes in it and a day standing against each of them, and the days are legible and the strokes are not even, and none of the six has been struck out at any point of this flood. The seventh place at the foot of that column is bare wood of the colour of the six above it.

She went at it from the head of that column to the foot of it four times that morning, and the work did not come out of it. There is nothing at the foot of that column to go down at, and a figure in that column means a day standing against it, and what a day stands against a figure for is hers to decide and she has not decided it.

**"That seventh place has been bare since the morning the sixth of them went in, and it is going to be bare this morning as well, and I am not going to put a mark in it because I have not got anything to put a day against," ** said the woman of about twenty-nine who keeps a public register, at the far end of that bench with her own hand flat on the wood and the date on the outside of that door behind her own shoulder.

She said that once, into that room, and then she went down that column again with her own finger beside it. Nobody in that room said a word back to her about it. Nobody asked her when she was going to fill it and nobody said anything to her about the six that are in it, and no mouth in that room said what those six were for or whose they were.

---

The man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board came up at the fifth hour with his own board under his arm and put it down on the near end of that bench with its face out and its foot toward the door, and he came down the face of it with the cloth from the top of the top column.

The cloth stopped at the foot of the middle column, where it stops every morning. At the foot of that middle column there is a space about the width of two fingers, and the cloth comes off the face of that board at the near edge of that space and does not go into it, and he folded the cloth back over his own shoulder and stood looking down at that space with his own two hands on the two ends of that board.

That work did not come out either. He came down the face of that board three times that morning and the cloth came off the face of it at the near edge of that space every one of those three times, and he did not write anything in it and did not take anything out of it and did not go at that space with his own thumb.

Nobody in that room said one word about that space. The keeper went on down her own column. The woman of about fifty-two stood at the far end of that bench and did not look across it.

---

The two books lay open side by side on that bench with the sheet beside them. The keeper's own register is open at the column that holds figures, and the second book lies open on its own leaf with its cover slab drawn back off the leaf under it, and the crack along the whole length of that column has gone hard and taken the colour of the stone round it and stands a hair proud of it, and the dry cloth is lying in it.

Those two books carry two accounts of one day. Nobody in that room compared them that morning and nobody in that room has compared them on any morning of this flood, and no mouth in that room said which of the two of them is the one that happened, because no mouth in that room has any way of knowing.

That sheet lies on the wood at the far end of that bench a hand's width in from its right edge with six lines on it in two blocks of three, and the space under the lower block is bare from there to the near edge of the sheet and across to the width of a palm.

At about the middle of the morning the keeper squared that sheet with the heel of her own hand and pushed it a finger's width along the wood with two fingers, so that the long edge of it stood square with the edge of that bench where it has not stood square at any point of this flood. The six lines on it did not move relative to the sheet, and the bare space under the lower block came with it, and nothing was added to it and nothing was taken off it, and the pencil stayed in the pocket of her dress all the rest of that day.

---

The carrier had the near wall with the satchel held up against his own ribs and the flap of it hanging down away from his own side, and he turned about off that wall twice and looked at the flags and turned about again. The man of about twenty-seven carried the bucket up that stair four times with the water standing flat in it.

The block of writing was on the wall along the far side of that room and nobody went and stood in front of it and nobody said a word about it. On the outside of that door at the foot of that stair the day stood out in chalk with about a finger's width of that writing gone down into the groove between the two planks, and under that day the bare piece of board carried the one mark that is on it.

About four people a day went past the foot of that stair on their own business with the light on the flags. Not one word of anything said in that room came past the bottom step of it.

---

The keeper's own hand comes to rest at the same place on the near edge of that bench every morning of this flood, about a hand's width in from the end of it, and the wood there stands a shade paler than the wood either side of it about the width of four fingers, and the grain of it has gone flat under her own hand while the grain on either side of that place is standing up out of the damp that comes up that stair.
