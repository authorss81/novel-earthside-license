# Chapter 0571 — A Barrow Put Down At The Wrong End Of That Yard

Adrian Vale put the flat of his left hand on the edge of the top board of that stack and his right hand through the near handle of that barrow and lifted both of them at once at about the seventh hour on the two hundred and fifty-sixth day of the Bare Month, a Friday, and the boards that were lying on top of that stack slid off it and came to rest again against the boards underneath with a noise like a door shut a long way off, and the salt that had come up that road in them and the grit that had worked into their joints since came out of the top of that stack and went down the side of it in a grey line onto the boards of that yard.

The salt wharf stands at the near end of eleven miles of flats, and the salt in that yard came up a road that is four hundred miles long and open from end to end, and the yard in front of the wharf is not as long as it is at the far end of it, and nobody in this city has taken a measure of that yard and nobody has written one down. There was a wind coming off the low side of the flats along that yard all morning and it came in a long way and went out again the same way and never settled anywhere in between. About four people walked the near end of those flats in the ordinary course between the seventh hour and the eleventh hour and none of them came into that yard.

---

The man of about thirty-eight at that wharf was at the foot of the stack with a knife in his own right hand and the handle of it turned away from him so that the edge of it was out of the way of the boards, and he had a tally-board of his own lying face down on the top of that stack where the boards had been until Adrian Vale put his hand on them. He has carried that board out of his own yard and into his own yard for a good many years and there is a figure of this city on the side of it that the sun does not get to, and it has lain face down on that stack since before the seventh week of this flood, and no hand in that yard and no hand in this city has ever turned it over and had a look at that side of it. He put his own left hand flat on the top of that board for a moment to steady it while he reached past it for the next board down, and he said nothing about the barrow and he said nothing about the board.

---

Adrian Vale took the top four boards off that stack one at a time and put them into that barrow and wheeled it along the boards of that yard to the trestle at the far end of it, and came back and did it again, and the second time he was wheeling it he came to the near gate of that yard and stopped there instead of carrying on, and he put the legs of that barrow down and let go of both handles and stood beside it.

The man of about thirty-eight at that wharf said the thing that is said, to the boards.

"That is the wrong end of the yard for that."

"**The far trestle is where I was taking them and the ground at the near gate is firmer, and if you want them stacked by the water they can be stacked in the morning when there is somebody to hold the stack.**"

Nobody in that yard acted on that. Nobody in that yard improved on him and nobody in that yard thanked him and nobody in that yard said that he was right, and the man of about thirty-eight at that wharf went on cutting the strapping off the boards with his own knife and did not ask him a question.

---

From the second time he came back to that gate until the light came off the flats, the boards at the top of that stack came off one at a time and went to the trestle at the far end of that yard by a way round the outside of that stack instead of through the middle of it, and the going round added the length of that stack to every one of those trips. Two men carried them, one of them about thirty-one with his own hands full the whole time and the other a man of that yard who has never been on a roll in this city and is not on one now.

Nobody in that yard said a word to Adrian Vale about any of it. He was at the far end of that yard with the barrow between his own boots when the last of them went by, and he did not know they had gone the long way, and nobody in that yard said that he was right.

---

He came back to that stack at about the tenth hour because there were two boards left on it that he had not taken, and he put his own hand flat on the near corner of the board lying face down on the top of that stack to hold himself on the edge of the boards while he got his other hand down to the lower one, and the face of that board was warm under his palm and the grit on it was thick and the corner of it did not move under him and he did not turn it.

Four mornings, four figures and no fifth, and that is the whole of what has ever come off these flats: the five hundred and eleventh day of this flood, the five hundred and fifteenth, the five hundred and seventeenth and the five hundred and twenty-first, and the ground was eleven miles of it and each of the four was got by a person with a slate under one arm and a tin in two hands. Those four numbers do not agree and they are not going to be made to agree in this city. Nobody counted anything at the near end of those flats on this morning, and there was nothing there that a person could have counted.

That stack is squared off at both ends and it stands about the height it stood at the seventh hour, and the grit that came out of the joints of those boards is still lying in a grey line down the side of it, and nobody in that yard swept it up, and the board on the top of it is lying the same way it was lying at the seventh hour.
