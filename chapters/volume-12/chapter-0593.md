# Chapter 0593 — The Last Morning On Which Nobody Asked Him

The man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board set his board down flat on the table under the one window of the room over a market and squared the near edge of it against the near edge of that table with both hands. Then he took his hands off it and stood looking down at the face of it. It was about the ninth hour on the two hundred and seventy-eighth day of the Bare Month, a Saturday. Nineteen rates run across that board in three columns, and at the foot of the column between the other two there is a space about as wide as two of his fingers with nothing whatever in it.

A few minutes later the man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall came up the stair at the back of that room with nothing in his hands. He put two fingers of his right hand on the near edge of that board where it sat on that table, and kept them there. The board did not slide again that morning. The date was chalked on the door at the foot of that stair and there is no name under it and there never has been one. About nine people were in that room at that hour and not one of them was sent for anything.

Nobody in that room said a word about the two fingers, and nobody in that room asked the man of about thirty-nine a single thing about the space. The name that was in that space is not printed on any page in four hundred miles. He has never said it to anybody, and nobody in this city has ever asked him what it is.

---

The man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall said the thing that is said, to the one window, and he said it in about nine words and it took him about four seconds.

"**Sixteen posts and eleven withies out past the last named house and I have never taken a cord to it, and nobody in this row is going to ask me why.**"

Nobody in that room improved on that and nobody thanked him for it and nobody said that he was right. He said it with two fingers of his right hand still on the near edge of another man's board, and he did not say a second thing about the fence that morning. Nobody in that room measured that fence. Nobody in that room moved a post in it.

He took those two fingers off the near edge of the board and put them on the face of it, a hand's width back from the space at the foot of the column between the other two, and said the thing that is said, to that space, and it was short.

"Where does that go."

"**There is a space on this board about two fingers wide with nothing in it, and I have not said where it came from, and nobody in this room is going to ask me.**"

No mouth in that room improved on what he said, and none of them thanked him for it, and not one of them told him he was right. The man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board put two fingers of his own hand on the face of his board a hand's width back from the space, and left them there.

---

Over the back of the chair at the far end of that bench there is a length of new rope, and it is as good as the day it was brought into this city, and it lay there the whole of that morning. No hand in that room lifted it off that chair or picked it up or took it up or cut it. Nobody in that room asked the man it belongs to one single thing about it, and he was not in that room and has not been asked anything by anybody anywhere in this city in this flood. The coil of rope in the handcart at the near gate is a different rope, and it has been used, and it is not that rope, and nobody in that room said a word about either of them.

---

The register is on its shelf behind that bench with its spine to the wall. Down that page there are five figures with a day written beside each of them, one under another, in the order they were made, and none of the five struck. The space on the right-hand side of that page is a column going the whole height of it with nothing in it from end to end. A second slate stands on that shelf edge up, its face to the plaster, a day cut into the head of it, nothing under that day whatever. It was not picked up and not turned over and not written on at any hour of that morning, and the only hand in that room which came near that shelf was the keeper's.

There is a piece of chalk in the inside breast pocket of the man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall, and it has not come out of that pocket since the two hundred and ninety-eighth day of this flood. Nobody asked him about it this morning. Nobody has asked him about it on any other morning in this flood either.

At about the eleventh hour he took two fingers off the near edge of that board and put that hand down at his side and went down the stair after the others. The man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board put his board under his arm with the face of it turned in against his ribs and went after him.

By then the room had lost the only hour in which it could have asked either of those men anything, and it had not asked. The space at the foot of the column between the other two was still about two fingers wide, and it still had nothing whatever in it. The wood of that table where that board had lain all morning was warmer than the wood round it, and had no mark on it at all.
