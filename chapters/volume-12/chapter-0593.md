# Chapter 0593 — The Last Morning On Which Nobody Asked Him

The man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board set his own board down flat on the table under the one window of the room over a market at about the ninth hour on the two hundred and seventy-eighth day of the Bare Month, a Saturday, and squared the near edge of it against the near edge of that table with his own two hands and then took his own hands off it and stood looking down at the face of it, and the face of that board is nineteen rates going across in three columns and a space at the foot of the column between the other two about as wide as two of his fingers with nothing whatever in it. The man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall came up the stair at the back of that room a few minutes after him with nothing in his own hands and put two fingers of his own right hand on the near edge of that board where it sat on that table, and kept them there, and the board did not slide again that morning. The date on that morning was chalked on the door at the foot of that stair and there is no name under it and there never has been one. About nine people were in that room at that hour and not one of them was sent for anything.

Nobody in that room said a word about the two fingers, and nobody in that room asked the man of about thirty-nine a single thing about the space at the foot of the column between the other two, and the name that was in that space is not printed on any page in four hundred miles and he has never said it to anybody and nobody in this city has ever asked him what it is.

---

The man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall said the thing that is said, to the one window, and he said it in about nine words and it took him about four seconds.

"**Sixteen posts and eleven withies out past the last named house and I have never taken a cord to it, and nobody in this row is going to ask me why.**"

Nobody in that room improved on that and nobody in that room thanked him for it and nobody in that room said that he was right, and the man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall said it with his own right hand still on the near edge of another man's board and did not say a second thing about the fence that morning, and nobody in that room measured that fence and nobody in that room moved a post in it.

The man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall took his own two fingers off the near edge of that board, put them on the face of it a hand's width back from the space at the foot of the column between the other two, and said the thing that is said, to that space, and it was short.

"Where does that go."

"**There is a space on this board about two fingers wide with nothing in it, and I have not said where it came from, and nobody in this room is going to ask me.**"

No mouth in that room improved on what he said, none of them thanked him for it, and not one of them told him he was right, and the man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board put his own two fingers on the face of his own board a hand's width back from the space and left them there.

---

Over the back of the chair at the far end of that bench there is a length of new rope, and it is as good as the day it was brought into this city, and it lay there the whole of that morning. No hand in that room lifted it off that chair or picked it up or took it up or cut it, and nobody in that room asked the man it belongs to one single thing about it, and the man it belongs to was not in that room and has not been asked anything by anybody anywhere in this city in this flood. The coil of rope in the handcart at the near gate is a different rope and has been used and is not that rope, and nobody in that room said a word about either of them.

---

The register is on its shelf behind that bench with its spine to the wall. Down that page there are five figures with a day written beside each of them, one under another, in the order they were made, and none of the five struck, and the space on the right-hand side of that page is a column going the whole height of it with nothing in it from end to end. A second slate stands on that shelf edge up, its face to the plaster, a day cut into the head of it, nothing under that day whatever, and it was not picked up and not turned over and not written on at any hour of that morning, and the only hand in that room that came near that shelf was the keeper's.

There is no morning after this one in this flood on which anybody in this city could have asked that man of about thirty-four what is wrong with his own board, and what is wrong with a page, and there is a piece of chalk in his inside breast pocket that has not come out of it since the two hundred and ninety-eighth day of this flood, and nobody asked him about either of them this morning and nobody has asked him about either of them on any other morning in this flood either, and there is no hand in this city that is going to.

At about the eleventh hour the man of about thirty-four took his own two fingers off the near edge of that board and put that hand down at his side and went down that stair after the others, and the man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board put his board under his own arm with the face of it turned in against his own ribs and went after him, and the space at the foot of the column between the other two was still about two fingers wide and still had nothing whatever in it, and he put the flat of his own hand flat on the wood of that table over where it had been lying and it was warmer than the wood round it and there was no mark there at all.
