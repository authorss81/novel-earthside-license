# Chapter 0108 — Four Days Out

On the eighteenth day of the month after this one, which was the hundred and eighth day of the flood and a Thursday, nine people went up a nine-mile road in the wrong weather because anybody may pick the work up, and the work is not a post, and the plan is four days out of this city and the date is the second of the four, and a woman of about sixty-one who cannot read handed nine years of paper to a girl of seventeen and said she would like somebody to have the date and not her, and a woman of seventy stayed behind and gave a reason that was about herself.

**The licence is fifty-eight days old. The undertaking is void. The field was not paid on the first of March. The fee is owing with no date for the forty-ninth time. The black line in his right palm is one hundred and seven days old and has not moved one sixteenth of an inch.**

---

**The nine are a woman of about sixty-one of a house with a burnt lintel who cannot read, a docker of fifty-eight who cannot read and who has one of the four slates, a Hearthguard of forty-four whose grandfather's hand is nine miles up this road, a mason of thirty-four years who has never read a join in the nineteen stones, a man of sixty-one who has copied other men's sentences for nineteen years, a girl of seventeen who has another of the four slates, a woman of twenty-four of the first group, a boy of nine, and a man of thirty-two from another world who is on no roster of anything.**

A keeper of about seventy did not go, and she gave the reason in a doorway in about nine seconds and it is in the book in her own words.

"**I have kept a floor for fifty-one years and I am not going to be a person who walks nine miles in the wrong weather for a wall, and if I go I will want to be carried, and then somebody will have to carry me, and I would rather be at home with a pail.**"

"Nobody is going to ask you why."

"**I know nobody is going to ask me why, and that is the fifth of the five things on a page in an arch and it is a question and not a duty, and I have not been asked anything in a hundred and eight days and I am seventy and I am going to keep it that way as long as I possibly can.**"

---

**And the four days are four days and the reason is not sentiment. They go up on the eighteenth. They are on the stones on the nineteenth. They come down on the twentieth and the twenty-first, because the weather on this road in this month is the weather on the walk-round in ice, and because the eighth of the eight is below the point where a cart can go and a man carrying a slate has to be brought back up it.**

"Nine miles out and nine miles back is a day's walk for a person who does it for a living," said a man of about forty-four of the outer ward, nineteen years in, "**and about nine people is not a day's walk for a person who does it for a living when three of them are past fifty and one of them is nine, and I have walked it in the wrong weather twice in a hundred and eight days and both times I was glad to be told the day before.**"

"And it started on the fourteenth," said a woman of twenty-four. "**And on the fourteenth four people finished failing to write down a room, and on the fifteenth a bone-setter accepted a thing, and on the sixteenth we found out we cannot answer the question, and on the seventeenth a clerk told us the answer is not a person, and today we go up a road, and I would like it entered that in five days this city has done four things in rooms and one thing on a road and that the one on the road is the only one any of us has proposed.**"

---

**And at about the fourth hour of the morning they went out past the salt works, and the wrong weather came with them, and a boy of nine was fifty yards ahead within nine minutes and a docker of fifty-eight was the last of the nine for the first two miles and did not apologise for it and was not asked to.**

The road is nine miles of packed stone and mud above the salt works, and it goes up past a burnt lintel, and eleven households keep it, and they are not a council and there is no levy and there is no clerk, and the keeping is a day each in a bad week and a quarter of a day in a good one, and it is a promise and not a sum, and the eleven households are named on nothing. Three of them had a gate open. Two of them had not. A carter said good morning to a docker and did not say good morning to a Hearthguard, and nobody remarked on either.

**And a boy of nine counted the households on the way up, because he had been counting gates since the fourth mile and nobody had asked him to, and he counted eleven, and then he counted them again and got nine, and a woman of twenty-four walked back two hundred yards with him to find the two he had missed, and they were a house with a shutter open and a house with a wall, and the wall is a household and the shutter is a household, and he counted a shutter.**

"Eleven households keep this road," said a woman of twenty-four. "**And a boy of nine counted nine because two of them look like something else, and I would like it entered that I walked back two hundred yards in the wrong weather to check a boy's count and that the boy's count was better than the number on the page, and that we are not changing the eleven.**"

"I counted a shutter," said Tam Crane.

"**You counted a shutter and you were right, and a shutter is a household and a wall is a household, and about nine people in this city would rather the road were kept by two gates than by eleven doors, and the reason is on a page and the reason is that a page is a thing you can be angry at.**"

**And a woman of about sixty-one gave something away on the second mile, in the rain, without stopping, and it is in the book in her own words, and the girl of seventeen wrote it down in a book and did not improve it and has it still.**

"You are giving me nine years," said the girl of seventeen.

"**I am giving you nine years of my own hand. The slates are the work. The slates are one year and four hands and a man of thirty-four years and a woman of fifty-eight who cannot read. My paper is nine years of a woman of sixty-one writing the same nineteen shapes out again in a colder hand each February, because that was the job, because nobody else was doing it, and because I have never once written down what I think they mean, and I have said that in about four rooms in nine years and nobody has written it down until the sixty-fifth day of the new month, which was the ninetieth day of this flood.**"

"What do you want done with it."

"**I would like somebody to have the date and not me.** The date is on a slate and it is the only thing on the slate and it is the nineteenth day of the month after this one and it is tomorrow. And I have been the only one doing this for nine years and I am sixty-one and I would like to stop being the person a thing depends on, and there is no other way to stop it than to hand it over on a road, and that is a ridiculous way to do it and I have thought about it for nine years."

Nobody improved on that. **And it is entered that she kept nothing back, and it is entered that the reason she kept nothing back is that she has said for four days that she would like somebody to have the date and not her, and that the handing over was done on the second mile in the rain in about four seconds by a woman of sixty-one to a girl of seventeen, and that nobody asked her twice whether she had.**

---

**And then, on the fourth mile, a docker of fifty-eight who cannot read said the thing that turned the rest of the day over, and she said it because she was looking at the paper, and it is in the book in her own words, and it is ten words, and the room did not check the count, which is entered.**

"**The oldest year has two lines struck out of it.**"

Nobody stopped walking for about nineteen seconds and two of the nine stopped walking altogether.

"Nine years," said the girl of seventeen. "**Nine years of nineteen shapes and the oldest year has seventeen.**"

"**It has nineteen lines and two of them are struck and one of the struck is struck twice, and I do not remember striking them, and I have looked at that page about four times in nine years and I have never once asked myself about the two.**"

"Then it is seventeen and not nineteen," said a man of thirty-two.

"**Then it is nineteen and two lines are struck,**" said a mason of thirty-four years, "**and a struck line in a working record is a line somebody crossed because they did not know, and it is not a smaller count, and I have watched four hundred men in nineteen years treat a crossed-out line as a number that has gone down, and the number has not gone down. The number is nineteen. It has been nineteen every year and it is nineteen now and the two lines are a hole in a page and a hole in a page is a place where a number used to be.**"

"A copy," said a man of sixty-one who copies other men's sentences. "**The slates are one year. Nobody in this city has compared nine years of a woman's handwriting in nine years, including me, and I have wanted to since I was asked to keep this city's words and I have never had nine years of anything of my own to compare.**"

"Then we compare them on the road," said a woman of twenty-four.

"**We do not compare them on the road,**" said the mason, "**because nine years of one woman's February in the rain is not a thing you read standing up in the wet, and because about four of us cannot read, and because if we do it here and get it wrong then in four months somebody will say this city read the nineteen stones on a road in the wrong weather. We take the paper down. Somebody who can read the local hand and somebody who can read the old hand sit in a room. And it is entered today that nobody has done that in nine years, with the date on it.**"

---

**And they came to the burnt lintel at about the seventh hour of the evening and it is nine miles, and one of the nine came in last, and the last one was not the boy of nine, who had been fifty yards ahead since the fourth mile and who came in first, and the last one was a man of thirty-two from another world, and the reason he came in last is that he spent about nine minutes at the third mile asking a woman of fifty-eight to read him a date off a slate, because he cannot read the local hand and the date was in it.**

"I have been the man who reads a number off a wall four feet back from it for a hundred and eight days," he said, "**and I have read out loud four times in five days and it has not moved, and I walked nine miles today behind a docker with a slate and asked her to read me a date, and I wrote it down, and that is the whole of what I did on a road.**"

"Entered," said a girl of seventeen, in a book, in a column four lines high.

---

**And in the evening a mason of thirty-four years, who has never read a join in the nineteen stones in thirty-four years, walked up past the last of the eleven to the first of the eight, and stood in front of it, and said nothing for about four minutes, and about nine people watched her do it, and it is entered that she said nothing, and that four minutes is a long time to say nothing in front of a stone, and that nobody asked her what she was looking at.**

"You have been up here before," said a Hearthguard of forty-four.

"**Nine times in thirty-four years,**" said Ottoline Vask, "**and once to look at a man and eight times to look at a wall, and I have not read a join in any of them, and I am not going to start at the top of a hill in the wrong weather at the seventh hour of the evening with a man of sixty-one behind me who has read nothing in nineteen years except other men's sentences.**"

**And nothing was gained on the eighteenth day of the month after this one except nine years of paper and a number on a boy's slate. The undertaking is void and is on a nail and is on a river and no office has been told. Nine Elms' water is not received, the quarter is gone, the fourth line of a term sheet is in force, and a village of nine households has refused three times and has not refused a fourth time. A field of ninety-one acres out of a hundred and forty is not paid, and the cost column has eleven entries in one month, no figure on the end of any of them, and no twelfth. The chair of holder of the pull is vacant and the instrument says nobody and has said nobody five times. The Narrow Mark has been opened twice in a hundred and eight days and was not opened today. The measurement column has twenty-four readings, both of the last two eight and seven-eighths, two steps in an unnamed mason's hand on the eighteenth and the twenty-fourth day of the new month, one ruled line left, and the twenty-fifth reading has not been taken. The chapel stone is silent and is not repaired, the ground under the chapel is a scar, and the boards have not been lifted since the fortieth day of this flood. The form on the nail has four items, the price of the salt under them, and a fifth that is a question, and none of the five can close anything or say where a residue goes or say who is in a room. Adrian Vale is Stage 2, Passage holder, in the doing, and holds no mark of his own, no workway, no title, no land right, no class privilege and no seat, and performed no working today, and the whole of what he did on a nine-mile road was ask a docker to read him a date. **There have been twenty-nine things the wall has said in this city, one of them has been refused, and a day the wall says nothing on is a silence, and this is the seventeenth, and the pattern is seventeen for seventeen.** The black line in his right palm is one hundred and seven days old and has not moved one sixteenth of an inch.**

She came back down. **And the nineteenth day of the month after this one is tomorrow, and the weather on this road in this month is the weather the fifth thing on a nail in an arch was written for, and nobody has written anything on the wall, and about nine people slept in a village at a fork where a stone carries three pages that they did not open.**
