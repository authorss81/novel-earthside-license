# Chapter 0061 — What a Person With No Work Is For

On the thirty-sixth day of the new month, which was the sixty-first day of the flood and a Saturday, a man of thirty-two went out into a city of eleven hundred people with a sentence in his head that a woman of twenty-nine had put there on a stair two days before, and he could not say it in any of the four kitchens he went into, and the reason he could not say it turned out to be the finding of the day.

The sentence was twenty-four words long and it was hers and she had said it once and said it on a stair and had entered that she said it and not that she found it, and it was:

> **Go and ask the city what a person with no work is for, and take the answer, and do not offer anybody a seat.**

He had been holding it for two days. It is in a private book on the last page in a hand of thirty-two that is not a Veyran hand, and the entry beside it is four lines long and says that he read it eleven times, that he agreed with it the first time, and that agreeing with a sentence is not the same as being able to ask it.

---

**The first kitchen was above a chandlery and the woman who keeps it offered him a chair.**

He had been in the room about nine seconds. There were two other people in it and both of them were eating and neither of them had stopped, and the woman put a third cup on the table, and the cup was not for him, and she put it in the place where a guest's cup goes, which is a place at a table that means a person is answerable for what is said in the room for as long as the cup is there.

"I have news," Adrian said, in the local language, and he heard himself say it, and he heard what it was. It was an opening. A man from another world was about to be given a chair.

"No," said the woman, before he had finished, and she took the cup away again. "**Not in that chair. I have had a chair like that in my kitchen twice this month and both times I could not get anybody out of it.**"

He apologised for about nine words and went down the stairs, and it is entered that he apologised in the local language, and it is entered that he had not planned to apologise and did it anyway, and that in sixty days he had never once apologised to anybody in this city for being in a room.

---

**The second kitchen was in Silt Row and a boy of about fifteen offered him a day's work before he had finished sitting down.**

The boy had a barrow and a list of nine errands on a slate and the slate was the boy's own and the list was of things that had to be carried between two places that Adrian could not name in either language without help.

"Tomorrow," the boy said. "Four hours. There is a half."

There is a half. He did not know what that was. He understood that a half was a coin, or a fraction of something that a boy of fifteen earned by carrying, and that the boy was offering it to a man of thirty-two out of the plain decency of a city that feeds people who have not asked to be fed, and that no one had asked the boy whether he wanted a fourth pair of hands.

*No,* he said. *And thank you.*

"You are the wall man," the boy said. "You do not work."

"I do not have work," Adrian said, in the local language, and then in English, because he wanted to be certain of the word, and the boy looked at him with the specific disappointment of a fifteen-year-old who has been given the wrong word on purpose.

Then he found out what the room's word is, and it is not *work*, and it took him the rest of the morning.

It is **turning up.**

There is no word in the ordinary speech of this city for a person who has nothing to do. There are four, and they are all about a place: a person who is *idle* is a person who is not at a bench; a person who is *out of a crew* is a person the crew does not owe; a person who is *spare* is a person in a slack moment of a season. Adrian had been asking the city a question in a language it did not speak, and every room he went into had answered him in the nearest wrong language it had, and the first three answers had been a chair, a day's carrying, and a place in a queue.

---

**The third was at a chandler's counter on the market, and a docker offered him a place in a queue.**

This was the worst one and it was nearly the best one, and the reason it was nearly the best one is that it came closest to the truth.

The docker was about forty and had a barrow and knew him and did not want anything.

"There is a line at the tally-house on Thursdays," the docker said. "For the flood work. It is not work yet. It is a place in a line where the work will be when the work is."

"How long is the line?"

"Nine days I have been in it. There are four before me. **It is not work and it is not nothing and it is the only thing in this city that a man with no work can get.**"

That is true. Adrian wrote it down that night in six lines and one of them is that the man said it without being asked, and one of them is that the man did not say what it was *for*, and one of them is that Adrian did not ask, because asking is a thing he has been told to do to a city and not to a man in a market, and he could not work out at the time which of those two he was doing.

---

**The fourth kitchen is where he asked the question properly, and it was not a kitchen.**

Marek Osley is a salt factor of about forty-eight with a waxed tablet and he keeps the count the Lock's stores draw on, and he was standing in a converted salt store at about the second hour of the afternoon with four other people in it, and in about eleven minutes he taught Adrian Vale the difference between a work and a wage, which is the difference between the page of the seventeenth day of the new month and the page of the twenty-sixth day of this flood: **the first of those two asked for a seat, and the second one banned a seat, and neither of them said a word about a turning up.**

Osley put down the tablet.

"Are you asking me what the work is," he said, "**or are you asking me what it pays.**"

"Both."

"They are not the same and I am going to tell you why in four sentences and then you can decide which one you came for, and then I am going to go back to a count, because it is Saturday and the salt came up a third on the twenty-seventh and there is a season of it in that yard and nobody has counted it since Thursday.

"One. A work is a thing somebody expects you to turn up for. A wage is a number with your name under it, and the name is the part that is dangerous, and the number is the part that is easy.

"Two. This city has a bench and a bar, four beds and a shelf, a chain, a floor and a pail, and in every one of those places there is somebody who expects the holder to be there on an ordinary morning, and that is what a work is. **There is no such place for a wage of any kind at all except the roll of strangers, and the roll of strangers goes to Aurel in a wooden case four times a year.**

"Three. So a wage in this city is a thing that only a person with a name can hold, and a name in this city is a thing that can be found.

"Four. And that is why your page of the twenty-sixth is the longest page in the licence and why it is perfect, and why it is going to fail, and I have been waiting sixty days for somebody to work out which of those three sentences is the one that matters."

"I have worked out two of them," Adrian said.

"Which two."

"That the second is right and that I have known it for sixty days." He put his hand flat on the salt bin, which was a habit and not a decision. "**And the third one, this morning, in four kitchens, because I have been asking this city a question in a language it does not speak and it has been giving me answers in the nearest wrong language it has.**"

Osley looked at him for slightly longer than a man with a count to finish needs to.

"Say that again in about nine months," he said, "when you have had one bad winter of asking it properly, **and I want to hear whether you still say the word *work* in this city.**"

---

**And then the answer, and the answer is a number, and it is nineteen lines long, and it is not a list of jobs.**

Ottoline Vask has been in this city thirty-four years and has a bar she does not always carry. She came into the salt store at about the fourth hour of the afternoon and she was told what the question had been, and she did not ask him to say it again, and she took a piece of chalk and the flat of a sawn board and she wrote for about eleven minutes and what she wrote is on a board in a converted salt store and it has been read out in about two hundred and forty people's hearing, and it is this:

> **What a person in this city may be asked to turn up for.**
>
> 1. Any person in this city may be asked to turn up for one thing, on one morning.
> 2. The thing asked for is named before the person is asked, and it is named in words the person uses.
> 3. The notice is a night, and a night is enough for any work in this city.
> 4. The rate is the ordinary rate for that work, and the ordinary rate is written where the work is done.
> 5. A person asked is told what the work is before being told it is wanted.
> 6. Nobody is asked to turn up for a thing they have not been shown.
> 7. Nobody is sent to look for the person who did not turn up.
> 8. A person who does not turn up has not refused, and has not been paid, and owes nothing.
> 9. A person who turns up and is not paid is owed it, and the owing is entered.
> 10. Nobody goes to the door of a person who has not been paid.
> 11. No name is written at the top of anything on this board.
> 12. No name is written anywhere on it except the name of the person asked, and that name goes in a book and not on this board.
> 13. A person may refuse, and the refusal is entered.
> 14. A refusal does not stop the line being offered again on a different day.
> 15. A person may be asked once about a thing and may not be asked about it again for nine days.
> 16. Nobody may be asked to turn up for a thing that is not paid, until the paying is entered.
> 17. A person with no work may be asked to turn up for anything, may refuse, and the refusal goes nowhere.
> 18. **This is not a rota. It has no order in it and it has no beginning.**
> 19. **A person may be asked whether he is able to do a thing. The answer may be no. The answer is entered, and the answer is not a fault, and is not a wage, and does not go in the roll.**

Line eleven is the one that matters and it is the one Vask said in a yard on the twenty-second day of the new month and has said once.

"It is a widening and not a rota," she said, and put the chalk down. "**And I have said that sentence before in a yard and I have been right about it and I am saying it now about a different thing and I am still right about it, and a mason who is fifty can tell you that a widening is the only kind of page that is not somebody's list.**"

And then she said the second sentence of it, out loud, to about nine people in a converted salt store on the thirty-sixth day of the new month, and it is ten words and it has been in a sealed instrument in this city since the twenty-fifth day of the new month:

> **A working can be made cheaper without being made private.**

And then she said the rest of the sentence, which is the part that is new:

"And that is the second time anybody has said that sentence in this city. The first time was in a converted salt store on the twenty-fifth day of the new month about a witness, and it was about a door. **This one is about a person, and it is the first time in sixty days that the sentence has been useful to anybody who is not already standing in a gallery.**"

Nobody argued with her. It is entered that nobody argued with her and that about nine people understood what the sentence was for and that one of the nine was a salt factor of forty-eight who had gone back to his count.

---

**And the two beds in Silt Row are the test case, and they fail the first time they are used, and the page does not say what happens, and nothing in this city has ever said what happens.**

Delya Crane owns the building the loft is the top of and has held the ground floor on a rent-free lease for eleven years. Sesh Brack is her tenant. On the twenty-ninth day of the new month the two of them offered two beds, and the reason was entered as a reason and not a debt, and both of them said *I do not know what I am proposing* and then said it anyway, in that order, and neither of them had practised it, and a woman of twenty-nine made them say both halves.

Underneath the offer there were three clauses and the clauses are the reason the two beds failed on the first night they were used.

> **A household may say no. A household need not give a reason. A household is not asked a third time after a second, and is not entered anywhere.**

Every one of those is correct. Every one of those is in the book. Every one of those was written by a city that spent nine days learning that a list of houses with two strangers in each is a roll, and a roll goes to Aurel in a wooden case four times a year, and the fear of the word *list* in this city is a fear of persons.

**So on the night of the thirty-sixth day of the new month, one of the two beds was slept in and the other was not, and there was nothing anywhere in this city to be done about the bed that was not slept in.**

Delya Crane could not ask. Asking is the third thing the page forbids and the page is hers.

Sesh Brack could not write the name of the person who did not come down. Writing the name is what makes a person findable and she is a woman of about fifty who has been the most useful person in every room she has walked into in this city for eleven years and she understood the whole of the reason in about four seconds and she said it out loud to a mason with a bar, in a yard, in the plain language of a woman who is not a notary:

"**If I write his name on that bed I have got a list of one, and a list of one goes in a case four times a year, and a list of eleven is the thing we all said no to, and nobody has ever once said what happens to the number eleven when the case is empty.**"

"The page does not say," Adrian said.

"No," said Sesh Brack. "**And nothing in this city has ever said, and that is the first time in eleven years I have wanted to write something down and found out that writing it down is the dangerous part.**"

The finding was written down in about nine lines by a girl of seventeen who keeps the entries, in a column four lines high, and the width of the column is entered as a fact for the fourth time in that year, and it is the most uncomfortable page written in sixty days, and it reads:

> **The thirty-sixth day of the new month. The first Wayhouse bed of the two offered on the twenty-ninth was not slept in.**
>
> **The household cannot ask a second time and cannot enter a name, and these are the same rule and not two rules.**
>
> **The bed exists. No page says what a bed that nobody slept in is, and no page in this city has ever said.**
>
> **It is entered that the rule which protects a household from being asked again is the same rule which makes a person who did not turn up unfindable, and that no person in this room has found a way to have one without the other, and that this is not a fault and is a shape.**

And the girl of seventeen asked for one more line and got it, and it is her own, and it is nineteen words:

> **The rule is doing both jobs at once and only one of the two jobs is about the house.**

---

**And at about the sixth hour of the evening, in a yard under the lock chapel, in about two hundred and forty people, the first of the nineteen lines was read out, and it was a Saturday, and nobody had asked for it, and the yard had filled up because it is a yard and it is Saturday and there is salt in it.**

The first line is the first line and it is the shortest:

> **Any person in this city may be asked to turn up for one thing, on one morning.**

It was read by a woman of seventy who keeps that ground and who has the four words and a fifth in chalk on one side of a door and four generations of women behind them, and she read it the way she reads the binding, in the open, at the ordinary volume, and she read it twice, and the second time was not for the yard.

**About nine people in that yard understood that it was a widening. About two hundred and thirty thought it was a job.**

It is entered that both numbers are entered as what a man said and not as a count, neither above the other, that no hand was raised in that yard on either side of it, and that a woman of fifty-eight who was present said afterward, in eight words, that a widening does not look like a job, and that about nine people had to stand still to hear her.

**Nothing was gained. Adrian Vale is Stage 1, Witness, Unbound, on no roster of the licence and on no roster of the instrument of the twenty-second day of the new month, at his own request, in his own hand, with the date on it. He holds no mark of his own, no workway, no title, no land right, no class privilege and no seat. He performed no working today and he was offered a chair in the first kitchen and he did not take it. He is party to the trial licence, which is eleven days old today, and is given nothing by it. The main breach is closed and dressed and has never said a word. The ground under the chapel is a scar and is not repaired and the boards have not been lifted since the fortieth day of this flood. The black line in his right palm is sixty days old and has not moved one sixteenth of an inch. The review is in fourteen days, on a slate on a nail, with a date on it and no questions on it, and a room with no window and no door anybody has found is three knocks into being knocked on and nobody has to open anything for that to happen.**

**And a girl of sixteen and a woman of thirty-eight are on no roll at all, and neither has been asked, and the roll of strangers has not gone into a wooden case since the third week of the new month, and a woman of twenty-nine of the second list has two days of a thing worked out in her head and has not told anybody.**
