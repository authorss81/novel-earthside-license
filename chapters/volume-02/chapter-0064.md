# Chapter 0064 — The First Wayhouse

On the thirty-ninth day of the new month, which was the sixty-fourth day of the flood and a Tuesday, a bed and a bowl for anybody travelling was written down in a book in this city for the first time, and it was nearly destroyed in about nine minutes by a board, and it was saved by moving four names off a wall and onto a page, and about two hundred and forty people watched a thing be made legal by a woman with a bucket, and nobody in that yard said the word *legal* and about nine of them had it in their heads.

**Local law in this book means somebody in the room had to say no to something.**

Nobody in Saltwake invented the word *wayhouse*. It was in Halvard Ketch's custody before the flood: a customs clerk of that century kept a leaf about a house on a road two hundred years back that kept a bed and a bowl for anybody travelling, paid out of a levy on the road's trade, and the leaf is genuine and it is in a file that was not important for three hundred years and the man in a good coat called it a fraud in a yard on the second day of this flood and let a whole room believe the whole of it was fake, and it was not.

The rest of it was argued out on the thirty-ninth day of the new month in a house on the upper road with about nineteen people in it, and it is in the book in the words of the people in it, and the shape of it is this, and there are six things and five of them were refusals.

> **A Wayhouse is a bed and a bowl for anybody travelling.**
>
> **One. The bed and the bowl are paid for by a levy on the road's trade, at a rate the traders on that road have agreed among themselves. The rate is not set by a keeper and is not set by a house.**
>
> **Two. The household that keeps it is named on the page, with a date. Not a person. A household. A household is a door with people in it and it does not go to Aurel in a wooden case and it can be asked by anybody who walks past.**
>
> **Three. It is open to a list of nobody. There is no roll of who has slept there. There is no roll of who has eaten. There is a line for the salt and a line for the bowl and a line for the washing, and the three of them are about things.**
>
> **Four. The household may close it, for its own reasons, on a date written on the page. It is not asked why. It is not asked a second time.**
>
> **Five. It is not a Crown instrument, it is not a page anybody may carry to a building in Aurel, and a Wayhouse in one village is not a Wayhouse in another village and does not answer for one.**

And the sixth thing, which is the one the volume is about, and which was got from a man of thirty-two who has written six pages about what a person may not do and one sentence about what a person may do, and who did not want it and lost the argument:

> **Six. Nobody may be turned away for asking what it is. A person may ask any of the six things above at the door, on the step, in any weather, and be answered there, and being answered is not agreeing to anything, and nothing on this page obliges a person to sleep in it, or to pay, or to be described, or to be on anything.**

"The first five of those are a wayhouse," said Peth Lomas, fifty, a mason of this city and not of the crew, who is one of the four who read the join-line and who has not been thanked for that. "**The sixth one is the only reason it will still be here in four months, and it is in there because a man from another world said it without thinking and about nine people noticed and I said it out loud before he could take it back.**"

"I did not say it without thinking," Adrian Vale said.

"Then say why you said it," said Peth Lomas, "and it is in the book either way."

---

**And the housekeeper of about fifty wrote the four households that have agreed to the levy on a board in chalk, in a yard behind a chandlery, in the way this city does everything, and it was nearly the end of it.**

Her name is Ilsa Roke and she is Hettis Roke's aunt by marriage and she has kept a house on the upper road for nineteen years and she is fifty-one and she is not on any roll of anything and she is the keeper of the first Wayhouse, and she did it because it is what you do. A board in a yard, in chalk, with the four household names on it and the rate under them, so that anybody walking past can see what the road has agreed to and who agreed to it. She wrote it on the Monday. She had it out in the yard by ten on the Tuesday morning.

**It is exactly what a board in a yard is for. It is exactly how this city keeps everything. And a board anybody can read is a thing anybody can copy, and four householders with silver in them is a list of persons with something, and a list of persons with something is the shape that this city has spent sixty-two days learning to be afraid of, and the fear is not stupid and it is not about guilds.**

"I want to say the finding out loud before anybody stops me," said Wenna Cray, forty-one, of Silt Row, who has entered four statements of error in the history of this book and has withdrawn a belief and not a vote, "**because a board in a yard is not a lie and it is not a secret and it is not even clever. It is the ordinary way this city writes a thing down. And it is the third time in nine days that the ordinary way this city writes a thing down has turned into a list of people, and the first time was a word on a market board and the second time was a page with a name in it and nobody has been harmed by either of them yet, and this is the third and there is no reason to be harmed by this one either, and I would like somebody in this yard to say that out loud so that the person who is frightened of it can hear a person say it.**"

Nineteen people in a yard and about nine of them frightened and no hand went up and the thing she asked for was said, eventually, by a woman of fifty-eight with a barrow who cannot read, and it was said in the plain language of a person who is not a notary and has never wanted to be:

"**A board is a page somebody can hold up. A page is a page. If I cannot read it, somebody else can read it for me, and the somebody else writes it out, and now there are two of them.**"

---

**And the board came down in daylight, in about nine minutes, in front of about two hundred and forty people, and it was not a raid and it was not a scene and nobody was angry.**

Ilsa Roke came out with a bucket and a cloth and washed it, and before she washed it she read out what was on it, in the local language, at the ordinary volume, which is the way this city does things and is the reason it is hard to stop them.

Then the four names went into a book, in a page, with a date on it — **the thirty-ninth day of the new month, and the rate, and the four households, and the fact that the rate was agreed among the traders and not by any keeper and not by any office.** The chalk came off. The board is a clean board. Ilsa Roke has it in a lean-to and it is a board.

"You have taken a thing off a wall and put it in a book," said a man of about thirty in the yard, to a man from another world, "and about two hundred and forty people have just watched you do it, **and I do not think one of us knows what to call that.**"

"No," Adrian said. "**Nobody in this yard said the word *legal*. I have been listening for about four minutes and about nine of you have it in your heads and none of you has said it, and I would like that entered, because a thing becomes legal in a city when a person can be angry at it, and not one person in this yard can be angry at a page with a date on it, and that is the whole of why it is legal, and it took four names coming off a wall to find it out.**"

**And it is the second time in twenty-three days that a thing in this city has been moved off a wall onto a page. The first time was the sixteenth day of the new month, when a keeper of about seventy washed three lines off her own board in front of about a hundred and forty people and read out what came off before she took it off, and the duty that was inside those three lines went into a form on a nail as a question, where it could be refused.** About nine people in that yard on the thirty-ninth day of the new month understood why the first time mattered. The rest of them watched a board get washed.

---

**And the rate was argued out by three traders on a Monday morning in a yard behind a chandlery, and the argument is the instrument, and the last thing said in it is a refusal and it is the fourth refusal of the six.**

There were three of them on the board. A carter who came up the upper road eleven times a month with elm and lime. A clerk of the Brass Measure who passes it twice a month and does not stop. And a woman of thirty-four who keeps a stall by the wharf and whose name is not on anything, including the four names, because she sells no goods on the road and pays the levy on what the others pull.

"Two in a hundred," said the carter. "Two in a hundred on a road with nobody on it is a penny and a half and there is no such thing."

"There is such a thing," said the woman by the wharf, "and it is called a bed in February, and I have had two men in my stable in eleven years and both of them were paid for in something that was not money, and one of them is dead."

"That is not a rate."

"That is the rate," she said. "**Two in a hundred, and it is collected on the loading and not on the coming back, and it is collected whether anybody uses the house or not.**"

And the carter said the fourth refusal, out loud, and it is in the book in his words and it is fifteen words long:

> "**A levy on a thing nobody is using is a tax and I know that.**"

"Yes," said Peth Lomas, and it is entered that he agreed in about four seconds and that he did not try to make it a better sentence, and that his reason is in the book and is his own: **a page that says a levy stops when nobody sleeps in the house is a page that a road can end in four months by doing nothing, and a road that ends is a road nobody can be angry at and a keeper is left with a bowl.**"

**And so the fourth thing is not a kindness. It is the only one of the six that costs somebody who is not in the room, and it was argued by three people who are in the room, and one of them is a carter who loses a penny and a half a month and said so twice.**

---

**And Hettis Roke was asked about his aunt, in daylight, in a yard, in about two hundred and forty people, because a keeper who is married into a Hearthguard is a keeper a Hearthguard can be asked about, and this city was not going to build a bed and a bowl out of a family and call it local law.**

He said no to the form, on the standing rule, and yes to a yard, and his answer was short and it was about the standing and not about her:

"**She is my aunt by marriage and she is not on my roll and I am not hers, and if I ever hold a gate in this city and she is keeping a house on the road above the salt works, I will be able to be asked about her and so will you, and that is what a keeper is. If I could not be asked about her she would be a post with a person in it and I would rather be asked than be safe.**"

And then he said the other thing, which is in the book because he said it and nobody made him:

"**And I have not been thanked for the nineteen stones and I am not going to be thanked for my aunt, and I would like both of those entered so that nobody in nine years thinks they were the price of something.**"

---

**And the first person turned away from the door of the first Wayhouse was turned away for asking, and was answered at the step, and slept in a barn, and the whole of it is four lines in a book.**

A man of about forty came at about the eighth hour of the evening on the thirty-ninth day of the new month, alone, and asked whether there was a list, and what was on it, and who kept the house, and how long the bed was, and whether anybody had been turned away this week, and he asked all five questions standing on the step in about forty seconds.

Ilsa Roke answered all five at the step, in the plain language of a housekeeper, and the sixth thing on the page is the sixth thing on the page, and it worked on its first evening, in the weather, on a Tuesday, with nobody watching except a man from another world who had not been asked to watch.

He did not sleep there. He slept in a barn four hundred yards up the road and he left two pence in the bowl and he was not asked for them and they went on the line for the bowl.

"It is entered," said the girl of seventeen, "that the page worked on the first evening, and that a man asked five questions at a door and was answered, and that he did not sleep there, and that nobody knows what he will do next, and **that the page does not care and was never going to.**"

---

---

**And the first night it was open, nine people ate at it, and four of them were Veyrans who were not on the list of anybody, because there is no list, and two of the seventeen were refused a bed for a reason that is on the page, and the reason is the one thing in this city that is nobody's fault.**

Nine people ate. The bowl is a bowl and the salt is a double handful and the keeper counted nine and entered the nine against the line for the bowl, and did not enter a name, and it is entered that she wanted to and did not, and that she has kept a ledger with three lines in it and no names, and that this is the first ledger in this city in sixty-three days with nothing in it that can be carried away.

**Four of the nine had never been on any list of anything in their lives.** One of them is a bargeman who has slept in eleven different yards in nine years. One is a woman of about nineteen with a child asleep on her coat. One is a man who sells nothing and has nothing to sell. One is a Hearthguard's widow who has been at the back of a yard, at a distance, at every reading of every page in this city for two months, and who came to a bowl of salt because a bowl of salt is not a reading.

**And two of the seventeen were refused a bed, and Ilsa Roke refused them herself, at the door, at about the eleventh hour of the night, in four sentences, and the reason is on the page and the reason is correct.**

> "I will not put two of you in this house. Not because of the levy and not because of the four names and not because of anybody's roll. **Because there are eleven people in a rope store up this road with a list of what is owed for it on the wall, and every one of them is on that list as a thing that is owed a room, and you would be the twelfth and the thirteenth, and the list is the only thing keeping that room dry, and if two of you are on it then the room is a roll, and I have read what this city did to a roll.**"

**She was asked whether she would rather not refuse them, and she said yes, and she said it out loud in a yard on the following morning, and she said that a keeper who is asked to explain a refusal has already lost the fourth thing, and that she had wanted to say yes to a woman of forty-four and had worked out in the doorway, with the door open, that a Wayhouse that cannot say no is not a Wayhouse, it is a hostage arrangement, and she had been married for twenty-two years and knew exactly what she was looking at.**

**Ilsa Roke is not on a roll. Her name is on a page with a date and the page says a household, and a household is a door with people in it. She has a key. About nine people in this city know she has it and about two hundred and forty do not, and it is entered that the key is a thing and not a person and cannot be described quarterly.**

---

**And the man from another world wrote four lines in a private book that night, and the fourth is the reason the fourth line is in the book at all and the first three are the reason he has not slept properly in three days.**

> *I have built, in sixty-three days, a protection for eleven people that consists of a room, a list and a key.*
>
> *The list is the reason the room is dry and the key is the reason the list is not a roll.*
>
> *The key is the strongest thing I own and it is the only thing in this world I have ever been able to give away.*
>
> *I have written about six pages of what a person may not do and the whole of it comes down to a door and a piece of iron, and this is not an answer, and I am going to be asked about it in about four months by somebody who will not accept it.*

**Nothing was gained. The first Wayhouse exists, on a page, with a date, and it is not a Crown instrument and cannot be carried to a building nine hundred miles away, and about two hundred and forty people saw a thing be made legal by moving it from a wall to a page, and nobody in that yard said the word. The trial licence is fourteen days old. The fee of eleven ounces of silver is entered as owing with no date, and the Measure's receipt is still on a table in a yard and nobody has torn it up. The second list has not gone into a wooden case since the third week of the new month, and a girl of sixteen and a woman of thirty-eight are on no roll at all, and neither has been asked. The main breach is closed and dressed and has never said a word. The ground under the chapel is a scar and is not repaired. Adrian Vale is Stage 1, Witness, Unbound, on no roster, has performed no working, and holds no mark of his own. A room with no window and no door anybody has found is four knocks into being knocked on and nobody has to open anything for that to happen, and nobody has asked what to do about it, and the review is in eleven days with a date on it and no questions on it.**
