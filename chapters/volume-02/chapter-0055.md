# Chapter 0055 — The Man Who Did Not Say

On the thirtieth day of the new month, which was the fifty-fifth day of the flood and a Sunday, a Hearthguard of nineteen years was asked about eight graves behind an ordinary wall in front of about two hundred and forty people, because a form with four items on it has a fifth item and the fifth item is a question and not a duty, and a person who has never been asked is not a person who has refused, and nine years of not being asked came to an end between the sixth and the seventh hour of the morning. He answered, and the answer is that the names were written down once, in a book, in his grandfather's hand, in the year the ground was made, and that the stones stayed, and that the book went to Aurel in a wooden case four times a year on a river boat, the same case the roll of strangers goes in. By the fourth hour of the afternoon a city of about two hundred and forty people understood that a description is not a record of where a person is and is a removal with a column for where the person is, and by the ninth hour a woman of twenty-four had asked in a room who fills that column in, and a notary of fifty who has never been asked anything in this city answered in eighteen words, and a man from another world asked out loud whether anybody in Saltwake has ever seen a name come back out of one of those cases, and about two hundred and forty people understood that nobody has. Nothing about Owen Park was touched. Nothing about the light was touched. The chapel stone said nothing and has said nothing since the twenty-fifth day of this flood.

---

**The keeper's board is washed every Sunday morning and on the thirtieth day of the new month it was washed at about the seventh hour in front of about a hundred and forty people, and what went on afterwards was the four words and the fifth and nothing else on the keeper's side.**

Nyla Ferrow does it herself, with a bucket and a cloth, and she reads out what comes off before she takes it off, and that is a rule she set herself in the first week and has never broken. On the sixteenth day of the new month she washed three lines that were her own, and she did not wash off a sentence a man had written about himself, and the reason is in the book in her words: *washing a man's own writing off a board is a decision and not a keeping.* On the sixteenth Adrian Vale washed off his own sentence with the same cloth, after reading it aloud, and the book has his reason for having written it: he wrote it because a man asked for the true reason and he did not have the man's.

On the thirtieth she washed the two days' worth of tide notes off the slate face of the board and put the four words and the fifth back on at about the eighth hour, and the four words and the fifth are the binding, and they are a keeper's labour and not a lock's pattern, and there are about forty people in this city who can say them and the set of five words is not the set of four words under the near gate and the two are not interchangeable and the whole cost of this city's winter is that they cost the same thing.

Ottoline Vask's four items are on the other side of the door and were not washed and are not washed, and there is a reason for that on the page from the sixteenth day of the new month and the reason is hers: *if a name goes at the top of it I will read it out in a yard, and I have said that about a list before and I have been right about it.*

**And the fifth item of the form on the nail was not the four words and it was not a keeper's labour, and it was added on the forty-ninth day of this flood by a docker of fifty-eight, and it is this:**

> **That the person who reads the join-line in this city is told and asked, may say no, that the refusal is entered, that a refusal is asked again on a different day, and that it is a question and not a duty, and the day it becomes a duty somebody is to take it off the wall in daylight and say so.**

It is on a slate on a nail inside the arch of the lock chapel with a date on the bottom of it that says the fiftieth day of the new month, and it is eleven lines long, and it is the fifth item and the price of the salt is not one of the four because the price is written under them, and on the thirtieth day of the new month somebody in that yard read it out loud in daylight to a man standing nine feet away who had never heard it.

---

**He did not put his hand up, which was the phrase, and the phrase was wrong, and the man who used it was a woman of fifty-eight who has never used it wrongly before.**

Renna Ord stood in about two hundred and forty people at about the sixth hour and forty minutes of the morning of the thirtieth day of the new month and said: "Hettis Roke. **You have not put your hand up in nine years, so I am going to stop asking for a hand.** The fifth item says the person who reads the join-line is told and asked and may say no. There is a line of join under this chapel that has been dressed since the forty-ninth day and there are four of us who read it, and we are the four, and the slate says four people with no list and no name at the top of anything. Nyla Ferrow, who is seventy and keeps this floor and reads the line. Peth Lomas, who is fifty and a mason and is not of the crew and reads the number. Elsa Skell, who is forty-four and works the bottom field and reads the edge. And me. I cannot read. I read the join by standing on it twice a day for fifty-eight days and knowing whether it has moved, and that is not reading, and it is the only one of the four of us that anybody here has ever asked me how I do. I am reading that list out in a yard in daylight because it is a question and not a duty and I have been on a roll of nine for three days and I would like to be on one for this as well.

"**And you are on it. You have been on it since the forty-ninth and nobody asked you, and that is not a fault of yours and it is not a favour of mine, and I am asking you now in about two hundred and forty people's hearing whether you would rather not, and you may say no, and the no goes in the book, and I will ask you again on a different day.**"

And the yard did what about two hundred and forty people do, which is to go completely quiet, and there is a thing about that quiet which is in the book from the seventh day of the new month and it stands: **about eleven people in that yard did not know what to do with their hands for the whole of the reading at a graveside because a reading is not a ceremony, and it is the same silence and it is a different cause.**

Hettis Roke is forty-four years old. He is of the Hearthguard of the outer ward, nineteen years in. He read nineteen shapes aloud in a yard in front of about two hundred and ten people on the fortieth day of this flood. He cut a name upside down on a slab in the ordinary ground behind the ordinary wall on the sixteenth day of the new month because he is not a mason and nobody in that ground said so to him, and he said in advance that nobody was to come and say so to him and not to the man under it. He entered that he has watched about four hundred men be told a true thing in a yard and has never once seen a face do nothing on a true thing until a man in a good coat's face did nothing on the nineteenth day of the new month. He wrote the work in the fourth column of a stranger's rota wall on the twenty-fifth day and put a single number at the bottom of it and said nine seconds about why.

**He said no, and then he said the other thing, and the other thing took about forty seconds, and it is the longest answer he has given in nine years and it is in the book in his own words and it is in the language of the room and it is not a sentence anybody had prepared for.**

"No," he said. "**Put me down. I do not want to read a line under a chapel on a Sunday and I have never wanted to and I am not going to pretend to you at seven in the morning in front of this many people that I have been waiting to be asked.**

"That is the answer to your question and it is entered and you are to ask me again on a different day, and I will say no again on a different day for about four years, and then one day I will not, and I do not know which day that is and neither do you."

"Then that is the entry," said Renna Ord, and made it, and the girl of seventeen took it down and the width of the column is entered as a fact for the second time in that year.

**And then he said the other thing, and he said it to the yard and not to her, and about nine people noticed that he had changed the subject on purpose and about two hundred and thirty did not.**

"There is a thing I have not said in nine years and you have not asked me and I am going to say it, and I am not saying it because you asked, because you did not ask. **I am saying it because you have been reading a list out in a yard and a man is going to be on it, and there is a name on that list who has a thing in his ground, and if I am on that list then somebody will be able to find out what is in my ground, and I would rather say it in daylight in front of two hundred and forty people than have it found out in a room by a clerk.**"

---

**And what he said was that the eight names were written down once.**

"**My grandfather held this gate. That is on the board and it has been on the board since before I was in the guard. When a man holds a gate he is given a roll of the dead of that gate, and he gives it to the Crown twice a year, and he keeps a copy, and the Crown takes the copy and the stones stay.

"**The year the ground was made — the year there were no stones in it at all, they put them in as they went — my grandfather wrote all eight of them into a book. In his own hand. Their names and the year. And the stones went up the same month and eight of them got a stone and four of them got a name cut on the stone of a person they were buried with, because there was not enough money for eight stones and there was a good deal for four, and I have known that for nine years and I have never said it in a yard.**

"And the book went to Aurel. Twice a year, then four times a year, then it was a case. **A wooden case, on a river boat, and the case went down the river four times a year for nineteen years and my grandfather gave it to a clerk on a quay and the clerk wrote the count in a book and the count was eight, and the count was eight every year, and it was eight until the year the count stopped being written down, which was the year the gate was taken off the roll.**

"Nobody came. **That is the thing I have not said in nine years and it is the whole of it: nobody came. Not a clerk, not a stone-cutter, not a woman with a list. The stones stayed, because stones stay, and the names went, because names go.**"

The yard was in about two hundred and forty people and a man of forty-four had said a true thing in it and the second time in his life that a face in this city has done nothing, and this time his own face did not do nothing either, and Vask entered that.

---

**And the room worked it out in about one afternoon, and the working out is the most frightening thing that has happened in this city since the twenty-fifth day of this flood, and it is not frightening because it is new.**

Adrian Vale did the arithmetic out loud, on the wall of the lock yard, with a stick of chalk, at about the eleventh hour of the morning, and about forty people watched him do it, and then about two hundred and forty people came.

"**A description is not a record of where a person is. A description is a list of where a person is, with a column for it.** One column for the name. One for the year. One for the gate, or the yard, or the house, or the street. **And one that says where the person is, and it is the last column, and it is the widest one, and every office in the world that keeps a roll has that column, and it is the one that is always blank, and it is blank because the office is nine hundred miles away and has never once sent anybody to fill it in.**

"Now put the roll of strangers in it. **The seventeen. Six on one page and eleven on another, and the page about what they do not have is the longer page, and Adrian Vale is on it in his own words in two dates. It goes to a building on a river in Aurel four times a year in a wooden case on a river boat.**"

"And their names are on it," said Marta Vry, who is not on the page, "**and the column that says where they are says a building in a rope store over a chandlery, and the column that says who is asking for them says a man from another world whose name nobody in that building has ever heard of and who cannot be reached by any road in this world or any other.**"

"Yes," said Adrian Vale. "**And it has gone four times. And it will go again, and the fifth one goes out of this city before the fiftieth day of the new month.**"

---

**And a woman of twenty-four asked the question that reorganises the next two months, and she asked it in a converted salt store at about the third hour of the afternoon in front of about two hundred and forty people, and it is in the book in her own words, and it is four questions and none of them has a rhetorical trick in it.**

Priya Shah, twenty-four, of the first group, who has produced the sentence that made a thing possible in this city three times, and who took a Crown assessor of strangers' pen out of his hand on the twenty-fifth day of the new month and would not give it back until he had written four lines that fit his column, said:

"**One. What is the column for where the person is.**

"**Two. And who fills it in.**

"**Three. And what is there for a person who is in a column and not in a country.**

"**Four. And how many columns are there, and how many of them are blank, and is there anybody, anywhere, in either of the two worlds either side of that wall, whose whole work is to look at a column and notice that it is blank.**"

And a notary of fifty who has been in this city all her life and who has never once been asked for anything, and who has not been asked for anything by anybody in fifty-nine days and who is not doing any of it knowingly, answered in eighteen words.

"**The column is for the office. The office is nine hundred miles away. It has never sent anybody.**"

And then a man from another world asked the question that about two hundred and forty people understood and nobody could answer, and it is in the book because Vask entered it, and it is seventeen words:

"**Has anybody in this city ever seen a name come back out of one of those cases?**"

And about two hundred and forty people stood in a converted salt store on the thirtieth day of the new month and understood, all together and separately, that nobody has.

Not one. Not in fifty-five days. Not in nine years. Not in the nineteen years before that, when a case went down a river four times a year and a clerk on a quay wrote a count in a book and the count was eight.

**A docker of fifty-eight who cannot read had it read to her and said thirty-five words, and they are the ninth sentence she has said in this city that has been entered whole, and they are the shortest account of this winter that anybody in this city has given.**

"**That is the price. Salt goes up a third. The Measure wants a list. And the whole of what we are worth is written down four times a year by people who are not coming.**"

And the man in a good coat was in that room for the whole of the afternoon, and said nothing, and got forty-one cups of water out, and asked a docker to carry them and to keep four of them, and she kept four of them, and nobody has asked him what he thought and nobody is going to.

---

**And at about the ninth hour of the evening, in a yard under the chapel, a woman of twenty-four told a woman of sixty-one what a man had said about eight graves, and the woman of sixty-one said a number out loud, twice, and nobody has asked her why, and the yard emptied.**

Sabra Vint is sixty-one, of the upper road, in a house with a burnt lintel. She cannot read. She has kept a sheet recording the shapes cut on **nineteen stones** on the upper road every year for nine years, and eleven of the nineteen shapes are the same and eight of them are not, and the eight that are not are further down the road and in a different order, and a mason of thirty-four years has never been able to get her hands on them, and she writes the shapes down every year and has never once written down what she thinks the shapes mean.

She came down two hundred steps for the third time in nine years on the twenty-fifth day of the new month and said the number nineteen to a boy of nine, and the work — the shapes, the eight that are different, and the meaning, which nobody has written down — went onto a wall in a room above a market in two hands on the same day, in a cell in the fourth column of a rota, with a single number at the bottom of it. **The work in that cell is the nineteen stones. It is not a post. Anybody may pick it up, including a docker, including a mason, and including a woman of seventy who cannot read it.**

On the thirtieth day of the new month at about the ninth hour, Priya Shah came up two hundred steps to a house with a burnt lintel and told Sabra Vint, in nineteen sentences and in the local language slowly twice, what a Hearthguard of forty-four had said in a yard about eight stones with no names on them, and then said: "**The names went to Aurel. They were written down once. The stones stayed.**"

Sabra Vint sat down on her own step.

And she said: "**Nineteen.**"

And after about nine seconds she said it again: "**Nineteen.**"

And Priya Shah said: "Do you want to tell me why?"

And Sabra Vint said: "**No. Ask me on a different day and I will probably say no.**" And then she said: "**No — ask me on a different day and ask me properly, in a room, and I will say it, and I have been asked properly about my paper exactly twice in nine years and both times it was by somebody who wanted something.**"

And the yard under the chapel emptied between the ninth and the tenth hour of the evening of the thirtieth day of the new month, and about nine people stayed, and one of the nine was a man of forty-four who had said a true thing in front of two hundred and forty people for the first time in nine years, and he did not go up the road, and he has not gone up the road, and there is a name in a cell on a wall above a market with a number under it and neither of them is a question and both of them are now a question, and it is not his to answer, and a person who has never been asked is not a person who has refused, and he was asked this morning in daylight and he said no to a form and yes to a yard, and the two are entered separately because they are two different things.
