# Chapter 0072 — What Are They Called

On the forty-seventh day of the new month, which was the seventy-second day of the flood and a Wednesday, three days before a review, a man from another world went into four kitchens, a market, a boat-yard at Long Water and a converted salt store, and asked nine people the same question in nine different rooms, and got nine answers, and every one of the nine was true, and none of the nine was the answer.

**The answer was not found. The not-finding is entered, in nine lines, in the hand of a girl of seventeen, in a column four lines high, and the width of that column is entered as a fact for the ninth time in this year.**

---

**He started at about the fifth hour of the morning in a kitchen with a blue door in Silt Row, because the keeper of that kitchen has not spent a coin in nine years and had told him a thing about her mother in seventy-one words and he had not earned the right to ask her anything and asked her anyway, and she let him.**

Orla Specht, sixty-one, had been in four rooms between the forty-third and the forty-sixth day of the new month and had written out a second page nine times in her own hand, and could not read a word of it, and Adrian Vale had spent two days being quietly furious about a different thing and had come to her kitchen to be told something.

"**The eleventh question is on Saturday,**" he said. "**What are they called. Four words, on the back of a slate. Somebody in this city wrote it in a hand over somebody else's fingers, and I am going to be in a room with two hundred and forty people when it is read out, and I do not know what the answer is going to be, and I have never in seventy-two days walked into a room not knowing that.**"

"Then you have come to the right kitchen," said Orla Specht, "because I have not got one either and I have had nine days."

"What would you say it was."

"**The ones the door costs.**"

"That's five words."

"It's four if you say *door-costs*," said Orla Specht, "and I have been saying that phrase for four days in my own head and I did not know it was a phrase. **Go on. Ask the next one.**"

---

**Nine rooms, and the nine answers, and the shape they make.**

He wrote them on nine separate pieces of paper because the girl of seventeen asked him to, and then kept them apart, and the page in the book reads:

> **One, in a kitchen with a blue door, from a woman of sixty-one who cannot read and who has written out the second page nine times in her own hand: *the ones the door costs.* Five words, and she does not know it is a phrase.**
>
> **Two, in a market, from a docker of about forty with a barrow he was not pushing: *workers.*** He said it before he was asked and he was about nineteen seconds ahead of the question and he is a man who has not been asked a question in this city in a long time and does not know what to do with one.
>
> **Three, in a boat-yard at Long Water, from a Hearthguard of forty-four who had come to look at somebody else's boat: *the dead.*** And then he would not say anything else, and the not saying was entered, and the room found out two days later that he has eleven people in a book of a gate's dead who have no stone and that is what he was reaching for, and that he had answered the question about the people the door costs and had given back the only people he has ever been able to count.
>
> **Four, in a converted salt store, from a woman of twenty-four: *people the door costs.*** Which is the same as the first answer, in a different accent, and neither woman has met the other, and it is entered that two people in this city arrived at the same four words independently in nine hours and that nobody has worked out why and that it may be a coincidence and may not be.
>
> **Five, in a market, from a man of forty-four who keeps a stall: *my grandfather.*** He said it in the local language and it took about nine seconds to get across the room and it is the only answer in nine that is not a plural and it is the only one that is about a person who is not in this volume.
>
> **Six, on a stair over a chandlery, from a man of thirty-one whose right hand will not close: *strangers.*** He said it and then said the other half, which is that it is the word on the roll of strangers, and that anybody on that roll is described four times a year in a wooden case, and that if that is the answer then the answer to the eleventh question is the name of the machine that removes them.
>
> **Seven, in a salt store, from a notary of fifty: *I have four answers and I have not got one of them and I am not going to say a fifth thing in front of a page in case I have an opinion about it.*** And it is entered that she had four answers written on the back of a sheet and that she did not read them out, and that about nine people in that room wanted her to.
>
> **Eight, at the foot of the two hundred steps, from a docker of fifty-eight who cannot read: *I am not going to guess and you did not ask me to guess.*** She had not been asked. She had come down to the lock because it is Wednesday's work and the forty men are going under the near gate in ice in about four hours.
>
> **Nine, in a yard, from a woman of nineteen who marks chalk on a floor for nine weeks of wage that a chandler did not pay: *I do not know and I am sixteen and I am on no roll and I would like to keep it that way.*** And it is entered that she is the second of the two people in this city who are on no roll at all and who have been asked nothing this volume, and that this is the first time either of them has been asked anything by anybody, and that she answered in about four seconds and that about nine people understood the answer and about two hundred and forty had been in the room the day before and had not heard it.

---

**And the shape of the nine answers is the finding of the forty-seventh day of the new month, and it took about nine minutes, and it is a girl's, and she is seventeen.**

She did it in a yard, on a slate, in chalk, with the slate held flat on an upturned pail because the wind was coming off the river, and she did it in about nine minutes and then she asked the room a question, and the question is the whole of the chapter.

"**Every one of those nine is a word for a thing or a word for a group of things,**" she said. "**And a question that is asking for a word for a group of things is not a question a city is frightened of, and this one is, and so it is not a question about a word.**"

"**Then what is it a question about,**" said a man of about forty-four who keeps a stall.

"**The heading of a column.**"

Nobody said anything for about nine seconds. There are about two hundred and forty people in this city who cannot read the local language and about nine hundred who can, and the finding was made on a slate in chalk on a pail by a girl who is on no roll at all and is seventeen, and it went into the book in her own words and in a column four lines high, and the width of that column is a fact.

**She said it again, slower, because about nine people had not followed it the first time and she is seventeen and she had not been asked to explain anything in her life:**

"**A word is a thing. A heading is a place a thing can be found in.** And this question is not asking us what to call the people the door costs. **It is asking us what to write at the top of the column they go in.**

"**And you only ask somebody for a heading when you have already decided there is going to be a column.**"

A man of about forty-four who keeps a stall said a heading, and he said it before he had thought about it, and the room refused it in about nine seconds and the refusal is in the book in a girl's hand and it is thirty-five words long:

"**We are not writing anything at the top of anything today, and a heading is the top of a column, and a column is where a person goes so that a person can be found.**"

"Then that settles it," he said. "**There is no heading. We answer it on Saturday with no heading.**"

"It does not settle it," said the girl of seventeen. "**It settles the room. It does not settle the page, and on Saturday about two hundred and forty people are going to be handed a slate with a question on the back of it, and every one of them is going to hear a noun, because that is what a room does at two hundred and forty people, and about nine of them are going to hear a heading instead, and the difference between those two is going to be the whole of the review.**"

"How do you know which one it is going to be?"

"**Because I am holding the slate,**" she said.

---

**The shape of the answer, and not the answer, is in this city already. It is in a document this city wrote on the forty-first day of the new month, in four lines, in a keeper's hand, and it is on a slate on the nail under the chapel arch, and about two hundred and forty people have walked past it nine times a day for three days.**

Nineteen lines went on that slate on the forty-first day of the new month at about the eighth hour of the evening, and Nyla Ferrow read the nineteenth one out and then read the eleventh one out, and the eleventh was the one that did the work. They are the nineteen lines of what a person in this city may be asked to turn up for, written by a woman of thirty-four with a bar in about eleven minutes, and a widening and not a rota, and the seventeenth of them is a person's answer going nowhere.

The nineteenth line is the only one of the nineteen that is a question. It is four lines long, and it is the only question in this city that anybody can use, and it has been used once, for a man of thirty-five with a shoulder that cannot be set, and about nine people noticed.

> **A person may be asked whether he is able to do a thing.**
> **The answer may be no.**
> **The answer is entered, and the answer is not a fault, and is not a wage, and does not go in the roll.**

"**Read it again,**" said the girl of seventeen, "**and this time count the words that are about a person.**"

A mason of fifty read it again in a yard in the wrong weather.

"**There are none,**" said the girl of seventeen.

"Then say what that is."

"**That is the answer's shape,**" she said. "**That line says what may be done to a person in this city and it does not say what a person is called, and it is four lines long and it is the only four lines in this city that anybody can use, and it has been on a nail for three days.**"

---

**Nobody in that city could put the two together, and that is the honest end of the forty-seventh day of the new month, and it is entered, and it is nine lines long.**

Adrian Vale stood in the yard and said the true thing out loud, because it was the third day out of three and he had promised himself on a stair that he would say the true thing by Saturday, and about nine people heard him and two hundred and thirty were not in the room.

"**The city has the shape of the answer on a nail and has not got the answer, and I have not got the answer, and on Saturday two hundred and forty people are going to be asked it in a room, and nobody in this city is going to know what they are agreeing to when they agree with the person who gives it.**"

"Then what is on Saturday," said a man of about thirty-one with a hand that will not close.

"**A review,**" said Adrian Vale. "**Which is a room with a date on it and eleven questions and a slate, and about six of the thirty lines are questions this city asks when it wants to be able to close the door and four are questions it asks when it wants to keep one and one is about a person, and the one about a person is four words long and it was cut down from mine and it is the only one of the thirty that could take the licence off this city in an afternoon, and I cannot answer it, and I have been the only person in this city who could have answered it for seventy-two days and I did not ask it in the right words.**"

"And nobody else could?"

"**No. And that is not modesty. It is that a question about what to call a group of people is answered by the people, and there are eleven of them asleep in a rope store and six of them in a room over a market and not one of them has been asked.**"

Nadia Brandt, twenty-nine, who worked out a whole case with nothing in it in about an hour on a salt bin, and who told a woman of twenty-four and not the man, and who said the second half of it to him on the forty-fifth day of the new month, said the thing that got put in the book:

"**So the review will decide it, and about nine people in that room will know it is being decided, and about two hundred and thirty will believe it was decided this morning in a yard.**"

---

**At about the seventh hour of the evening, in a yard under the lock chapel, in about two hundred and forty people, a woman of fifty-eight who cannot read was asked what she thought the answer was.**

She had been asked nine times in seventy-two days and had answered five times and owes nothing and was going to be asked again. She said she would answer this one and she said it before anybody had put the question to her, which is a thing she has done twice in seventy-two days and both times it was on a slate.

"**What do you think it is,**" said the girl of seventeen.

Renna Ord thought about it for about nine seconds, and then she said four words, and **the four words are not a noun.**

> **Nobody has asked them.**

And about nine people in that yard understood all four of them at the same moment, and the room went quiet in a way that two hundred and forty people can make and almost nobody ever does, and the girl of seventeen wrote them down on the slate with a stub of chalk in her own hand, and the width of the column is entered as a fact for the tenth time in that year.

"**That is four words,**" said the girl of seventeen.

"Yes."

"**And it is not a noun, and it is the only answer anybody has given in nine rooms today that is not a noun, and it is true, and I do not understand why it is true.**"

"Because it is the eleventh question and it is the only one of the thirty about a person," said Renna Ord. "**And a person has never been asked, and a person who has never been asked is not a person who has refused, and I have watched this city learn that nine times and it has still not learned it.**"

"Do these go on the slate?"

"Yes."

The girl of seventeen turned the slate over. **The eleventh question is on the back of it, in the same hand, over the same fingers, in a chalk that is a week old.** And the four words went on the front of it, under the eleven questions, in a woman's own hand with a girl's hand holding the slate flat as a rest, and the girl of seventeen entered that she did not write them then either.

"It is on both sides," said a woman of seventy, looking at it. "**And the question is on the back and the answer is on the front, and it is going to be on a nail in a yard for three days, and nobody is going to turn it over, because nobody has to.**"

"Then that is the fault of the slate," said Adrian Vale, "and not of the answer."

"**It is the fault of a nail,**" said Nyla Ferrow. "**I have had that nail for fifty-one years.**"

Then she said the other half, and it is thirty-one words and it is in the book:

"**And I have put four hundred things on that nail in fifty-one years and none of them was an answer, and it turns out I have been saving up for this.**"

---

**Nothing was gained. Nine answers in nine rooms, nine of them true, and none of them the answer. The shape of the answer is in four lines on a slate on a nail and about two hundred and forty people have walked past it nine times a day for three days. The not-finding is entered in nine lines. Four words are on the front of a slate and the question they answer is on the back of it and the two are three days apart and about nine feet of weather apart and nobody has turned it over. Adrian Vale is Stage 1, Witness, Unbound, on no roster of the licence and on no roster of the instrument, holds no mark of his own, no workway, no title, no land right, no class privilege and no seat, and is party to a trial licence twenty-two days old and is given nothing by it. He performed no working today. The black line in his right palm is seventy-one days old and has not moved one sixteenth of an inch. The main breach is closed, dressed, measured and silent and has never said a word, and there is one ruled line left in the column and it is not filled and it is not going to be filled. The chapel stone is silent and is not repaired. The ground under the chapel is a scar, is not repaired, and the boards have not been lifted since the fortieth day of this flood. The form on the nail has four items with the price of the salt written under them and a fifth item that is a question and not a duty, and none of the five can close anything or say where a residue will go or say who is in a room. A room with no window and no door anybody has found is three knocks and four knocks into being knocked on, both entered and neither deleted, and the second page says in nineteen lines that this city is not asking about it and why. Nine Elms' water is not received. The fee of eleven ounces of silver is entered as owing with no date for the thirteenth time. March is thirteen days off, and the season of a field four miles down the Reach road is still unpaid, and the whole of the Reed Reach's standing charge is on a man of thirty-two who is on no roster of anything.**
