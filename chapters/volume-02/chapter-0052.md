# Chapter 0052 — Salt

On the twenty-seventh day of the new month, which was the fifty-second day of the flood and a Thursday, the price of a form that watches a hole and cannot mend it went up a third, and the form has four items on it and a price written under them, and the price is salt. A keeper of about seventy has a bucket and eleven weeks in it and a winter market. The second of the Brass Measure offered the salt anyway, on credit, against the crew's shift list, and it was refused in a yard in about eleven words by a rule that has been in the book since the fortieth day of the flood, and the rule is that a wage drawn from another list is not a wage. The salt was bought with labour taken out of the crew's shift, and in the doing of it a room of about two hundred and forty people found out that the four people who change the salt under the chapel arch twice a day are the four people who sign the fresh inspection before every opening of the only door in this world, and that the nine named persons who may stop the working may not stop either of those two things. Nobody had asked the four, and one of the four is seventy and did not mention it because nobody had asked her whether she would rather not. And at about the third hour of the afternoon a docker's apprentice of about twenty went down on the rail on the two hundred steps, on ice, and could not get up for about nine minutes, and the four had to choose between the man on the ice and the seam at the bottom, and no instrument in this world has a clause about that, and the choice was entered afterwards in nine lines by a girl of seventeen in her own hand as what a person chose and not as what was right.

---

**The market on the river sells four things in winter and two of them are salt.**

That is the finding of the twenty-seventh day of the new month and it is in the book in the words of a woman of thirty-four who has been in this city thirty-four years and who has never once entered a price without saying where it came from. Ottoline Vask does not sell salt. She has a bar and she reads join-lines and she wrote two sentences in the second week of this flood that this whole volume is built out of, and the third one this morning was said on a wharf in the middle of an argument and is sixty-eight words long:

"**It did not go up a third because there is less of it. It went up a third because everybody in four miles wants it at once and the field went under the water on the second day and the winter came early, and a price is not a fact about a thing, it is a fact about how many people want the thing on the same morning.**"

"And it will go up again before March," she said, "**because March is when a field is decided and everybody who has been waiting nine days finds out on the same day.**"

---

**The bucket is under the arch of the lock chapel and it is a salt bucket and its price is on a slate on a nail, written under the four items and not as a fifth of them.**

The slate has four items and a keeper's hand and the word *form* written beside a name, and under the four of them is **the price of the salt**, and the price is a double handful twice a day, and it has cost the keeper of the chapel eleven weeks' worth of her own ration since the forty-ninth day, which is a figure entered in the book and not a complaint, because a keeper who complains about a form is a keeper who has made it a favour, and a form with no price on it is a favour and a favour is a thing that stops.

Nyla Ferrow is seventy. She keeps the lock chapel. Four generations of women in her family have kept it. On the sixteenth day of the new month she washed three lines off her own board in daylight in front of about a hundred and forty people and read out what came off before she took it off, and she did not wash off a sentence a man had written about himself, and the reason is in the book in her words: *washing a man's own writing off a board is a decision and not a keeping.*

On the twenty-seventh day of the new month she was in the yard at about the sixth hour of the morning with a scoop and a pail, and Adrian Vale was there, because he had been asked to be there and had not been asked anything else.

"How long is the bucket for," he said.

"Eleven weeks," said Nyla Ferrow. "**It was sixteen on Tuesday. It went down four days' worth in one morning because a man from the salt works on the upper road took a cartload of it at the fourth hour and paid for it and I have the entry.**"

"Did you sell it?"

"I have never sold salt in this yard. **Somebody asked me to and I said no and he went and bought it at the works, which is what the works are for, and the price I have is the retail and the price he paid is the works and there is a third price between them and it is called somebody's winter.**"

---

**And then the Brass Measure came, and it came because the Brass Measure is very good at this and not because anybody in it is a villain.**

Ottavia Reek is second of the Brass Measure. She offered Jonah Pike forty elms, twenty of bar, a set of shoring oak and six weeks of rope in the second week of this flood for priority on every working at the lock, and it was refused in one sentence in a yard, and she came back in the sixth week with four sheets and a fourth term, and the fourth term was that the dockers' labour be drawn from the Brass Measure's own list, entered as a standing order and not an agreement, and a docker of fifty-eight who cannot read had it read aloud and said nine words: *that is a lock that a guild can close.*

She came back a third time on the twenty-seventh day of the new month, into a yard of about two hundred and forty people at about the seventh hour of the morning, with one sheet, and it was the best offer anybody in Saltwake has made anybody since the flood, and it is in the book in her own words and it is a factor with a quarter saying a true thing about a bad winter.

"**Thirty tons of salt, this week, at the works, on the Measure's account, at the retail and not the works price, which means the Measure eats about a third of it and I can tell you exactly why I am offering.**"

"Why," said Renna Ord, who had heard the whole of it and had not moved.

"Because the rail on your two hundred steps is going to come off and somebody in this room knows it, and because timber in this weather is the second thing that has gone up a third, and because I have nine elms in a yard behind the salt works and four of them have been idle since the second day of the flood and I am not able to keep paying them for a wall that is dressed and a gate that does not step." She put the sheet down on a trestle. "**And the price is the crew. Not a wage. A drawing. The day and flood crews' labour comes off the Measure's list instead of off the city's roll, and the city stops owing forty men and the Measure starts owing forty men, and the men are the same men on the same days doing the same work under the same near gate, and there is a clerk in this instead of a woman with a stick.**"

And about two hundred and forty people were in that yard and about nine of them wanted it, and it is entered that nine wanted it, and it is entered as a show of wanting and not as a count, and Vail entered it in that form for the ninth time in her career and has stopped apologising for the form.

Renna Ord said the eleven words. She has said them before, on the forty-sixth day of this flood, under the near gate, to about forty-one people, and they are:

"**A thing nobody has asked for cannot be owed, and a thing that cannot be owed cannot be paid, and a thing that cannot be paid is a tax. A wage off another list is not a wage. It is a favour with a handle on it.**"

"Somebody has to draw them," said a man in the crowd.

"**Somebody has to draw them and it does not have to be him,**" said Renna Ord, "and that is the whole sentence and I have said it twice in this city and I am not saying it a third time for a woman with a good offer."

And Ottavia Reek took her sheet off the trestle, and said: "Then the rail is your rail," and went, and it is entered that she said it without heat and that two people in that yard thought she had been refused and about nine thought she had not been.

---

**And the salt was bought with labour, and the labour came out of the crew's shift, and that is how a room of about two hundred and forty people found out something that had been true for four days without anybody noticing.**

The boy's plan was simple and it took about eleven minutes to write. Hesk, the foreman of the day crew, who said the four words under the near gate for the first time in his life at the sixth hour of the morning of the forty-sixth day before anybody had asked him for anything and then voted against the same thing fourteen hours later, put it in forty-nine words: the crew's shift is a day of work, and the salt is a day of work, and a day of work for the wall can be a day of work for the salt, and it is the same forty men either way and it is not a new cost.

Which is true. And it is what Renna Ord calls a widening and not a rota, and Vask wrote the sentence on the sixteenth day of the new month over the ninth page: *it is a widening and not a rota, and if a name goes at the top of it I will read it out in a yard, and I have said that about a list before and I have been right about it.*

The four who change the salt twice a day under the chapel arch are named on the slate, because the slate says four people and no list and no name at the top of anything. They are the four who go down the walk-round with a gauge before every tide, and they have been the same four since the forty-ninth day because a form of watching a hole and an inspection of a door happen to want the same people in the same weather on the same two hundred steps, and **nobody had asked whether they were the same people.**

The four are Nyla Ferrow, seventy. Peth Lomas, fifty, a mason, who is not of the crew. Elsa Skell, forty-four, of Corrow, who works the bottom field and has not spoken to Adrian since the eighteenth day of the new month. And a woman of fifty-eight who cannot read, whose name is on the roll of the day crew in her own hand and who is not of the crew either and is on the roll of nobody.

"There is the finding," said Sera Vail, in about two hundred and forty people, at about the ninth hour of the morning, reading her own book. "**One set of four people now holds the licence's inspection and the scar's watching at the same time, in the same weather, on the same walk. And the nine named persons who may stop the working may not stop the salt and may not stop the inspection, because the salt is a form and the inspection is in the licence, and the nine are in neither.**"

"And the four can't stop the inspection either," said Wenna Cray, of Silt Row, forty-one. "**The four sign it. Four people who sign a thing are not people who can be asked not to sign it, because then it is not a signature, it is a favour.**"

"So the salt and the door have the same hands," said Adrian Vale.

"They have had the same hands for four days," said Vask, "and the price of the salt went up a third yesterday and I have been trying since the sixth hour of this morning to work out whether that is a coincidence or a cost, **and it is a cost, and it is a cost that nobody is going to be able to enter in a column because the column is the salt and the salt is what went up.**"

And the sentence that the room took away was Renna Ord's, and she said it in nineteen words, and she said it twice because about forty people did not have it the first time:

"**The four who hold the door must not be the four who need the door. And now they are.**"

---

**And nobody asked the four, and the one who is seventy is the reason it was not asked, and the reason is on the page in her own words and she gave it in a doorway in about nineteen seconds and she did not give it in the yard.**

"You did not tell us you were four of the four," said Adrian Vale, in a doorway under the chapel arch, on the twenty-seventh day of the new month, at about the tenth hour of the morning.

"I have been four of the four since the fortieth day and I was asked for the salt on the forty-ninth and I said yes and nobody asked me the other thing and I did not offer it." Nyla Ferrow had the pail in her hand and she was about to go down two hundred steps with it. "**I am seventy. I have kept a floor for fifty-one years. I have never once in my life asked a person whether they would rather not, because in this yard asking a person whether they would rather not is how you find out that they are tired, and then you have to do something about it, and there is nothing in this yard to do.**"

"Would you rather not."

"I would rather be four of the four," she said. "**I would rather be asked. That is the whole of it and I have had fifty-one years of people not asking me and I have got very good at it.**"

And she went down the two hundred steps with a pail of salt, and about four feet down it she stopped and said, without turning round: "**The rail is going. I have told the man who owns the rail twice and he is a good man and he has not got the money. Do not wait for me to say it a third time.**"

---

**And the rail went on ice, at about the third hour of the afternoon, and it was half-rotten, and it had been half-rotten for years, and the man who owns it is a good man and has not got the money.**

The walk-round is two hundred steps down a river wall with a low parapet and a rail. It has been in the book since the first week. The rail is oak and it is fastened at the top and at the bottom and it is fastened in the middle, and the middle fastening is into a post that is not rotted, and the rail itself is rotted through for about four feet at about the fortieth step, and nobody has put a hand on it in the wrong weather with a body against it until the twenty-seventh day of the new month.

The apprentice is called Tam Ockley and he is twenty and he is on the flood crew's second page and he is not a name anybody in a yard has said out loud. He had a barrow of chalk and a straight edge for the chalk depths and ice is not a thing a straight edge works on, and the four had decided between themselves, on the way up, that the depths would be taken from the top step where the cut is in the boarding, and the apprentice went down anyway because he is twenty and there was a barrow.

He went down about four feet, on ice, with the barrow behind him, and the rail went where the rail was, and he went down with it and took the parapet with his left side and his elbow opened a hole in his sleeve and nothing broke and he could not get up for about nine minutes.

It was about nine minutes. It is entered as about nine minutes. Renna Ord and Peth Lomas were the two hundred steps above him and Elsa Skell was on the parapet and the fourth of the four was a woman of fifty-eight who cannot read and who went down it **on her hands and knees with a barrow tied to her back**, which nobody asked her to do and which is entered as what she did and not as what was right.

And the four of them, at the top of two hundred steps, in the wrong weather, on the twenty-seventh day of the new month, at about the third hour of the afternoon, had to choose, and there was no clause, and the choice was this:

The instrument says a fresh inspection signed by four people before every opening in whatever weather there is. **Not the tide's weather. The weather that is.** And the four go down the walk-round to the cap of the old landing to look at the seam in the outer wall of the outer channel, and it is two hundred steps down and the rail is where the apprentice is, and there is a man on the ice eleven steps below the parapet with a hole in his sleeve.

They could send somebody else down. There was nobody else. They could send one of the four down past him, which meant walking on the ice, which meant the rail was not the only thing on that walk that could be relied on.

What they did is in nine lines in the day book in the hand of the girl of seventeen, and it is the first thing in the history of this book that has been entered as **what a person chose and not as what was right**, and Vask read it out in a yard on the twenty-ninth day of the new month and entered that she had not known a book could carry that, and that she had been a public record for about nineteen minutes in the sixth week of this flood and had entered a great many things she had believed were right.

> **At the third hour of the afternoon of the twenty-seventh day of the new month, four people stood at the top of two hundred steps in ice.**
>
> **The apprentice was down and could not rise and had been down about four minutes.**
>
> **The inspection of the seam could not be signed by four people unless four people went down the two hundred steps, and there was nobody else, and the rail was where the man was.**
>
> **Nyla Ferrow, seventy, said she would go. Peth Lomas said she would not and that the two of them were the same answer and that the argument was waste. Renna Ord said the inspection would not be signed and the walk would be done without it, and that the door would therefore not open today, and that there was no opening today.**
>
> **Elsa Skell said: *then it is not signed and I am going down to him and you can put that in the wrong column.* And she went down on the rail.**
>
> **The apprentice was got up at about the ninth minute by four people. The walk-round was not completed. The door was not opened. The chalk depths were taken from the top step with a straight edge and not with a barrow.**

And the paragraph under it, which is the girl of seventeen's and which she read out herself:

> **I am seventeen and I take these down and I have put what happened in a book that people will argue about for four months, and I want it entered that nobody did a wrong thing and that is not the same as saying that nothing went wrong, and I do not know the word for the difference and I have looked for one.**

---

**And the rail was condemned at about the sixth hour of the evening, and the mending of it is a cost, and the cost went on the column for the eighth time in one month, and there is still no figure on the end of it.**

Vask's sentence about a dressed joint is on the page from the forty-ninth day: *a closed join does not get narrower, it gets dressed.* It is about stone and it is the only sentence anybody in this city has that is true about wood as well, and she said so and was not thanked for it.

The timber is four weeks away. The Measure has it. The Measure's price is the crew's list.

And a man of nineteen who has had eight watches in three weeks, and who wrote *works* in the last column of the tide table on the forty-third day with a stick of chalk, small, with the k pressed too hard, and who then put the chalk down on a ledge rather than write the same word a second time, stood at the top of the two hundred steps at about the seventh hour of the evening and looked down at the rail for about four minutes and said one sentence, and it is in the book in his own words, and it is thirty-seven words:

"**I have been down these steps about thirty times since the second day of the flood and I have put my hand on that rail every one of them and I never once asked who cut it.**"

---

**And on the twenty-seventh day of the new month the word on the market board was on three boards, and the man who wrote it counted them, and he had not told anybody he had written it.**

The board is on the market wall by the wharf, in a mason's chalk, in a hand that is not a Veyran hand, and the word is *player*, and it went up on the ninth day of this flood in the second week, and about forty people have copied it since, and Adrian Vale has known it was there for six weeks and has not said one word about it in public, and on the twenty-seventh day of the new month, at about the eighth hour of the evening, on his way back from the condemned rail, he stopped and counted, and it was three boards, and one of the three had the word twice on it in two different hands, and about nine feet of market had it in chalk and one shop had it painted.

He stood in front of it for about eleven minutes.

And then he went up to the room above the market and got out the notebook and turned back nine days to the ninth day of the flood, and it is in his own hand, in English, in a line he has never read since he wrote it, and it says: *they think it is a game*.

And under it, in the same hand, in smaller letters, because he was writing fast and because he was describing other people's error and not making an accusation, he had written: *they think it is a game — the Veyrans, not the ones who came with me.*

Nobody has read it. It is a private book. It is the only sentence in the history of this city that is going to be read out loud in front of about two hundred and forty people, and it is going to be read out on the twenty-ninth day of the new month, and the man who wrote it is going to be asked to say it again in a yard, and about nine people are going to understand that it is not a slip.

It is not a slip. He has had forty-one days.
