# Chapter 0054 — The Word He Wrote Down

On the twenty-ninth day of the new month, which was the fifty-fourth day of the flood and a Saturday, a man of twenty-seven from the second list proposed in about four minutes that the seventeen be split among eleven households for the winter, two to a house, and it was the first thing one of the seventeen had proposed in fifty-four days and it was a good idea, and about four people in that room were frightened by how good it was. A widow who held a twelve-month lease with two conditions that were never met offered two beds and the reason was a man who is under a board behind an ordinary wall, and the reason was entered as a reason and not as a debt. And at about the fourth hour of the afternoon, in a yard under a chapel, in front of about two hundred and forty people, a man from another world said out loud, in the local language, that he wrote the word *player* on a market board in the second week of this flood, in his own hand, in a language almost nobody in that yard could read, and that about forty people have copied it since and that it is on three boards and in about nineteen mouths. A woman of forty-one of Silt Row entered the fourth statement of error in the history of the book, four sentences, against a person, naming him, and every one of them is correct. A docker of fifty-eight who cannot read said the shortest sentence of the day and then said four more words, and the four more words are the day. And a woman of twenty-four got up on a trestle with a stick of chalk and wrote a longer true thing over a shorter one, in the market, where the shorter one was, in front of about two hundred and forty people, and the longer one is nineteen words long and anybody may read it.

---

**Gareth Mbeki put it in four minutes and he did it in a room with the door shut and he did it to about nine people and he did not want it to be a speech.**

He is twenty-seven and he is of the second list and he came out of a chapel floor on the twenty-fifth day of this flood and he has not asked for anything in twenty-nine days. On the twenty-ninth day of the new month at about the tenth hour of the morning he stood up in the loft and said: "Right. **Eleven houses.** Not the Measure's, not the Concord's, ours. Ask eleven households. Two to a house, one of us and one of ours is not how it works — one of us, and a household takes the other. Beds, a kitchen, a floor, a door that shuts. Not money. Nobody pays anybody. A household that says no does not have to give a reason and does not go on a page, and if it says no twice we do not ask a third time."

Nobody spoke for about nine seconds.

"That is good," said Nadia Brandt, twenty-nine, of the second list. "**That is genuinely good, Gareth.**"

"I know that is good," said Gareth Mbeki. "I have been in this world twenty-nine days and I have not had a good idea once, and I have been ashamed of it every day, and I have been counting the flour."

---

**And about four people in that room were frightened by it, and one of them was Mina Okoye, and she was the one who said so, and she said it in eleven words, and she was wrong about one of them and right about three.**

"I am frightened of it," she said. "**Two of us in a house with a Veyran family who we have not met.** Not of the houses. Of what it means on the sixth week, when the road is closed and there is no way out of this city and nobody has gone anywhere since the twenty-fifth day."

"Then we go back to a rope store with a list of what is owed on the wall," said Kirsty Lund, sixteen, and did not sound bitter, which made it worse.

"Yes," said Mina Okoye. "**And I would like it entered that I said yes to that and that I am the one with the room over the salt weavers' chapel with a shelf of instruments in it, and I would like it entered that I said yes quickly and that quick is not the same as right and I have been wrong ninety times and I do not round the figure.**"

"You have been wrong ninety times about bones," said Renna Ord, who was in the room, in English, and got a laugh, and did not look like a woman who had made a joke.

"And you have been wrong about a gauge for nine days and you were right," said Mina Okoye, "**so neither of us is going to be the judge and that is why we are arguing in a room with the door shut.**"

Adrian Vale wrote it in the notebook that night and the sentence is in the book in his own hand because he put it into the fourth volume himself in the morning: *it is what a person says when they have stopped believing a door home exists. Being settled is the shape of giving up wearing, and this city has watched two people do that and one of them is under a board.*

**He did not say that sentence in the room, and the reason he did not is entered, and the reason is that it is a sentence about a person's belief and he has no standing to have it.**

---

**And the household that offered first was the household that owed an answer, and the reason it offered was a man who is dead, and nobody in that room made them say so twice.**

Delya Crane holds the ground floor of a building on Silt Row and has held it for two years, and she has never charged one of the seventeen a thing for bread or a floor or a wash-tub in fifty-nine days, and she has an entry in the Concord's book about a twelve-month lease that Owen Park signed in his second week here and never met two conditions of, and one of the conditions is a door and one of them is a season's work, and the lease carried two conditions and neither was met and neither ever will be, and both women are owed an answer and have been owed one for thirty-four days.

Sesh Brack is the other one. She is a needlewoman and she is Delya Crane's sister and the two of them have kept that floor for nine years.

They came up at about the second hour of the afternoon on the twenty-ninth day of the new month, together, without being asked, and they had thought about it, and Delya Crane said the first thing:

"**Two beds. Ours, not the room over the shop, ours. And we are not asking the eleven to be asked and we are not putting you on anything.**

"And I am going to say the reason and you are not to thank me for it, because the reason is a man called Owen Park who slept in that room in the third week of this flood and put his hand on my doorframe on the way past and asked me whether the plaster came off in eleven days, and he died on the thirty-eighth day of this flood under a board behind an ordinary wall and he is the seventh name I have had in that building since the flood and I have not slept properly since the fifth week."

Hesper Saye, fifty-eight, who was standing in that room because she is asked about everything a person says about their own body, said: "**Do you understand what you are proposing.**"

"No," said Delya Crane.

"Say that again and then say it anyway," said Hesper Saye. "**Both. In that order. And then nobody can come at you in four months with the fact that you did not know.**"

And Delya Crane said: "I do not know what I am proposing. I am proposing it anyway," and Sesh Brack said the same four words after her, in the same order, and neither of them had practised it, and Saye entered that neither of them had practised it and that was the only evidence in the room.

**And the two beds were accepted, and the eleven-household plan was not, and the difference is nine lines and it is the most important page written in this city so far.**

> **Two beds are accepted at the house of Delya Crane and Sesh Brack, Silt Row, from the first hour of the third hour of the afternoon of the twenty-ninth day of the new month, and the names of the two householders are on the page with the date, and the two people going into them are named with the date, and the page can be read by anybody.**
>
> **The plan of eleven households is not accepted. The reason is: eleven houses with two strangers in each is a list, and a list of houses is a roll, and a roll goes to a building in Aurel four times a year in a wooden case on a river boat, and the person who wrote that rule on the twenty-sixth day of this flood did not write it about houses and would not have cared.**
>
> **A household may say no. A household that says no is not asked a reason. A household that says no twice is not asked a third time. A household that says no is not entered anywhere.**

Gareth Mbeki read it and said: "That is better than mine."

"It is smaller than yours," said Priya Shah, twenty-four, in English, "and it is on a page, and yours was a good idea and I want it entered that it was a good idea, because in four months somebody is going to read that we turned down a good idea and I was in the room."

---

**And at about the fourth hour of the afternoon, in the yard under the lock chapel, in front of about two hundred and forty people, a man from another world said the thing he had said nine times in fifty-four days and then said the rest of it.**

He had said it nine times. In the market, in a yard, in a hall, in a room with a burnt lintel, in a converted salt store, always with the same shape: *nobody who comes through that wall is a special kind of person, they are people, and they are in trouble, and the word for them in my own language is a word for somebody who is playing.* He had said it in a market on the ninth day of this flood to six Veyrans and they had repeated the word back at him and he had told them it was their error.

He had never once said where the word came from.

He got up on nothing. There was no trestle. He stood in about two hundred and forty people at about the fourth hour of the afternoon on the twenty-ninth day of the new month and said, in the local language, and slowly, and a docker of fifty-eight translated the second half of it for the people who had not followed the first:

"**That is my word. *Player.* It is a word in my language and it means somebody playing. I put it on that board on the ninth day of this flood in my own hand in a language that about four people in this city can read, and I told six men in this market that it was their error when they said it back to me.

**Nobody in this city wrote it down. I did not put it in a book. I put it in chalk, on a board, at eye height, on a market wall, and about forty people have copied it since, and it is on three boards and it is painted on a shop, and there is about nine feet of that market in my handwriting now, and about nineteen people say it, and every single one of them learned it from a person who learned it from a person who learned it from me.**"

He stopped there. About two hundred and forty people were in that yard and the yard is a bad place to stop in and he stopped in it.

And Renna Ord said, from nine feet away, in English, and then in the local language: "**Then take it down.**"

"I cannot take it down."

"**You can take it down. You are the man who wrote it. I have watched you say no three times to a thing that worked and I have never once seen a man in this city take down a thing he had made.**"

"There are about nineteen copies and about nine feet of chalk and a shop with it painted, and a woman of nineteen painted it because I asked her what the word meant and she asked me to write it for her so she would not get it wrong," said Adrian Vale, "**and I am not going to go round this city with a cloth. That is what happened to a keeper's board in the second week of the new month and it was a decision and not a keeping and it was correct, and this is not the same thing and I am not going to pretend it is.**"

"Then say over it," said Renna Ord.

He looked at her.

"**Then say over it,**" she said. "**In chalk. Where the other one was. Longer. So a person standing at that wall reads the longer one. That is all you can do and it is not nothing and I have watched a man not do it for four days.**"

---

**And a woman of forty-one of Silt Row had written four sentences the night before and shown them to nobody, and she had read them back to herself twice, and on the twenty-ninth day of the new month at about the fifth hour of the afternoon she stood up in the same yard and read them out in the same voice she has used three times, and it is the fourth statement of error in the history of this book, and every sentence in it is correct.**

"**One. You wrote the word and you told six men it was their error and you have said nine times since that the word is not a fact about them. It is a fact about you, and it has been a fact about you for forty-five days, and you have let a city of eleven hundred people build a suspicion on it while you were being fair in about nine rooms.**

"**Two. A word is not a rule. A word on a board is not a page and it has no date on it and no price and nobody can be refused by it, and it is the cheapest thing in this city to say and it has cost this city about nine days of a market's temper and will cost it more in March.**

"**Three. You have been right and you have been fair and only one of those two is any use to anybody. On the fortieth night you refused a pale field in a yard of a hundred and forty people and called it a version lock with better manners, and you were right about the field. And on the twenty-ninth day of the new month you stood in two hundred and forty people and said the true reason for the word, and the true reason is that you were embarrassed, and being right and being fair are not the same act and only one of them is on a page.**

"**Four. I am not withdrawing the three above. I am not withdrawing my vote about the seat on the sixteenth day of the new month. **I am withdrawing the belief that a man can hold a thing in his mouth for forty-five days and still be the man who says it out loud, and I would like that entered, and I would like it entered in the form I am using, which is that I said it to him in a yard and not to nine people in a room.**"

And Adrian Vale stood in about two hundred and forty people and said:

"**All four of those are correct and I am not going to say the fifth thing, which is that I am sorry, because I have been asked what I was sorry for and I have been asked three times in this city and I have been sorry twice and one of those was for the wrong reason.** Enter that I stood by the four sentences, that I can say no more than that, and that I am going to go and find out what the word costs, which is a thing I have not done."

"You are not going to find out what it costs," said Wenna Cray. "**You are going to be asked about it every day for a year by people who have decided you did it on purpose.**"

"Yes," he said. "**That is the cost, and I got it for a chalk mark, and that is the best trade I have made in this world.**"

---

**And Priya Shah got up on a trestle at about the sixth hour of the evening, in the market, in front of about two hundred and forty people, with a stick of chalk, and wrote a longer true thing over a shorter one, and it is on a board in a market and anybody may read it, and it is nineteen words long.**

She did not scrub the board. She wrote over the top of the word, large, in two lines, and left the word underneath, and anybody standing at that wall reads the two lines first and the word second, and about nine feet of that market now has both.

> **A person who came through that wall is a person.**
>
> **The word for one is a word from a place nine days of travelling away, it means somebody playing, and no person in this market is playing, and the man who wrote it is sorry, and the man who wrote it is in this city and can be asked.**

Nine words and eleven words, and it is the only sentence in the history of this city that is in the market in a language almost nobody reads, and it is in English, and about four people in two hundred and forty could read it before this afternoon and by the seventh hour of the evening about nine people had asked her to say it again in the local language, and she said it about nine times and did it badly and said so.

"Not my language," she said, to the ninth person who asked. "**It is not my language either. Ask him. He is the one who has to say it nine times and I only have to say it once and I am not going to be the woman who stands in front of a word instead of the man who put it there.**"

And Adrian Vale said it nine times, in the market, in about an hour and a half, to about ninety people, and the ninth time he said it to a woman of sixty-one who cannot read and who had come down two hundred steps for the fourth time in nine years, and Sabra Vint said: "Say the other one as well. Say the word you wrote. I want to have heard both of them in the same mouth in one evening, because in nine years I am going to be asked which one came first and I will not remember."

And he said it, and she said: "**Good. Now the wall has two sentences on it and the older one is underneath, and that is the correct order, because the older one is the one that happened.**"

---

**And on the twenty-ninth day of the new month at about the ninth hour of the evening, in a room over a market, a man of thirty-two got out a notebook and turned back forty-five days to the ninth day of the flood, and this is what he found, and it is in his own hand, in English, in one line, and he wrote it at about the second hour of the morning of the ninth day because he was writing fast and because he was describing other people's error:**

*they think it is a game*

*they think it is a game — the Veyrans, not the ones who came with me.*

And underneath it, in smaller letters, four words:

*this is their error.*

He read it four times.

And then he wrote, at the bottom of the page, in the same hand, a new line, and it is nineteen words and it is the only correction he has ever entered in that book and he entered it himself and nobody checked it:

*Day twenty-nine of the new month. Read this again. It is the only sentence in this book that I quoted back at myself. It is four words long. It is mine.*

**And the yard under the chapel emptied at about the tenth hour and a woman of twenty-four went home and a docker of fifty-eight went to a shift and a man of thirty-one from Leeds went to count a bucket of salt into a slate, and a woman of seventy picked up a pail and went down two hundred steps past a rail that is going, and told nobody, and the four words on a market wall had nineteen mouths by the seventh hour and about nine feet of chalk and a word underneath them that is not going to come off.**
