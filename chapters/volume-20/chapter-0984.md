# Chapter 0984 — The Cloth Stops At The Foot Of The Column

The cloth came down the face of that board at the top of the top column at the usual pace, and it got to the foot of the middle column and stopped, the way it stops every morning of that flood.

It was Friday, the six hundred and sixty-ninth day of the Bare Month and the second hard morning of that run. The light came over the roof line at the far end of that market a good deal after that row had woken, and the light that did come lay in one long stripe down that row with the shade of the trestles going ahead of itself the whole length of it.

The sheet lay on the wood of that bench at the far end of that room with three lines on it a hand's width in from its right edge. The second book lay open beside the register with its cover slab drawn back off the leaf under it. There was ice in the trough on the flags at the top of that step about the depth of a thumb, and the bar lay out of its two sockets on the top step. That door at the foot of that stair is walked past by about four people a day and nobody among them has ever stopped at it.

The block of writing was still on the wall along the far side of that room. Nobody read it out loud, and nobody said that it was right or that it was wrong.

---

The man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board went at the cloth four times that morning.

He came up that stair at the fifth hour with his own board under his arm and the cloth over his own shoulder. He put the board down on the near end of that bench with its face out and its foot toward the door, took the cloth off his own shoulder, and started at the top of the top column.

The middle column stands about the depth of a thumb deeper than the two either side of it, and that is how it has been as long as anybody can remember. The cloth has stopped at the foot of it every morning of that flood and every morning before the water came. The wood at the foot of that column stands a shade proud of the wood beside it, not much of a proudness, and the edge of the cloth finds that and hangs on it.

He tried it dry first and it stopped in the same place. He put the cloth over the edge of his own board and drew it down with the weight of his own arm and it stopped in the same place and left the fold of it standing up off the wood where he had made it. He damped the end of it with the wet off his own mouth, which was not much, and tried it a third time and it stopped in the same place. On the fourth time he went slower and got the cloth down to the top of the proud place at the foot of that column and let it sit there.

**"That cloth is not going to go down past that column and I have stopped trying to make it. The middle of that board stands a thumb deeper than the two ends of it and the foot of it stands up off the wood about as much as a coin, and the edge of the cloth finds that and hangs on it every single morning. I could shave it off with a blade and get the whole face of that board in one pass. I am not going to. I have a hand for cloth and I have not got one for wood, and there are nineteen rates on that board that are worth more to me than the length of my own arm with the cloth on it," ** said the man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board, at the near end of that bench with the cloth in his own hand and his own board face out on the wood in front of him.

He said that to the bench. Then he folded the cloth over his own shoulder and went down the face of that board again, from the top of the top column to the top of the proud place at the foot of the middle one. It stopped there again, and he did it a fourth time after the light had begun to go.

---

Nobody who came up that stair that morning said a word to anybody else about the first thing that had been said in that room, and the rest of it went on.

The woman of about fifty-two stood at the far end of that bench with her own shoulder to the wood behind it and her own hands at the sides of her dress, and she gave the whole of that morning back in her face and had no part in it.

The keeper went down the column that holds figures and came back up it, lifted the topmost page of her own leaf at its outer corner and put it down square against the fold, and did the same again an hour after that. Her own hand went flat on the sheet with the three lines on it and stayed there a while and came off it.

The stallholder came up that stair at about the ninth hour and took the middle of that bench with his own board across his own knees. He went along the foot of it with his own thumb, from the top of the middle of it to the foot of it and back up again, twice. His own hand went flat against the front of his own coat over that pocket once and came off again, and the chalk stayed where it has been all that flood.

The man of about thirty-one who carries things for a living stood at the near wall with the satchel held up under his own arm and the flap of it hanging away from his own side. He turned about off that wall once, looked at the flags, and turned about again.

The man of about twenty-seven carried the bucket down that stair and up it three times, and the last of those three times he did not go on down the row afterwards. He stood on the flags at the top of that step with his own hand on the outside of the rim of that trough and stood there looking at the ice in it. Nothing had been fetched for it and nobody offered him anything.

The tradesman came up that stair a fourth time in the middle of the afternoon on the same errand as the morning, and did not stop in that row at all and did not come up the stair.

At about the eleventh hour the tradesman took the cloth off his own shoulder and laid it on the face of his own board at the foot of that middle column. He left it there and went along the foot of the board with his own thumb instead, and the cloth lay where he had put it with its edge caught on the proud place and the fold still up in it.

---

The cloth was lying on the face of that board when the light went, at the foot of the middle column and no further. Its lower edge was not square with the wood it lay on, because he had let it go where it caught and had not straightened it after, and the fold he had made in it on the second attempt was still standing up off the face of the board where the damp had got into it.
