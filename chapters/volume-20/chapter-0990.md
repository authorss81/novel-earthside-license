# Chapter 0990 — His Hand Beside Six Strokes And Not On Them

The sheet lay on the wood of that bench at the far end of that room a hand's width from the register, with one thing written on it a hand's width in from its right edge and the space below that thing empty.

It was Thursday, the six hundred and seventy-fifth day of the Bare Month. The sun was up before the light and came over the roof line at the far end of that market at about the fourth hour, and it came along that row with the shade of the trestles going ahead of it. The air over eleven miles of flats had gone warm in the night and stood close under the awning at the store's end until about the seventh hour and then thinned.

Out of its two sockets on the top step of that stair the bar lay where it has lain every morning of that flood. The trough on the flags beside it had ice in it about the depth of a thumb, and nothing had been fetched for it and nothing had been broken in it. About four people a day went along the walking space at the foot of that stair, most of them going up and some of them going down.

The block of writing was still there on the wall along the far side of that room. The day was chalked on the outside of the door at the foot of that stair, and the bare board under it stood clear of the near plank by the width of a thumb with one mark on it.

Adrian Vale had the back of his own right hand on that sheet from the fifth hour, and his own left hand flat on the wood of the bench about a hand's width off from it, and the register lay open between them with the column that holds figures showing.

---

He wanted the seventh stroke to go into that column.

There are six strokes in it, and there is a day standing against each of the six, and not one of the six has been struck out. At the foot of that column there is a bare place about the length of a thumb, where the wood is the colour of the wood above it and nothing has been in it since it was made.

He went at it the way he goes at everything. He put his own hand flat on the register leaf on the open side of it and looked down the column from the head of it to the foot of it and back up again, and he did it three times that morning. He read the six days standing against the six strokes and he could not tell from any of them whose hand had put the stroke down, and that was not because the days were hard to read. It was that a day against a figure says when and does not say who, and that is the whole of what a day against a figure has ever said.

He wanted a seventh stroke in that column more than he had wanted anything in a long while, and it was not his to enter, and it never had been and never would be. The book on that bench belonged to the woman of about twenty-nine who keeps a public register, and a stroke went into that column when she put it there with her own hand. No hand but hers had ever put one in it. Nobody in that room had asked him to put one in it, and nobody had said one word to him about it.

There is no page in this city that anybody has offered him and he has looked at all of them. He had asked for his own name on a page for thirty-one volumes, and the one thing he has got out of that is a line with a day against a figure in it and nothing at all saying whose figure it was. He wanted the seventh stroke in that column to be his, and he could not have said in what way it would be his except that he was the one putting it there.

---

The space below the one thing on that sheet was about the width of his own hand and the height of his own two hands laid flat, and it was bare paper.

His own hand was on that paper at the side of it and not in it. He got the heel of his own hand to the edge of it at about the seventh hour and left it there for about as long as it takes to get a good edge on a thing with a blade. He did not put the point of anything on that paper at any point of that day.

Nobody in that room said his name at any hour of it. Nobody in that room was waiting for him to be useful to anybody, and his own two hands were of no use to any of them. He went on standing at the far end of that bench with the back of his own hand on a piece of paper that had one thing on it and nothing at all under it.

---

Everything else in that room went on as it had gone on for eleven days.

The woman of about fifty-two stood at the far end of that bench with her own hands at the sides of her dress. The tradesman came up at the fifth hour and went along the face of his own board with the cloth. He took it down that stair to his own trestle before the light had got off the roof line and came back up with nothing in his hands. The stallholder came up at about the ninth hour and put his own board across his own knees in the middle of that bench, the bare back of it to the room, and began at the head of the middle of it with his own thumb.

At the near wall the man of about thirty-one who carries things for a living stood with the satchel held up under his own arm and the flap of it hanging down. The man of about twenty-seven carried the bucket down that stair and up it again three times, and the third of those times he stayed on the flags at the top of that step with his own hand on the outside of the rim of that trough for a while.

At about the tenth hour the keeper came back up that stair and wanted to lift the topmost page of her own leaf at its outer corner, and his own shoulder was in the way of the wood of that bench where her own hand wanted to go. She waited about as long as it takes to read a thing twice. Then she reached across him with her own left hand, took the dry cloth off the head of her own leaf, and laid it along the head of the second book's leaf instead, and squared it against the crack in the column of that leaf with her own thumbnail.

She went on down the column that holds figures and came back up it with the cloth lying on the other book.

The light came along that bench about the middle of the afternoon and reached the sheet, and the back of his own hand was on it, and the three lines were under his own hand where they had been since the light. He took it off the paper at about the time the light went off the wood and put it down at his own side.

---

That cloth was on the head of the second book's leaf when the light went off that room, and it was on the leaf and not on the slab. The slab lay drawn back off the leaf under it in the place it lies in, and the crack in the column of that leaf stood a hair proud of the stone about it with the cloth's edge along the whole length of it.
