# Chapter 0965 — The Near End Taken By Two Hands

Two hands of a man lay flat on the wood of the near end of that bench, palms down and fingers out and about the width of a hand apart, and they had been in that place since the fourth hour and were still in it.

It was Sunday, the six hundred and fiftieth day of the Bare Month. There had been no sun before the light and the air stood close in that market until about the eighth hour, and then the light came along the row and stopped in a stripe about the middle of the bench in the room over that market. The bar lay along the top step of that stair out of its two sockets with the frost gone out of it, and the two sockets on the far side of the door below stood empty. On the outside of that door there was the day in chalk, and the bare board under it carried the one mark that was on it. The box of chalk stood on the table inside that room with the lid of it off at one corner.

The woman of about fifty-two came up that stair at about the fifth hour and went to her own far wall and put her own hands at the sides of her dress.

---

It was the first thing said in that room that morning, and she said it out loud.

**"I am not having it written down, and I have known that since before I came up these stairs, and I am going to ask it anyway," ** said the woman of about fifty-two, at her own far wall with her own hands at the sides of her dress and the stool standing against the wood a foot off her own skirt.

She said it out loud in that room in daylight with the date on the outside of the door behind her own shoulder. No mouth in that room said one word back to her about it. She did not say it a second time before the light went or after it.

---

The man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board had come up that stair at about the fourth hour to put his own board down on the near end of that bench, as he has done every morning of that flood, and the near end of that bench was taken.

That was all it was. Two hands on the wood at the near end and nothing in them. He stood at the door of that room for a moment with his board under his own arm and the cloth over his own shoulder. Then he went the length of that bench and put his own board down at the far end of it, past the keeper. The keeper moved her own register along the wood about the width of a hand to give him room to stand, and the second book came a hand nearer to her than it had stood all week. Nobody said anything to anybody about where the tradesman was going to keep his board. He went on down the face of it with the cloth and the cloth stopped at the foot of the middle column the way it stops there every morning.

The keeper had her register open in front of her with the dry cloth along the head of her own leaf and the two books open side by side in front of her and a foot of bare wood at the far end of that bench with a man's board on it. The man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall two stalls along came up that stair at about the tenth hour with his own board under his arm and sat down in the middle of that bench. The man of about thirty-one who carries things for a living had the near wall with the satchel held up against his ribs.

---

At about the ninth hour she put it to him.

She put it across about nine feet of that room, to the man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board, and it was said once. It went from her mouth into his ear and it stayed there. Nobody in that room repeated it to anybody and nobody in that row was told what it was. There is no leaf in either of those two books that has a scratch of it on it, there is no board in that market with any part of it on, and there is no chalk anywhere in this city that has carried one word of it out of that room.

He answered her. Nobody in that room was told what the answer was. He said it once and it went the same way the putting went, out of that room and down that stair and along that row to his own trestle, and he did not say it a second time before the light went or after, and nobody in that room asked him for it again.

**"That is the whole of what I am going to say to you about it in this room and it is the whole of what I am going to say to you out of it. I am not going to have it written down anywhere and neither are you, and if either of us says one word of it to anybody in this row it will have gone out of this room twice, and that is twice too many," ** said the man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board, at the far end of that bench with his own hand flat on the face of his own board.

She did not thank him. Nobody in that room told him he was right, and nobody told him he was wrong. The keeper's own hand went to the dry cloth along the head of her leaf, lifted one end of it about the width of a nail, and put the end down again without moving the leaf.

---

The rest of that morning in that room was work.

The man of about twenty-seven carried the bucket down that stair past the bottom step and up it again with the water standing flat in it, and did it twice more after the light had begun to go. The ice in the trough at the top of that step was still whole about the depth of a thumb and nothing had been fetched for it and nobody offered him anything.

The man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board took his own board down that stair to his own trestle in the middle of the morning and came back up with nothing in his hands, and put it down at the far end of that bench again. Adrian Vale was still at the near end of it with his own two hands flat on the wood and nothing in them, and nobody in that room was waiting for him to be useful to anybody.

What he wanted, and did not get, was for the woman of about fifty-two to come to that end of that bench and say, in her own words and to him and not to the room, what she had just refused. She did not come to that end of that bench. She stayed where she was all morning with her own hands at the sides of her dress, and nobody in that room put anything to Adrian Vale at any point of the day, and nobody said his name.

---

By the light's going the two books were lying open side by side on that bench with a leaf showing on one of them and a leaf showing on the other and neither of them shut, and no leaf on either of them had anything on it that came out of that morning.

The woman of about fifty-two had her own two hands on the wood of that bench at the far end of it by then, flat, about the width of a hand apart, well past the second book and not on it. She had had her hands at the sides of her dress from the fifth hour until some time in the middle of the afternoon, and the wood where she put them was bare wood that nothing else in that room had been against all day.
