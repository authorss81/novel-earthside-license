# Chapter 0979 — That Foot And That Lid And Neither Comes Up

**"This lid has been standing off that corner since before the water came. I am going to put it down on the box one more time this morning and it is going to stand off at the same corner. I have said as much to myself twice already this week, and I am saying it out loud this morning because a thing you have only said to yourself is worth nothing," ** said the woman of about twenty-nine who keeps a public register, at the table in that room with her own right hand flat on the lid.

It was Sunday, the six hundred and sixty-fourth day of the Bare Month. The night had been hard and clear for the fifth morning running, and there had been no sun before the light for five days. The light came over the roof line at the far end of that market a good deal after that row had woken. It came in under the awning at the store's end, lay down the length of that bench, and stopped short of the middle of the wood. Out of its two sockets on the top step of that stair the bar lay where it has lain all that flood, and there was ice in the trough on the flags beside it about the depth of a thumb. Chalked on the outside of that door was the day, and under the day the bare board stood its own width of a thumb off the near plank with the one mark on it.

The woman of about fifty-two came up at about the fifth hour and went to the far end of that bench. The man of about thirty-one who carries things for a living took the near wall that morning, with the flap of his satchel down. The light was on the flags there, and about four people a day went along the walking space.

---

She put the lid down on that box and it stood off at that corner. She took her hand off it, looked at the corner, and put it down again, and it stood off at that corner. She put it down a third time with the heel of her own hand, and it stood off at that corner.

That lid is a little proud of the rim on one side of it and not on the other. The wood of the box underneath the corner it stands off at has gone pale in a crescent about the width of two fingers and about the length of a thumb. The air has been getting at the inside of the box and at the wood under the lip of that lid all that flood, and the dust of that room has not been able to get at either of them. A lid that stands off at one corner is a lid with a corner it does not come down over, and putting it down on the box does not change the corner. The two sticks inside lie one across the other where they have lain every morning of that flood.

She put the lid standing off at that corner, the way it had stood at the light, and went back to the far end of the bench. She put her own hand flat on the page in front of her and went on with the column that holds figures, from the head of it to the foot of it and back up again, with a day standing against each of the six strokes in it and not one of the six struck out. The second book lay open beside her with its slab drawn back off the leaf under it.

---

Down that row the man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall two stalls along went at the near foot of his own trestle again.

He had left it standing in that flag on the ninth day of that flood and had stopped working at it before the light began to go because the ground had been wet. The two days since had dried it. He went along that row at about the ninth hour with the flat of his own board under his own arm. He got his own hand at the near foot of that trestle and worked at the flag round it with his own thumb and then with the edge of his own board, which is what he wanted to try on the wet ground and could not.

He got the flat of his own board down against the walking space and put his weight onto it. The trestle did not lift, and the flag did not lift either. He stood on that walking space with his weight on his board for about as long as it takes to get a good edge on a thing with a blade.

**"It has gone down with the foot. That flag has not been sitting on the top of a stone for eleven years. It has been sitting on the top of that foot, and the foot has been going down into the ground a little at a time, and the ground has come up round it while it was doing it. That is why it is in there and why a blade will not get under it. It is not coming up this morning and it is not coming up in a week. If it goes over in the wet it goes over on the walking space and takes everything on that board down with it, so it is standing and I am leaving it standing," ** said the man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall two stalls along, on the walking space at his own trestle with the flat of his own board down against the flag.

He picked his board up off the walking space and wiped the grit off the edge of it with the flat of his own hand. He turned the face of it out to the row, stood at his own trestle, and went along the foot of the face of it with his own thumb from the top of the middle column to the foot of it and back up again, twice.

---

That flag came up at its near corner about the width of two fingers.

It came up on the side away from the trestle, where the grit had been standing in the joint all that flood and had been packed flat by the boot of a person going up and down the walking space. It lifted with the corner of it and stood up on the edge, where the frost had got under it in the night and the grit beneath had dried to a crust about the depth of a grain of rice. Under that corner there was the earth of the walking space, pale and damp and packed hard, standing about the depth of two fingers up on the edge with the crust on the top of it like a lip.

That flag stood up on its edge about the depth of two fingers for the rest of that day, with the foot of the trestle in the flag it had gone down into and nothing at all under the corner of it holding it up.
