# Chapter 0996 — Out Past The Last Named House Along The Flats

It had rained hard in the night and stopped a little before the light, and the standing water in eleven miles of flats was about the depth of two fingers in the flat places and had gone down off the crust on the high places by about the fourth hour.

It was Wednesday, the six hundred and eighty-first day of the Bare Month. The sun was not up before the light and the wind came off that rain out of the west and went along the flats and died about two miles out. The ground out past the last named house had taken two days of that rain and had gone hard on the top of it, and the carts and the barrows and the feet of everything stood on that crust with soft ground an inch or two under it. The walking spaces of that row stood pale and hard in the middle and the joints of them had not dried at all.

The trough on the flags at the store's end of that row had ice in it about the depth of a thumb and nothing had been fetched for it. The bar lay out of its two sockets on the top step of the stair at the foot of that store. That board on the outside wall at the far end of that market was on its one nail above its step, with the hole of a second nail empty above it and the wood around the lip of that hole gone soft a whole finger's width from the hole all the way round. No second nail went into that wall that day and nothing was written on that board.

---

The man of about thirty-one who carries things for a living came out past the last named house at about the sixth hour with the satchel on his own back and nothing in it.

He went along the flats and not the tops of the ridges, and he went out to the side where the ground is worst, and the water came up over the sole of his own boot about every third step and went down again. Where the crust was broken he went round it. Where it was not broken he went straight over it. About halfway along that stretch his own heel went through in one place about the size of his own foot, and he stood in the soft ground under it up to the ankle for about as long as it takes to read a thing twice before he got himself out of it and went on.

There is a shut road at the top of that ground and the fence is along the top of it.

The fence is made of willow posts with withies bound onto them out of one cord, and it has been there since before the water came. Nobody has measured that fence, and nobody has walked it with anything in their hands, and no post of it has been moved since it was put up. The withies on it are the same withies and the cord is the same cord and the willow is the same willow that it was made out of. The ground at the foot of it had come up round the bottom of those posts a little at a time all that flood and had not come up on one of them more than another.

He came up onto the top of that road at about the seventh hour and walked along it, and the fence went along on his own left with the withies standing out of it about the depth of a hand. The posts stood up out of the ground and every one of them was in the same place.

He did not stop at the fence and he did not go along it with his own hand on it, and there was nothing in his own hands to go along it with.

---

The house of the man of about fifty-seven stands on the far side of that road with its own door standing open and the step of it down to the ground.

The man of about fifty-seven was in his own yard with the door of that yard standing crooked on its own hinge the way it had hung since the water came. A bucket stood under the run-off from the corner of his own roof where it had been running all that flood. He came out into his own yard with the bucket in his own hand and stopped when he saw the other man on that road, and he stood there.

His own coat was buttoned and his own boots were wet to the top of them and he did not go out onto that road. He stood where he had stopped and looked at the man on that road for about as long as it takes to say something twice, and neither of them said anything at all to the other.

Then he turned about and went back in under the roof of that yard with the bucket, and he came back out with it about a quarter full and set it down under that same corner and stood looking at the water standing in it.

The other man went on along the top of that road and came past that house and did not stop at it.

---

Through the open door of that house, from the road, there is the inside of one room and the back of one chair standing in it.

Over the back of that chair lies a length of new rope. It came into this city with him and it has lain on that chair every morning of that flood and it is as good as the day it arrived. No hand went near it that day and nothing was lifted off that chair and nobody in that road asked that man anything at all.

On the peg inside that door there is a length of cord and a length of iron with a hole in the end of it, and they were on that peg when that man went out into his own yard and they were on that peg when he came back in.

---

The man of about thirty-one who carries things for a living came back along that road at about the ninth hour and went down onto that ground and across the flats and in past the last named house and up that row. The satchel on his own back was empty when he went out and empty when he came back, and the flap of it hung down the whole way.

At the store's end of that row the ice in that trough was still whole about the depth of a thumb. The thread of water out of the crack in its near end was going across the flags and off the bottom of that step onto the walking space, and nothing had been fetched for that trough that day and nothing had been broken in it. The man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall two stalls along had his own board face in against the wood of his own trestle at that end of that row and did not look up as the other man came past him.

---

There was no wind on that road at that hour at all. The withies between one pair of those posts were slack and hung about the depth of a hand, and the withies between the next pair were not slack and stood out of that fence straight and stiff. Nobody untied any of them and nobody tied any of them and nobody counted them.
