# Chapter 0969 — Two Mornings Nobody In That Room Saw

Two hands came up off the satchel strap at the near wall and went into the inside of a coat and came out again empty, and the man who owns the satchel went back to the wall with nothing in them.

It was Thursday, the six hundred and fifty-fourth day of the Bare Month. The sky had gone hard and white over the flats in the night and there was no sun before the light and the light came up at about the seventh hour and came along that row all at once. That light came in under the awning at the store's end of that market and lay down the length of that bench and stopped at the middle of it and did not reach the far wall.

The bar was on the top step of that stair where it has lain every morning of that flood, and the trough on the flags beside it had ice in it about the depth of a thumb and no heat had been fetched for it. Nothing went under the date on the outside of that door that morning and nothing has gone under it since the one mark was put there.

---

The man of about thirty-one who carries things for a living went into his own coat twice that morning and brought nothing out of it either time.

The first was at about the sixth hour, before the light had got along the row. The second was at about the eleventh hour, when the tradesman had gone down that stair with his own board and the near end of that bench was standing empty. There was chalk in that coat and it has been in that coat since before this flood. About four people a day go past the foot of that stair and he takes one stroke against each of them, and what he does with the strokes when he has finished them is his own business. They are not on the ground in front of that stair, not on that door, not under the date on the outside of it and not in either of those two books.

**"There is chalk in this coat and it is a piece of chalk and it is not a thing anybody in this room is going to get out of me this morning. I take one against them and I put it back and I take another out, and I have done that since before the water. What I do with them is my own business and it does not go on that door and it does not go in either of those books, and I am not going to say anything else about it to anybody in here."** said the man of about thirty-one who carries things for a living, at the near wall with the satchel against his own ribs.

---

At about the tenth hour the woman of about fifty-two said a thing at her own far wall.

**"Somebody put a thing to me on the five hundred and ninety-eighth day of this flood. I have had the answer to it this whole week and I did not know until this morning that I had it. I have been carrying it about with me since then and it has not been on any surface and I have not said one word of it out loud, and I am saying now that I have got it and not saying what it is."** said the woman of about fifty-two, at her own wall with her own hands at the sides of her dress.

Then she said what it costs her, in her own mouth, once.

**"That is said in front of about nine people in this room and it goes out of here in their mouths as well as in mine and I cannot put any of it back in, and nobody in this room is going to write one word of it down for me, and I would not let them if they offered."** She said it at that wall and stopped there and said nothing else about it before the light went or after.

Nobody thanked her for saying it, and nobody told her she was right, or that she was wrong.

The man of about thirty-eight at that salt wharf had come up that stair with his own board under his own arm that morning, and he heard her, and he stood at the near end of that bench and waited until she had finished before he said anything at all.

**"I was in that yard on that morning. I do not remember that morning at all. I have stood here this morning trying to bring that morning up and there is nothing standing behind my own eyes when I go at it, and I am not saying that is an answer to what she has said and I am not saying it is not one. I am saying what I can stand here and answer for."** The man of about thirty-eight said it at the near end of that bench with both his own hands on the face of his own board.

---

Nobody in that room compared one of those two mornings with the other of them, and nobody said that she had remembered it right, or that she had remembered it wrong, or that he had remembered right, or that he had remembered wrong.

The only person in that room who could have said anything about either of those two mornings was the woman of about twenty-nine who keeps a public register, and she was not there on the five hundred and ninety-eighth day of that flood any more than either of the other two had been there together. She said nothing about it and went on with her own page.

The man of about thirty-eight at that salt wharf took his own board down that stair and back up again in the middle of the afternoon and stood at the near end of that bench with both his own hands on the face of it and the cloth folded over the top of it. The rest of that room went on. Nothing had been fetched to warm that trough and nothing had been broken in the ice of it. The gate at the far end of that shut road was not mentioned. The fence along the top of that road was not measured and no post of it moved.

---

By the light's going the man of about thirty-one who carries things for a living had his own satchel standing on the flags inside that room about a foot to the left of the place it had been standing in all that flood.

He had carried it out to the low place in the stone at the side of that yard and back up that stair and had set it down where he set it down. The place it had been standing in had been against that wall for a week with the damp coming up behind it and no air at all getting in behind it. That place was a shade paler than the flags either side of it, in the shape of the satchel itself, with a soft edge to it about the width of a nail all round, and nothing was standing on it and nothing was going to stand on it again.
