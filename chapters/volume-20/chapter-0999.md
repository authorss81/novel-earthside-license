# Chapter 0999 — The Space Below The Writing Left Bare

The keeper was three-quarters of the way down the column that holds figures when the light got under the awning at the store's end of that row, and she went the rest of that column and came back up it and did it again.

It was Saturday, the six hundred and eighty-fourth day of the Bare Month. The air came off eleven miles of flats and stood close under that awning until about the ninth hour, and the light came along that row with the shade of the trestles going ahead of it. Four hundred yards of flags ran from the store's end of that row and gave out into the side of that yard at the far end of it. The walking spaces of them had begun to go pale and hard from the under side while the joints of them were still black from the rain of the night.

Along the top step of that stair, out of its two sockets and clear of them, lay that bar, and the sockets themselves stood empty in the far side of the door below. The trough at the store's end of that row had ice in it about the depth of a thumb and nothing had been fetched for it that day and nothing had been broken in it. Outside that door at the foot of that stair there was a day in chalk, and the chalk of it stood broken along the joint between the two planks with about a finger's width of that writing gone down into the groove. The bare board under that day stood clear of the near plank by the width of a thumb and carried one mark on it. About four people a day went past the foot of that stair while the light was on the flags, and that stair is walked past at other hours of that day as well.

The block of writing stood on that wall with the plaster either side of it the colour it has been for sixteen days, and it was not read out that day and nothing was done about it at any hour of it. The two books lay open side by side on the wood of that bench at the far end of the room and the second book had the dry cloth lying in the crack of its leaf where it had gone down on Tuesday.

---

The sheet lay on the wood of that bench beside the register.

The three lines on it from the earlier morning of that week were where they had been since the morning they were written, a hand's width in from the right edge of that sheet. Under them there were three more lines, written a hand's width in from the same edge, and the second of those three was about the length of the first of them and the third of them was the longest of the six. Below the lowest of those six lines the paper was bare from there to the near edge of the sheet and across to the width of a palm on the other side of them.

Nothing was added to that sheet on that day and nothing was taken off it, and neither of those two books was written on at any hour of it.

At about the middle of the morning the keeper took the stub of the pencil out of the pocket of her dress and put it down on the wood of that bench beside the sheet. She put her own hand flat on the bare paper below the lowest of those six lines and left it there about as long as it takes to read a thing twice. Then she took her own hand off that paper and put the pencil back into the pocket of her dress, and she did not take it out again that day.

---

That room went on with what it had been doing.

At about the fifth hour the woman of about fifty-two came up that stair, and what she carried up it was that stool, which she set against the wood of the wall along the far side of that room in the place she sets it down. Then she stood at the far end of that bench with her own shoulder to the wood behind the far end of it and her own hands at the sides of her dress.

The man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board came up at the fifth hour with his own board under his arm and the cloth over his own shoulder, and put his own board down on the near end of that bench with its face out and its foot toward the door. He came down the face of it with the cloth from the top of the top column, and the cloth came off the face at the foot of the middle column where the middle of that board stands about the depth of a thumb deeper than the two ends of it. He folded the cloth back over his own shoulder and left it there.

The man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall two stalls along came up about the ninth hour, and before he sat down he stood in front of that bench for about as long as it takes to look at a row of trestles from end to end and be doing nothing else while you do it. Then he took the middle of it. Then he took the middle of that bench with his own board across his own knees, the bare back of it to that room, and went along the foot of that board with his own thumb three times that day.

His own palm went flat against the front of his own coat over the inside breast pocket once, stayed there, and came off again, and the chalk stayed where it has been all that flood.

At about the tenth hour he went down that stair to the store's end of that row and got his own hand at the near foot of his own trestle and looked at the near corner of that flag.

**"That foot has gone down into its own flag about as far as it is going to go and the ground has come up round it while it was doing it, and there is a corner of that flag standing up on its edge now that will go over with the next cart that comes along this walking space. I am not going to be the man that says anything about that corner to anybody, and I have not said anything about it to anybody," ** said the man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall two stalls along, at the foot of his own trestle with his own hand flat on the wood of it.

He went back up that stair and went along the foot of his own board with his own thumb again.

The man of about thirty-one who carries things for a living came up at about the sixth hour and stood at the near wall with the satchel held up against his own ribs and the flap of it hanging away from his own side. He turned about off that wall twice and looked at the flags and turned about again, and the flap of the satchel was not turned back.

The man of about twenty-seven made four trips up that stair with the bucket, and the water stood flat in it every one of the four times. On the fourth of those trips he stayed on the flags at the top of that step with his own hand on the outside of the rim of that trough, looking at the ice.

Nobody went and stood in front of that block of writing on that wall, and no hand in that room went near it while the light was on that wall, and not one mouth in that room said a word about it.

In the middle of that afternoon she went out of that room, down that stair and along that row and away past the store's end of it, and the stool stood in that room where she had left it.

---

The space below the lowest of those six lines had the dust that had come up that stair that morning standing on it in a film about the depth of a grain of rice. Where her own hand had been flat on it there was the shape of her own hand pressed into that dust about as deep as the dust itself went, and the shape of it was there when the light had gone off that bench.
