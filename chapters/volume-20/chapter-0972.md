# Chapter 0972 — Four Things In That Room That Did Not Agree

The woman of about twenty-nine who keeps a public register had been at the column that holds figures for the better part of an hour before anybody else came up that stair.

It was Sunday, the six hundred and fifty-seventh day of the Bare Month. The sun was not up before the light and the air stood close along that row until about the seventh hour, and then it came along the flags in one piece and put the damp up off them. The bar lay along the top step of that stair out of its two sockets and the trough on the flags beside it had ice in it about the depth of a thumb. There was the day in chalk on the outside of that door at the foot of that stair, and the bare board under it stood clear of the near plank by the width of a thumb with the one mark on it that has been on it since the morning before this flood came. About four people a day come along that walking space outside, and that is what that is.

The woman of about fifty-two came up at about the fifth hour and went to the far end of that bench, where she had stood the day before, and put her own hands at the sides of her dress. The man of about thirty-one who carries things for a living had the near wall with the satchel up against his own ribs. The two books lay open side by side on that bench with a leaf showing on one of them and a leaf showing on the other of them.

---

There were four things in that room a person could read that morning. Any one of them could be read in a different direction from any of the others, and no two of them agreed about anything.

The first of them was a page. The keeper's own leaf lay open in front of her at the far end of the bench, and there was a column down it that holds figures. There are six strokes in that column and a day standing against each of them and not one of the six struck out. It is read from the head of it to the foot of it, the head being the top of the leaf and the foot being the bottom of it, and that is the only direction it is ever read in.

The second of them was a surface. The man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board put his own board down on the near end of the bench at about the fifth hour with the face of it out to the door. There are nineteen rates on that face in three columns. The middle one of the three comes down about the depth of a thumb further than the two either side of it. There is a space at the foot of the middle column about two fingers wide with the wood in it paler than the wood either side of it, and it stands empty. A board is read across, from the near edge to the far edge, and that is the only direction it is ever read in. Not one of the nineteen rates has a day standing against it anywhere on that face. The cloth comes down the face of the board every morning and stops at the foot of the middle column and goes no further.

The third of them was neither of those. The second book lay on the wood beside the register with its cover slab drawn back off the leaf under it and that leaf lying flat, and it is not a page to be read down and it is not a surface to be read across. There is a crack down the length of the column in that leaf and a different stone in it which has taken the colour of the stone round it and stands a hair proud of the surface it sits in. There is a word near the head of the column on the right of the leaf which the people who own that book have never printed and have never asked anybody about. There is a day entered in that book with nothing standing against it anywhere. Nobody in that room read the leaf down and nobody read it across, because it is neither of the two things a person reads in this world, and the slab stands about the width of a card proud of the leaf on its own end, where it has stood all that flood.

The fourth of them was the bare back of the man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall two stalls along's own board, which lay face down across his own knees in the middle of the bench from about the ninth hour. The wood of it holds its own grain and two battens across the back and nothing written on it at all, and it is a surface read across by nobody, because there is nothing on it to read.

Those four things did not agree about anything. One of them holds a figure against a day. One of them holds figures with no day against any of them. One of them holds a day with nothing standing against it. And one of them holds nothing at all. No mouth in that room put two of them together and no mouth in that room said which of the four was the kind of thing that mattered, and the room went on.

---

The morning went on in the way that room goes on.

The tradesman came up that stair at the fifth hour with his own board and the cloth over his own shoulder. He took the board down again to his own trestle before the light had got off the roof line at the far end of the market. He came back up with nothing in his hands and stood at the near end of the bench. The man of about twenty-seven carried the bucket down that stair and up it three times with the water standing flat in it. The ice in the trough at the top of the step stayed whole, no heat was fetched for it, and nobody offered. The stallholder went along the foot of his own board with his own thumb from the top of it to the foot of it and back up again, twice over. His own hand went flat against the front of his own coat over the inside breast pocket once at about the eleventh hour and stayed there a while. The chalk stayed in that pocket where it has been all that flood. The carrier turned about off the near wall, looked at the stair, and turned about again. The keeper lifted the topmost page of her own register at its outer corner, turned it and put it down square against the fold. She lifts it at the outer corner because the edge of it lies down on the leaf under it along about the whole of a hand of the head of that leaf, and the dry cloth will not go down into a gap that is not there. No leaf of either book was picked up, turned over, closed or written on at any point of that day, and the two of them lay open side by side on the bench from the light to the going of it.

When the light had got off the roof line at the far end of that market the stallholder took his own hand off the face of his own board, turned the board over on his own knees, and laid the bare back of it face up across them in the light. The grain of it and the two battens across the back of it were the whole of what was on it. He sat with his own two hands at the ends of it and looked at the wood of it the way a man looks at the back of a thing he has been looking at the front of all week.
