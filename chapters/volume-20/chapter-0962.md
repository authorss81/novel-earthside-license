# Chapter 0962 — A Hand On The Pocket And Nothing Out

A hand went flat on the wood of that bench at the far end of it, on the square of it about as big as a palm, and pressed, and stayed there while the light came along the room and stopped short of it.

It was Thursday, the six hundred and forty-seventh day of the Bare Month. The sun had been up before the light and had come over the roof line at the far end of that market and along that row and in under the awning at the store's end by the fourth hour, and it lay down the length of that bench and stopped about the middle of the wood. The bar lay along the top step of that stair where it has lain every morning of that flood, out of its two sockets, and the two sockets on the far side of the door below were empty. On the outside of that door the day stood out in chalk and the bare board under it carried the one mark that was on it.

The woman of about twenty-nine who keeps a public register had the far end of that bench with her register open in front of her and the dry cloth along the head of her own leaf. The second book lay on the wood beside it with its cover slab drawn back off the leaf under it and that leaf lying flat. The man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall two stalls along sat in the middle of that bench with his own board flat across his knees and the bare back of it turned out to the room and one hand down the side of his coat. The man of about thirty-one who carries things for a living stood at the near wall with the satchel held up under his arm and the flap of it hanging away from his own side. The woman of about fifty-two held her far wall with her hands down the sides of her dress, and the stool stood against the wood a foot off her own skirt. The tradesman came up that stair twice and went down it twice.

---

Nobody came up that stair and stayed. The man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board put his own board down on the near end of that bench at about the fifth hour and went along the foot of it with the cloth. He took it down that stair again to his own trestle before the light had got off the roof line at the far end of that market, and came back up with nothing in his hands.

The room carried on. The keeper turned one leaf of her register and squared it against the fold and put her own finger into the gap at the head of it and took the finger out again and looked at the finger and wiped what was on it off on the corner of her own sleeve. The woman of about fifty-two did not move off her wall. The man of about thirty-one who carries things for a living turned about off the near wall twice and looked at the flags inside that room and turned about again. The two books lay side by side on that bench the whole of it with a leaf showing on one and a leaf showing on the other and nobody in that room comparing one with the other.

The second book had its cover slab drawn back off the leaf under it and the leaf under it lying flat, and the slab stood about the width of a card proud of the leaf on its own end where it has stood all that flood. The dry cloth along the head of the keeper's leaf was not long enough to reach across the two of them, and it lay along the head of the register and stopped at the near edge of the second book and did not go on across it, and nobody in that room moved it so that it would.

About four people a day went past the foot of that stair while the light was on the flags, and that is what that is, and nobody in that room has ever taken it and given it to anybody.

---

At about the ninth hour the man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall two stalls along put his own hand flat against the front of his coat over the inside breast pocket and left it there.

The pocket did not come open and nothing came out of it. He stood with his own hand on the outside of that pocket for about as long as it takes to get a good edge on a thing with a blade. Then he took the hand off and put it back on the foot of his own board across his knees and went along the foot of it with his thumb the way he had been going along it before.

He did it again at about the eleventh hour. Nothing came out of that pocket either time, and the chalk in it is a piece of chalk and has been a piece of chalk for a week, and no hand in that row reached for it and no mouth in that row asked him about it.

**"It is in there and it is going to be in there this evening, and nobody in this row has asked me for it and nobody has asked me why I am not giving it to them. I have not said a word to anybody about that pocket this morning and I am not going to say a second word about it this evening either," ** said the stallholder, in the middle of that bench with his own hand down the side of his coat.

He said it once, and the keeper went on with her own page. Nobody in that room told him that it was wanted and nobody in that room told him that it was not.

---

By the light's going the gap at the head of the keeper's leaf was not the width it had been at the light.

It had dried. The two pages that had come away from one another in the night had gone back into one another over about three days of damp and drying. The edge of the topmost of them lay down on the one under it the whole length of the head of that leaf, with no daylight at all between them along about the whole of a hand of it. The cloth along the head of that leaf could not go down into a gap that was not there, and the topmost page had to be lifted at its outer corner to be turned, and she lifted it at its outer corner for the rest of that flood.
