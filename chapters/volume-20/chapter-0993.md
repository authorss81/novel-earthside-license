# Chapter 0993 — She Carries That Stool Up That Stair Again

The woman of about fifty-two set that stool down against the wall along the far side of that room, went the length of that room to the far end of that bench, and stood with her own back to the wood behind the far end of it and her own hands at the sides of her dress, and she left that stool standing where she had put it.

It was Sunday, the six hundred and seventy-eighth day of the Bare Month. The night had been hard and clear for the eleventh morning running and the sun came up over eleven miles of flats a little after the light and stood over the roofs at the far end of that market before it came over the ridge of them. The air stood white and close under the awning at the store's end of that row until about the eighth hour. Four hundred yards of flags ran from the store's end of that row to where the row gave out into the side of a yard, and the crust where the carting has been stood pale and hard in the middle of the walking spaces.

That stool has been carried up that stair and down it every morning since the water came and it has not been sat on once. It stands against the wood of that wall a foot off where it stood before she began carrying it, and the grit in the joints of the flags inside the strip between the foot of it and the wall stands up about the depth of a grain of rice and nothing has walked on any part of that strip. The woman of about fifty-two came up that stair at about the fifth hour with the stool carried in her own two hands and set it down in the same place she sets it down and has set it down every morning since she came off that wall.

---

The rest of that room was doing what it does.

The keeper was at the far end of that bench with her own register open in front of her, and the second book lay open beside it with its cover slab drawn back off the leaf under it and the dry cloth along the head of that leaf. The sheet lay on the wood beside them with three lines on it a hand's width in from its right edge, and the space below the third of those lines was bare and stayed bare through that day. She went down that column of figures and came back up it. There is a day standing against each of the six strokes in it and not one of the six struck out, and the seventh place at the foot of that column is bare wood of the colour of the six above it.

She squared her own leaf against the fold with the flat of her own hand and lifted the topmost page of it at its outer corner and put it down square again, and did the same again before the light had got off the roof line.

The man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board came up at the fifth hour with his own board under his arm and the cloth over his own shoulder and put the board down on the near end of that bench with its face out. He came down the face of that board with the cloth, starting at the top of the top column, and the cloth came off the wood at the foot of the middle column, where the middle of that board stands a thumb deeper than the two ends of it. He tried it a second time with the weight of his own arm on it and it stopped in the same place. He folded the cloth over his own shoulder, took his own board down that stair to his own trestle two along, and brought it back up with nothing in his own hands.

The man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall two stalls along came up about the ninth hour and put his own hand flat on the top rail of that bench before he sat down on it. He took the middle of it with his own board flat across his own knees, the bare back of it to that room, and worked along the foot of that board with his own thumb from the top of the middle of it to the foot of it and back up again, twice.

The heap of shavings that had lain at the foot of his own trestle was gone off the walking space, and the earth round the foot of that trestle was packed flat and pale where the shavings had been lying on it. The near corner of that flag was standing up on its edge about the depth of two fingers with nothing under it holding it up.

The man of about thirty-one who carries things for a living came up at about the sixth hour and took that near wall with the satchel up under his own arm and the flap of it away from his own side. At about the middle of the morning he took the satchel down off that wall and set it on the flags against the skirting on one of the two paler places that stand along it, about the length of a forearm and about the shape of that satchel. The flap stayed down and he turned about off that wall and looked at the flags and turned about again.

---

The man of about twenty-seven went down that stair with the bucket four times and brought it up four times, and the water stood flat in it every one of those four. On the last of them he stayed on the flags and stood with his own hand on the outside of the rim of that trough.

The carrier stopped at that step on his way past and looked at the water standing in the bucket.

**"That bucket comes up four times and every one of those four times it is the same bucket with about as much in it as it had at the last, and there is a hole out in that yard that would take as much as that in about a quarter of an hour if a man would sit down on his own heels in it," ** said the man of about twenty-seven, at the top of that step with his own hand on the rim of that bucket.

The carrier said one ordinary thing back about the length of that walk and went on up that stair with the satchel in his own hand, and neither of them said anything else about it.

The block of writing was on the wall along the far side of that room and it stood there with the plaster either side of it the colour it had been for eleven days. Nobody said one word about it that day, and no hand in that room went near it at any hour of it, and every person who came up that stair that morning looked at the bench and the register and the sheet on the bench and not at that wall.

---

The woman of about fifty-two carried that stool down that stair at the middle of the afternoon and up it again, and she was not carrying anything else up or down it before the light went or after it.

That bar lay along the top step of that stair out of its two sockets, and the two of them were empty in the far side of the door below. There was a day in chalk on the outside of that door, and the chalk of it crossed the joint between the two planks and about a finger's width of that writing was lost down in the groove of it. Below that day the bare board stood a thumb clear of the near plank with one mark on it.

---

The top of that stool was dark with the damp it had come up that stair on, and where her own hand had been on it in the morning there was a ring about the size of a coin that was dry, and the ring stood out pale against the wood inside it.
