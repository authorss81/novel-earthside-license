# Chapter 0981 — Three Lines On The Sheet Before It Was Asked

The sheet had been folded in three and opened out again twice over before the light, and it lay on the wood of that bench at the far end of that room with the creases standing up in it and its two ends not square with one another.

It was Tuesday, the six hundred and sixty-sixth day of the Bare Month. The wind had gone into the north-east again in the night and had come down by the light to something that could not lift a corner of a cloth, and the air over eleven miles of flats stood close under the awning at the store's end of that row until about the seventh hour. The sun came over the roof line at the far end of that market at about the fifth hour, and by six it was in under the awning at the store's end and standing along that bench, and it never got past the middle of the wood all morning.

The bar lay along the top step of that stair out of its two sockets, where it has lain every morning of that flood. Beside it, the trough on the flags had ice in it about the depth of a thumb, and a thread of water was coming out of the crack in the near end of it and going across the flags and over the lip of the step. The day stood out in chalk on the outside of the door at the foot of that stair. Under the day the bare piece of board stood clear of the near plank by the width of a thumb, with one mark on it, and the two sticks inside the box of chalk lay one across the other where they had lain since before the water came. About four people a day went along the walking space at the foot of that stair while the light was on the flags.

The woman of about fifty-two came up that stair at about the fifth hour and went to the far end of that bench, where she had stood every morning since the morning she came off that wall, with her own hands at the sides of her dress. The man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board had the near end of that bench with his own board lying face out on it and the cloth over his own shoulder.

Adrian Vale had both his own hands flat on that sheet, and the wood of it lay under his wrists, and he had been in that place since the fourth hour.

---

She said what it cost her before she said what it was.

She said it out loud in that room in daylight with the date in chalk on the outside of that door behind her own shoulder, and no mouth in that room said a word back to her about any part of it.

**"I am going to put a thing to the woman at the far end of this bench this morning and I am going to say what it costs me before I say what it is. It costs me that I am the one who puts it on that paper. It costs me that she is on it whatever she says to me and whatever she does not, and that the paper is on this bench at the going of the light with the two of us standing in it, and neither of us able to take anything off it. I have known that since before I came up these stairs. Nobody in this room has to do anything about it, and I am not going to tell anybody in this room that they are right about it," ** said the woman of about twenty-nine who keeps a public register, at the far end of that bench with her own hand on the sheet and her register open in front of her and the second book lying open beside it.

That was the first of the morning and she did not come back to it before the light went or after it.

Then she took a pencil out of the pocket of her dress. It was a stub of one about the length of a finger with the point gone blunt against the wood of the box it had lived in. She squared the sheet with her own hand and wrote on it, and she wrote three lines and no more.

The first line was the day. The second line was who it was about. The third line was the thing she meant to put to her, and it was one line long, and it said in the plainest words she had: whether there was a fire for her at the end of that row.

Adrian Vale read those three lines with his own hands flat on the sheet and the sheet lying face up under them. He read them from the head of the sheet down the way a person reads a page, and he read the third line twice.

She wrote them a hand's width in from the right edge of that sheet, because his own two hands were on the wood at the left of it and she did not ask him to move them and he did not move them. Those three lines were not square with that sheet at the light's going, and the edge of the paper on the far side of them was clear for the width of a palm.

He wanted to see one thing written about himself before it was put to anybody, so that he could read it and know it was on its way. There was nothing in those three lines about him. There was a day, and a woman with a tin, and the end of a row of trestles.

---

The woman of about sixty-nine with her tin came up that stair at about the seventh hour with the tin under her right arm and her own stick in her own hand, and she came the length of that room and stopped at the far end of that bench.

Coming up that row she had stopped at the trestle of the man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board and read the middle column of that board across, three lines of it from the left of it to the right of it, the way a person reads a line of anything, and had gone on up the row.

The keeper turned the sheet round on the wood so that the three lines of it faced her.

She read it, and it was the first time that anybody had been shown what was going to be put to them before it was put to them.

She read it with her own thumb under the second line and her own hand flat at the near edge of it. She read it again. Then she answered.

She answered out loud in that room and the answering took about as long as the reading had. What she said went out of that room in her own mouth. It went down that stair with her and along that row and out past the store's end of it. No leaf on either of those two books carries a scratch of it, and there is no board in that market and no chalk anywhere in this city that carries one word of it.

Nobody in that room repeated it to anybody. Nobody in that row heard it. The keeper put her own hand flat on the page of her register and went down the column that holds figures, and there is a day standing against each of the six strokes in it and not one of the six struck out.

The tradesman took the cloth off his own shoulder and came down the face of his own board with it from the top of the top column, and the cloth came off the face at the foot of the middle column, where it stops every morning, and he folded it over his own shoulder and did the same again. The man of about twenty-seven carried the bucket down that stair and up it three times with the water standing flat in it, and the ice in the trough at the top of that step stayed whole. The woman of about fifty-two stood at the far end of that bench with her own hands at the sides of her dress and took no part in that morning at all.

No mouth in that room said Adrian Vale's name at any point of that day. Nobody in it was waiting for him to do anything, and his own two hands were of no use to any of them, and he went on being a man at a bench with a sheet under them that had three lines on it and not one of them about him.

---

Outside, on the bare piece of board under the day chalked on the outside of that door, there is one mark.

It is not a column to be read from the head of it down. It is not a line of writing to be read across from one end of it to the other. There is one stroke on that board about two fingers in from the near edge of it, thick at the top and thin at the bottom, and there is no direction in it that a person could read a thing in, and nothing has ever been read off it either way.

Three grains of graphite came off the point of that pencil while the keeper was writing. They stood on the wood of the bench at the far end of that room in a line about the length of a grain of rice, and they were the only thing on that bench that morning that had come off a thing a person was holding in her own hand.
