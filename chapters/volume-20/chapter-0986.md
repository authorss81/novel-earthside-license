# Chapter 0986 — Out Past The Store's End To That Yard

It had rained hard in the night and stopped a little after the light, and the water was standing in the low place in the stone at the side of that yard about the depth of two fingers.

It was Sunday, the six hundred and seventy-first day of the Bare Month. Four hundred yards of flags ran from the store's end of that market row to where the row gave out into the side of that yard, and there was not a dry joint in the whole length of them. The walking spaces of them were pale and hard in the middle, with the wet standing in the joints about the depth of a finger. The air came off eleven miles of flats behind that rain and stood close along the walking spaces and thinned about the ninth hour.

The man of about twenty-seven filled the bucket at the store's end of that row and put the rag folded over the rim of it and carried it the whole way.

That is about nine hundred paces out past the store's end of the row and along four hundred yards of flags to where they give out into the side of that yard. He went along the walking spaces and not the joints. He went past the trestle of the man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board and past four trestles with nothing at them, and the man of about thirty-nine had his own board face out on his own trestle with the cloth over his own shoulder and did not look up.

---

What is at the end of that walk is a yard about as deep as a room is wide, with a stack of boards standing along the far side of it and a tally-board lying face up on the top of that stack.

The tally-board has two lines of grey grit lying across the face of it from one edge to the other. They had been there before the light and they were there at the going of the light, and nobody in that yard counted them and nobody turned that board over. The grit had come off the top of that stack in the night rain and had settled in two lines across the face of it and had stayed where it settled.

The man of about thirty-eight at that salt wharf had that stack for his own, with his barrow standing behind him and the shafts of it up, and his knife in his belt. The store door at that wharf stood a hand's breadth across and had stood that far across for as long as anybody in that yard could remember. The boards of it have gone dark along the foot of them in a band about the depth of two fingers, where the damp of that wharf has been standing against the inside of them all that flood.

He came out to the edge of the walking space with his own shoulder and stood a while and watched the other man bring the bucket in.

**"You will get about a quarter of it out of that hole and the hole will be the same hole by the time you have got your breath," ** said the man of about thirty-eight at that salt wharf, at the edge of the walking space with his own hands down his own sides.

The man of about twenty-seven put the bucket down on the flags and set his own foot against the side of it. Neither of them said anything else, and the man of about thirty-eight went back to the stack and stood at the end of it with his own arm along the top of it.

He worked at the near end of that stack for about half an hour while the other man was at the bucket. He had the boards squared off at the end of it, one at a time, on the flags, and he set each one down flat after he had squared it and walked on to the next. The squared ends went along the yard in a row and came to the edge of the grit at the low place. He put the ninth of them down with its squared end over the water there for about as long as it takes to say something twice, then took it out and put it on the stack with the others.

---

He put the bucket into the low place in the stone and it went down about a hand's depth before it found the bottom of it.

---

The water in the low place was clear and cold and about the depth of two fingers, and there was a film of grit on the stone at the bottom of it that came up on the rim of the bucket and stood on the wood about the depth of a grain of rice. He pulled the bucket up and about a quarter of it was in it, and the hole was about the same size it had been before, because a bucket takes what it can hold and does not take the hole with it.

He carried it back to the side of that yard with the weight of it in his own right arm and his own left hand on the handle, and got it up onto the flags. Then he stood a while with it where it stood and put the rag on the wet face of the stone at the side of that low place.

The rag went down flat on the wet and came away with about half of what the rag had carried in the grit on it, and the stone under it came dry in a patch about the size of a hand. He looked at the patch. He put the rag on a second patch, lower down, and the stone under that one came dry too. Then he picked the rag up and carried the bucket out past the flags with the rag folded over the rim of it again and the rim of it wet all the way round and dry underneath.

Nobody at that wharf said anything about either of those two patches, and the man of about thirty-eight went on standing at the end of that stack with his own arm along the top of it and did not come over to that side of that yard again.

---

What he had walked out to that yard for was about a quarter of a bucket of water, and he had it. He carried it back up that row the whole way with his own arm taking most of the weight of it. About two hundred paces from the store's end his own forearm began to be sorry about it, and he shifted it to the other side without stopping. The walking spaces of that row were hard that morning and his boots found them all, and the water in the bucket moved against the side of it at every step he took and had gone round twice before he came to the store's end.

At the store's end of that row the trestles stood with nothing at them. The ice in the trough at the top of that step was whole about the depth of a thumb, with the crack in its near end running its thread across the flags, and nothing had been fetched for it that day and nothing had been broken in it. The man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall two stalls along had his own board face in against the wood of his own trestle there, the bare back of it turned out into the row behind his own shoulder. He did not look up from the foot of his own trestle as the other man came past him with the bucket.

---

The rim of that bucket stood on the stone at the store's end of that row at the going of the light with the rag folded over it, and the wet had got up into the wood of the rim from the inside about the depth of a grain of rice and had come up through it in a ring, and the wood under that ring stood the colour of the wood at the bottom of the bucket.
