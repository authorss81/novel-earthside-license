# Chapter 0959 — Grit Out Of A Worn Place

Both of a man's hands went down flat on the stone of the ninth step down from the top of that stair and went over the worn place in the middle of it, and they stayed in it. That step was the ninth one down and there were two more under it before the flags, so it was neither the bottom step nor the top step, and the trough was a long way further up.

It was Monday, the six hundred and forty-fourth day of the Bare Month. The morning came up white and dry with the wind gone round to the east and the light in that market thin until the sun got over the roof line, and then it came along the row in one stripe and stopped about the middle of the bench in the room over the market. The bar lay along the top step of that stair with the frost gone out of it, and the two sockets on the far side of the door below stood empty. The day was chalked on the outside of that door and the bare board under it carried one mark. On the table inside the room the box of chalk stood with its lid off at one corner.

Adrian Vale was at the ninth step from the top with his hands on the worn place in the middle of it. The man of about thirty-one who carries things for a living was on the step below him with the satchel against his ribs. The woman of about fifty-two was at the bottom of that stair on the bottom step, which is the eleventh and the last of them. The keeper was at the far end of that bench with her register open. The tradesman was at the near end with his board propped against the end of the wood and the cloth over his shoulder.

---

That step is worn in the middle of it and nowhere else, and the wear is about the width of a heel and about the length of a heel, and it has come out of the stone because every boot that goes up and down that stair goes over the same square of it. The grit of the flags had been walked into that wear all that week, and the wet had gone into it and packed it. It stood there then about the depth of a nail and hard as a crust. A boot going over that place in the wet does not slip on it. A boot going over it dry goes along on the grit and does not touch the stone at all.

He got his own thumbnail into the near edge of that packed grit and worked it out a piece at a time for the better part of a quarter of an hour. It came away in flakes about the size of a coin and it went down the two steps onto the flags inside the room and stood there in a small heap at the foot of the ninth step. When the middle of that step was clear he put his own hand flat down on it again and the stone under his hand was smooth and cold and worn a shade paler than the stone either side of the place, and there was nothing else in that room that had changed.

**"There is a week's grit packed into the wear in the middle of that step and it is hard as a crust, and a boot in the wet goes along on the top of it and does not touch the stone at all. I have got the satchel up under my own arm with the flap down and I have not opened it this morning, and nobody in this room has asked me why I open nothing I carry."** said the man of about thirty-one who carries things for a living, on the step below him, with the satchel up against his own ribs.

Adrian Vale did not answer him and the man of about thirty-one who carries things for a living did not say a second thing about it, and neither of them put a hand on the flake-heap at the foot of that step.

---

The man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall two stalls along came up that stair at about the seventh hour with his own board under his arm. He went over the middle of the ninth step in the dry and did not slip and did not notice. He sat down in the middle of that bench and went along the foot of his own board with his own thumb. The woman of about fifty-two came up off the bottom step a little after that. Going up, she stopped on the ninth step with her own hand on the frame of the door and looked down at the middle of it. She stood there longer than she meant to. Then she went on up and put her own hands at the sides of her dress at her own wall, and did not say one word about it to anybody.

The tradesman came down that stair and went over the same place going down and came back up again and did not stop.

**"That bench is standing a foot further along toward that wall than it stood at the light and it was not me that moved it, and there is grit under it that has not been touched and there is grit on the place it came off that has been pressed flat. I am going to be at this end of it all morning and I am not going to be the one that puts it back."** said the keeper, with her register open on the wood in front of her and her own hand flat on the page.

**"The middle column of my own board comes down about the depth of a thumb further than it did in the spring and the cloth has stopped on it in the same place it has stopped every morning of that flood, and there is a rate at the foot of it that wants a day against it the way it wanted one yesterday and the day before. I have not given it one and I am not going to, and that step you have been picking at is not going to make me."** said the man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall two stalls along, in the middle of that bench with his own board across his knees.

There was a thing Adrian Vale wanted and he did not get it. He had asked one thing once in that room and it was not written down anywhere and it had not been answered, and he had come up that stair since then carrying it in his own head with nothing on any surface about it, and he wanted somebody else to put it again and mean it the second time. He did not say one word about it that morning, and nobody in that room put anything to him at any point of the day, and nobody in that room said his name, and nobody came up that stair to stop on it and put anything to him.

---

He went down that stair at about the eleventh hour and went along the row past the store's end and out to where the flags give out into the side of a yard, and he was not on that row on anybody's account and nobody asked him where he had gone. The tin was not in that room that day and he did not have it. The sill of that window stood with the two shapes pressed into the dust of it and he went past it and did not look at it, and the chair was where he had put it, against the side wall, with the sheet of road still folded on the chair.

---

By the light's going the middle of that ninth step from the top was showing the stone. It showed about the width of a hand and about the length of a hand and it showed a shade paler than the stone either side of it, because the stone that has had a week's grit packed into it and taken off it again is not the stone that has had a boot standing on it bare for eleven years. The flakes of that grit were still standing where they had fallen in a heap about as big as two fists at the foot of that step, and they had not been trodden down. That heap lay a foot to one side of the middle of the step and against the riser where the stone of it stands out past the flags, and every boot that went up and down that stair all day went over the middle of it and came down on the other side of it, and not one of them went near that heap.
