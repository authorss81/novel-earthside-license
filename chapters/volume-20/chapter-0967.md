# Chapter 0967 — A Stool Moved A Foot Further Out

A hand went flat on the top of a stool that stood against a far wall and pushed it a foot further out from where it had been standing, and set it down, and stood looking at where it had been.

It was Tuesday, the six hundred and fifty-second day of the Bare Month. The rain had stopped in the night and the flags of that row were wet through and the light came up hard and white off them and into that market before the trestles had any shade to give it. That light came in under the awning at the store's end and lay along that bench and stopped about the middle of the wood, and the far wall of that room stood in the shade of the stair and has stood in the shade of the stair every morning of that flood.

The stool stood against the far wall of that room a foot off a woman's skirt, and it had stood there every morning since before this flood, and nobody had ever sat down on it and nobody had ever asked the woman it belonged to why she had carried it out of the room it came out of.

---

She was not in that room for the first three quarters of an hour of it.

She went down that stair at about the fifth hour and stood on the strip of ground at the foot of it with her own shoulder against the frame of the door and the wind off the flats going at the back of her dress, and no mouth in that room asked her where she had gone. That room does not count anybody and does not measure that strip of ground and has never put a name or a figure on it.

That left the man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board alone in that room with the stool for a good part of three quarters of an hour.

He stood at the near end of that bench with his own board propped against the end of the wood and the cloth over his own shoulder, and after a while he was not looking at his board. He was looking at that stool standing against that far wall a foot off where a skirt ought to be, with nobody standing near it and nothing on it and nothing under it. The dust stood up around the leg of it, where the skirt of that woman had been against the wood on every morning of that flood.

He did not put his own hand on it. He did not move it, and he did not turn it round, and he did not go over and look at it from the other side. He stood and looked at the dust that stood up in a ring round the near leg of it, which is the shape a person makes who has been standing in one place all day. He thought about a room where a stool is brought out of another room and put against a wall and never sat on, and he did not get as far as any conclusion about it, and he did not say one word about it out loud.

What he did do, after a while, was go and get the end of the cloth off the face of his own board and look at the floor, and then come back and put the cloth where it had been and fold it over his own shoulder again. The floor was flags and the grit was standing up in the joints of them, and it was the same grit that stands in the joints of the floor at the near end of that bench. He stood on it while he was thinking about it, and put his own boot down on two joints of it and took them off again.

There is nothing in that room that says where the stool came from except the woman it belongs to, and she is not a person who has ever been asked anything about where she was before she came up that stair, and no mouth in that room opened itself on the subject while she was out of it.

---

She came back up that stair at about the eighth hour and went to her own far wall, and found that stool where she had left it with the dust standing up round the near leg of it.

She moved it herself. She put her two hands on the top of it and lifted it clear of the floor and carried it a foot further out along that wall and set it down. Where it had been standing there was clear floor, and the place her own skirt had been standing against that wall all that flood was bare wood with the dust taken off it.

Nobody in that room asked her why. The keeper went on with her own page and the tradesman went on with the face of his own board at the far end of that bench, and neither of them said one word about a stool being moved a foot further out from where it had stood.

**"That stool came out of a room with me and it has been against this wall since before the water and I have not sat down on it and I am not going to. I have put it a foot further out because I stand here and I was against it, and that is the whole of the reason and it is not a reason anybody is going to be satisfied with."** said the woman of about fifty-two, at that wall with her hands down the sides of her dress.

She said it once and did not say it again that day.

The keeper came up that stair at about the ninth hour and went to the far end of that bench, and the stool stood a foot further out from that wall by then, and she looked at it and at the bare place on the wall where it had been and did not say anything about either of them. The tradesman said one word about it, out loud, to himself and not to anybody.

**"A foot further out."** the tradesman said it at the near end of that bench with the cloth over his own shoulder and went back to the face of his own board and said nothing else about it before the light went or after.

---

By the light's going that stool stood a foot further out from that wall than it had stood at the light, with its near leg on grit that had not been walked on and its two back legs on the grit that had been under it all that flood.

On the flags inside that room where that near leg had been standing there was a clean square about the size of a hand and a bit more. The dust had come away with the leg of it and had gone under the stool with the lifting. The square was the colour of flags that have not had a week's standing against them on them, and it stood out against the walking space by about as much as a fresh chalk mark on a board stands out.
