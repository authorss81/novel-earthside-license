# Chapter 0980 — Nineteen Rates And About Four Who Read Them

The man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall two stalls along had come the length of that row at the light and had set his own board face in against the wood of his own trestle with the bare back of it turned out into the row behind his own shoulder. He had gone along the foot of it with his own thumb once, with the face of it standing against that wood and nothing of it showing to anybody going past.

It was Monday, the six hundred and sixty-fifth day of the Bare Month. The sun was up before the light and came over the roof line at the far end of that market by the fourth hour, and came along the row with the shade of the trestles going ahead of it. Four hundred yards of flags ran from the store's end of the row to where they gave out into the side of a yard, and the walking spaces were pale and hard in the middle and black in the joints, the way they had been for a week. The board on the outside wall at the far end of that market stood on its one nail above its step with the hole of a second nail empty above it. The wood around the lip of that hole was soft about the width of a nail all the way round it, and there was nothing in it.

The man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board had his own trestle with the face of his own board out on it, and there are nineteen rates on that face in three columns.

---

About four people a day got a thing off that board. About four people a day went along that walking space and got nothing off it at all.

A man going up that row at about the sixth hour stood in front of that board and read down the middle column of it from the top to the foot and went on. A woman going the other way stopped at it and asked the man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board, out loud in that row, what the rate at the foot of the middle column was. He told her, and she said it back to him once to see whether she had it right, and he told her it was right. She went on her own way with it. A boy came down that row and looked at the face of that board for as long as it takes to read two lines and went on without stopping at it. A man went up with his own hands full and did not turn his head at it.

That is the whole of what that board does in that row on a morning. The man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board has never been asked to write anything else on it, and nobody has ever stood in that row and counted a figure that was not already there.

The middle column comes down about the depth of a thumb further than the two either side of it. There are rates down all three of them, and not one of the nineteen has a day standing against it anywhere on that face. At the foot of the middle column there is a space about two fingers wide, the wood inside it paler than the wood either side of it, and nothing standing in that space. Above the space is a rate which wants a day against it the way it wanted one the day before and the day before that. The cloth comes down the face of the board every morning from the top of the top column and stops at the foot of the middle column. Along the foot of that column, below the space and running the width of it and a little beyond it at each end, there is a line about the length of a hand where the wood is darker than the wood either side of it. That line is where the edge of the cloth stands every morning.

---

**"Somebody will come along this row in the next day or two and stand in front of that board and ask what that rate at the foot of the middle column is, and the man it belongs to will tell him, and it will be the same answer that man gave that woman this morning. The chalk is in my own pocket and it has been in there all that flood, and it is not coming out of it this morning. I could walk two stalls along and put a figure in that space before the light went off that board, because I have the hand for it and the chalk to do it with. And the whole of it comes to nothing, because that board is not mine and nobody at that trestle has asked me to write on it, and a figure of mine put in there would be about me and not about the day," ** said the man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall two stalls along, with the bare back of his own board turned out into that row and his own hand down the side of his own coat.

He said it once into that row. Nobody in that row asked him about the space at the foot of that column, and he went back to his own trestle and put his own thumb along the foot of his own board, which was face in against the wood of it, and the bare back of it stood out into the row while he did it.

At about the eleventh hour the man of about thirty-one who carries things for a living came along that row with the satchel up under his own arm and the flap of it hanging away from his own side. He stopped in front of that board and read down the top of the middle column about three lines, read it again, and went on along the row. The chalk stayed in the inside breast pocket of the man who keeps a stall two stalls along.

At the store's end of that row the woman of about sixty-nine with her tin had the tin standing on the walking space with the lid of it on it and her own thumb off the lid. The stick was in the pocket of her skirt. She picked the tin up and went up the row with it under her arm. The man of about twenty-seven had the step at the top of that row with the bucket on the stone beside his own foot and the rag folded over the rim of it. The ice in the trough was whole about the depth of a thumb, and the crack in its near end was running its thread across the flags.

---

By the time the light had got off the roof line at the far end of that market, that face had been read down and read across about as many times as it gets read in a day. The wood inside that space at the foot of the middle column stood pale and empty in the open, and the dark line where the edge of the cloth stands ran under it the width of a hand and a little beyond it at each end.

In the inside breast pocket of the man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall two stalls along the chalk was where it has been all that flood, and the cloth had come down the face of the board two stalls along twice that day and stopped at the foot of the middle column both times.
