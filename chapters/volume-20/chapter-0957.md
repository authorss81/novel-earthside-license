# Chapter 0957 — A Thing Of His Own At His Stall

Two hands lay flat on his own trestle at his own stall two stalls along, spread about the width of the wood, and the board that is face in against that trestle had its bare back turned out into the row behind his own shoulder.

It was Saturday, the six hundred and forty-second day of the Bare Month. The wind had gone into the south in the night and the light came up that market early and came along the row before the row had woken, and by the time the sun was on the trestles the flags had gone pale and hard in the middle of the walking spaces. The trough at the top of the step had ice in it and the crack in its near end was running a thread across the flags away over the grit. Out on the top step of that stair the bar lay along the stone where it has lain every morning of this flood, and the two sockets it belongs to are on the far side of the door below and are empty. The chalk was in a box on the table and the lid of that box stood off at one corner.

The man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall two stalls along had his own stall and his own trestle and his own board face in against the wood of the trestle. Nine feet off him, at the near end of the bench in that room over the market, the woman of about twenty-nine who keeps a public register had the date in chalk on the outside of the door at the foot of that stair standing behind her own shoulder, and the two books lay open on the bench in front of her.

---

She said it before anything else that morning. She had been in that room since the fourth hour and she had not sat down.

**"I am going to put a thing to somebody in this room this morning and I am going to say what it costs before I say what it is, and the cost is that it goes out of this room in my mouth and there is no page in this city with it on, and there is not going to be one, and there is not one for me to put it on. That person will remember what he was asked or he will remember what he said back, and the two of those are not the same thing, and there is no way on any surface anybody has to show which of the two it was. I know that before I open my mouth and I am saying it before I do anything else because after it is said I cannot take it back quietly. Nobody has to do anything about it and I am not going to tell anybody they were right or wrong."** said the woman of about twenty-nine who keeps a public register, at the far end of that bench, with the two books open in front of her and the date on the door behind her own shoulder.

No mouth in that room said a word back to her about it, and nobody in that room thanked her. She sat down at the far end of that bench and put her own hand flat on the page in front of her and went on with her own page.

---

The morning went. The tradesman came and went at the near end of that bench with his own board and the cloth over his own shoulder and did not ask her what she had said. The carrier stood against the near wall and did not ask her. The woman of about fifty-two stayed against the far wall with the stool standing a foot off her own skirt. The man of about twenty-seven carried the bucket down the nine steps and up them twice with the water standing flat in it, and nobody in that room asked him anything about the crack in the near end of that trough and nobody offered him anything. The stallholder came up the nine steps at about the tenth hour with his own board under his arm and the bare back of it turned out into the row, and went along that bench and sat in the middle of it, and he did not know yet that anybody had said anything about him.

**"It is about your own stall and it is about your own board and it is about nothing else and it is about no day that is not your own. I have put nothing on any page about it and I am not going to. I am putting it to you in front of the room because you would not take it standing at your own trestle in this row. I would not have put it to anybody else in here in your place. That is not a reason that is going to be written down either."** said the woman of about twenty-nine who keeps a public register, at the far end, with the date on the outside of that door standing behind the shoulder of her.

He answered her.

There was no keeping of it in that room. Nobody in that room was ever going to have it. What he said went out of the room with him, down the nine steps and along that row to his own trestle. What was asked went out of that room in the other direction and off past the bottom step. No leaf in either of those two books carries any part of either of them. The keeper's own page stands with the dry cloth along the head of it exactly where it stood at the light.

Nobody in that room thanked anybody for it. Nothing whatever was done about it before the light went or after it.

---

He was not asked a second thing about it. The tradesman had come away from that bench. He was standing at the bottom step of that stair inside the door, with his own board stood against his own knee and his own hand on the wood of the end of the bench above him. He stayed down there for the rest of that morning and did not come up to the near end again. The near end of that bench stood with nothing against it from about the eleventh hour, and there were people standing in that room behind it, and it had not stood empty like that before in the whole of that flood.

**"That is all of it, and I am not saying it again to anybody in this room or out of it."** said the man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall two stalls along, at his own place on that bench, with his own board flat across his knees.

---

By the light's going the two books were lying open side by side on that bench. The second of them lay with its cover slab drawn back and the leaf under it showing flat. The register lay open at the other end of the wood with the dry cloth along the head of it. Nothing had come into that room onto either of them that day. The near end of that bench had a week of dust standing on it. The week of it had been broken in one place by one hand that had been on the same square of that wood for most of a morning. There was a print of it in the dust about as long as a hand, four fingers at the top of it and the heel of the palm at the bottom. The dust stood up in a rim round the print where the hand had not been. On the outside of the door at the foot of that stair the bare piece of board under the date carried the one mark that was on it when the morning began, and the wall along the far side of that room had nothing on it at all.
