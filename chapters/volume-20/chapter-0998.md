# Chapter 0998 — Two Hands On The Bed And Neither Letting Go

The man of about sixty got the shaft of that handcart up off the walking space and let it back down onto its own prop at the store's end of that row, and took his own two hands off the wood of it and stood beside it.

It was Friday, the six hundred and eighty-third day of the Bare Month. It had rained in the night of that day and stopped before the light, and the crust of that ground out past the last named house had gone soft on the top of it again in about an hour. Four hundred yards of flags ran from the store's end of that row to where that row gave out into the side of a yard, and the walking spaces of them stood pale and hard with the wet standing in the joints about the depth of a finger. The air came off eleven miles of flats behind that rain and stood close under the awning at the store's end until about the ninth hour.

The trough at the store's end of that row had ice in it about the depth of a thumb and the crack in its near end was quiet after the first hour. The rag lay folded on the stone lip of that step, and the thread of water came over the lip of the step and stopped in the joint of the flags a foot short of the walking space. That bar lay along the top step of that stair and not in its two sockets, and it has lain out of the weather on that step every morning of that flood. The board on the outside wall at the far end of that market stood on its one nail above its step with the hole of a second nail empty above it, and there was nothing in that hole and nothing went into it.

---

That handcart had come up the four hundred yards of that row from out past the last named house that morning, one wheel at a time up the walking spaces. It stood at the store's end of that row across the near walking space with the shaft up and the bed of it level.

On the bed of it there is a coil of rope and a hazel mallet with its head bound in cord, and under a rope across the boards there is a load, and the boards of the bed are bowed about the middle of it by the weight. Nobody in that row looked at what was under that rope and nobody in that row asked what was in that cart.

The man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board had his own trestle two along with his own board face out on it and the cloth over his own shoulder, and he came down the face of his own board with the cloth and the cloth stopped at the foot of the middle column where it stops every morning. The man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall two stalls along had his own board face in against the wood of his own trestle at the store's end of that row, and the near corner of the walking space at the foot of that trestle was standing up on its edge about the depth of two fingers, with the pale damp packed earth under it.

The man of about twenty-seven was at the step at the top of that row all morning, and the bucket stood on the stone beside his own foot with its own handle turned to the wall.

---

The man of about thirty-one who carries things for a living came along that row at about the eighth hour and stopped at the store's end of it in front of that cart.

He wanted it out to the yard at the far end of that row. He had a thing to put into that yard and he had come along that row to put it in and he had got as far as the store's end of it before he saw that the cart was standing across the walking space he wanted.

The man of about sixty wanted it out along the flats past the last named house, and he said so, and the man of about thirty-one said what he wanted it for and where he wanted it to go. Neither of them put anything to the other one about any of it, and neither of them gave an account of where that cart had been or what had been done to it.

Then the man of about thirty-one put his own hands on the end rail of that bed about as wide as his own two hands together, and the man of about sixty kept his own hands on the shaft of that cart, and neither of them let go.

---

What a cart does between two men who each want it going a different way is stand still.

That cart stood across the walking space at the store's end of that row with the weight of the two of them on it and neither of them giving way. The coil of rope and the mallet did not shift on the bed of it, and the boards under the rope across them did not creak. About four people a day went along that row on their own business, and the light was on it when they went. Two of them looked at those two men and that cart and could have said afterwards that neither of them had let go of it, and two of them went past with their own eyes on the walking spaces and could not have said what was standing at the store's end of that row. Nobody stopped.

Then the man of about thirty-one took his own hands off that end rail. The man of about sixty got the shaft of that cart up and turned it out of the walking space and set the wheel of it down on the crust of that ground past the trestles. The man of about thirty-one came back and took the end rail of that bed again from the other side of it than he had taken it from before.

They went out past the store's end of that row with a hand on the cart from either side and neither of them letting go of it.

They went the whole length of those four hundred yards of flags and out past the last named house and along the flats. The wheels of that cart went into the wet crust about the depth of the thickness of a hand and cut two lines in it as they went, and the water came up into those two lines and stood in them. The two of them did not say one word to each other for about as long as it takes to say something twice. Then the man of about thirty-one said one ordinary thing about the length of the ground and the man of about sixty said one ordinary thing back, and they went on with a hand on that cart from either side.

---

Nobody in that row asked the man of about thirty-one what was under the rope across the boards of that bed, and nobody in that row asked the man of about sixty where he had got the load from or where he was taking it. The man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall two stalls along got his own hand at the near foot of his own trestle and worked at it with the edge of his own board. About an inch came off the near side of that foot in shavings the length of a finger, and he put them in a heap at the foot of that walking space. The tradesman took his own board down off his own trestle and up again twice.

When the light had gone off that row there was a heap of shavings at the foot of that walking space with the grain of them lying one way, where a foot would go down beside it in the morning without seeing them, and about an inch was gone off the near side of that trestle foot in the length of a finger. That heap was the only thing on that walking space that had not been there at the light.
