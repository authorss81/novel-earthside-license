# Chapter 0978 — A Thing She Said Once Up That Row

The woman of about sixty-nine with her tin came along that row at about the sixth hour with the tin under her right arm and her own stick in her own hand, and she went past the store's end of that row instead of stopping at it.

It was Saturday, the six hundred and sixty-third day of the Bare Month. The night had been clear and hard for the fourth morning running and the sun was not up before the light. The air over eleven miles of flats stood white and close until about the eighth hour. Then it thinned along the ground and let the light come in under the ridge at the far end of that market at once. Four hundred yards of flags ran from the store's end of that row to where they gave out into the side of a yard, and the crust where the carting has been all that week stood pale and hard in the middle of the walking spaces.

The man of about twenty-seven had the step at the store's end of that row with the bucket on the stone beside his own foot and the rag folded over the rim of it. The man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall two stalls along had his own trestle with his own board face out on it and the cloth over his own shoulder. The man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board had his own trestle two along with his own board face out on it.

---

She went up the row with the stick in her own hand and did not set it into the grit anywhere.

She went the whole length of that row. She walked the walking spaces and not the joints, and she stopped at a dozen places, and at every one of those dozen places she stood with the end of the stick held down against the stone and her own thumb on the top of it, and she lifted it and carried on. There is a stroke in the grit of that row about the length of a hand at every joint along it, and the more of them have been made at every morning for a week. She stood over the whole length of that row that morning with the thing in her hand that makes them. She made none of them.

The man of about twenty-seven came out to the edge of the walking space at about the seventh hour with the bucket in his own hand to fill it.

**"You have got a bucket and you have got no handle on it a man can get a hand round, and it is going to cut the palm of you in about a week. I have had one of them with a bad handle for eleven years. I put a length of rope through the eye of it and made a loop, and it held for me and it will hold for you. I am not going to do it for you, because you have come down this row with your own arm full for a week and you have not asked anybody for anything, and I am not going to be the one who starts it," ** said the man of about twenty-seven, at the store's end of that row with the bucket in his own hand.

He said that and put the bucket down and filled it. She listened to all of it standing still with the stick down against the stone, and she did not say a word back to him about it, and he went on with what he had come out to do.

---

At about the middle of that row she said a thing out loud.

**"There is a place about the middle of this row where the light comes down between two of those trestles and lands in one piece and nowhere else, and it takes the sun until well over the roofs to get there, and the patch it makes is about the length of a good walk. I have walked this row since before the water came and I know that patch the way I know the back of my own hand. There has not been one person in this market in eleven years who has stopped in it, and nobody is going to stop in it this morning either, and I am not going to stand in it. That is the whole of what I have to say about it and I am not going to say it again today," ** the woman of about sixty-nine with her tin said at the joint about the middle of that row, with the stick in her own hand and the tin still under her other arm.

She said it out loud into that row with the trestles going on about their own business on either side of her, and it was said once, and she did not say it again before the light went or after it. Nothing was written down anywhere of what she said that morning, and she said it to nobody in particular. No leaf on any of the books in the room at the top of that stair carries one word of it, or one word of anything else she said in that row.

She came back down that row at about the middle of the morning with the stick still in her own hand. She went along the flags past the store's end out to where they give out into the side of a yard, and stood there a while looking at the ground on the far side of it where nothing stood. Then she came back along them and went past the store's end of that row again with the tin under her arm.

Nobody in that row put anything to her. The man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board went along the face of his own board with the cloth, and the cloth stopped at the foot of the middle column where it stops every morning. He folded it over his own shoulder, looked up that row once, and went back to his own trestle. The man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall two stalls along went along the foot of his own board with his own thumb twice, looked up that row twice, and went on with his own board either time. About four people a day went along that row on their own business and some of them looked at the old woman going up it with a tin under her arm and some of them did not.

---

The sun came over the roof line at the far end of that market about the middle of that morning and came along that row with the shade of the trestles going ahead of it. By the time that light had reached the store's end of that row, the shade of the awning there had gone off the walking spaces and come down onto the stone at the top of that step instead.

The bucket stood where it had been filled, full, with the rag folded over the rim of it. The ice in the trough beside it was whole about the depth of a thumb. The thread of water out of the crack in the near end of that trough was running across the stone of that step and off the bottom of it onto the flags.
