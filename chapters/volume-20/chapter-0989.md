# Chapter 0989 — She Named What It Cost Her And Then Asked

The air over eleven miles of flats stood white and close until about the eighth hour and did not come down inside that room until the light had got off the roof line at the far end of that market.

It was Wednesday, the six hundred and seventy-fourth day of the Bare Month. There had been no sun before the light and the wind had gone into the north and died there, and nothing in that row moved. There was ice in the trough on the top of that step about the depth of a thumb. The thread of water out of the crack in its near end had come over the lip of that step and stopped in the joint of the flags a foot short of the walking space, where it had stopped every morning of the week.

The bar lay where it has lain every morning of that flood, out of its two sockets on the top step. The block of writing was on the wall along the far side of that room. The sheet lay on the wood of that bench at the far end of it with three lines on it a hand's width in from its right edge and nothing under the third of them. The man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board had the near end of that bench with his own board face out on it and the cloth over his own shoulder. About four people a day went past the foot of that stair that morning in that cold.

---

The woman of about fifty-two came up that stair at about the fifth hour and went to the far end of that bench and stood with her own hands at the sides of her dress.

She said what it cost her before she said what it was, and she said it out loud in that room with the date in chalk on the outside of the door at the foot of that stair behind her own shoulder. No mouth in that room said a word back to her about any part of it.

**"I am going to put a thing to the man who came up that stair this morning and I am going to say what it costs me before I say what it is. It costs me that I have to stand in this room and say it out loud, and that I am asking a man about a column of figures in a book he has never once opened in his life. If he tells me, then I am the only person in this city who has it and I cannot show anybody where I got it. If he does not tell me, then he and I are two people who know nothing about it and neither of us can put the other right. That is what it costs, and I am saying it now because once it is said I cannot put it back quietly," ** said the woman of about fifty-two, at the far end of that bench with her own hands at the sides of her dress.

That was the first of that morning and she did not come back to it before the light went or after it.

Her own hand went to the pocket of her dress after that and came back out with nothing in it, and it went to the dry cloth along the head of the keeper's leaf instead, and she put the cloth down square against the fold and left it there.

---

She said a second thing out loud in that room about that column before she put the thing to him, and it was hers and it was in her own words.

She said that she had stood at the far end of that bench every morning since the morning she came off that wall. She had gone down that column of figures with her own eyes as far as the top of it every one of those mornings, and the wood inside every one of those six places is the colour it was on the day it was made. The seventh place at the foot of that column has been the same bare wood that length of time. She has been trying at that for as long as she has stood at that end of that bench, and she cannot make out from the shape of a stroke whose hand put it down.

Nobody in that room compared what she had said with what the keeper of that column had said out loud about that same column some days before, and nobody in that room repeated either of them to anybody, and she was told nothing about whether what she had said was right or wrong.

---

The man who came up that stair had been in that room since the fifth hour and had done nothing in it. He stood at the window at the side of that room with his own hands hanging at his own sides and looked out of that window at the row and the trestles and the four hundred yards of flags beyond them. Twice he turned round from that window and went along the room to the shelf at the height of a person's shoulder, stood in front of it, and came back to the window.

Nobody in that room went with him. Nobody in that room asked him what was on that shelf, and nobody in that room had any idea that he had stood in front of it twice.

Then she put it to him.

The man who came up that stair was still at that window with his own coat open and one sleeve of it wet to the shoulder and the other sleeve dry. She said it to him standing about a foot off his own shoulder, and it was said once.

She put it to him about those six strokes in that column, and whether anybody knew whose lines they were.

He answered her. What he said went from his mouth into her ear and stayed there. It went down that stair in her own mouth when she went out at the middle of the afternoon and along that row and out of that market. There is no leaf on either of those two books with a scratch of it on it, and no board in that market and no chalk anywhere in this city that carries one word of it.

Nobody thanked either of them. Nobody in that room said that either of them had remembered anything right or wrong. The keeper went down the column that holds figures and came back up it with the dry cloth along the head of her own leaf. There is a day standing against each of the six strokes in that column and not one of the six struck out. At the foot of that column the seventh place is bare wood of the colour of the six above it, and nothing was put in it and nothing was taken out of it.

At about the tenth hour the stallholder came up that stair and took the middle of that bench with his own board across his own knees and the bare back of it turned out into the room, and the man who came up that stair did not turn about from the window a second time.

The man of about thirty-one who carries things for a living stood at the near wall with the satchel up under his own arm. The man of about twenty-seven carried the bucket down that stair and up it again, twice over, with the water standing flat in it both times, and nothing was fetched for the trough at the top of that step and nothing was broken in it.

---

The cloth at the back of the woman of about fifty-two's dress had been pressed flat against the wood behind the far end of that bench for the best part of two hours, where her own shoulder stood against it. Where she came away from it at the middle of the afternoon it stood out off the wood in a soft ridge about the width of two fingers, and it stayed standing out for the rest of that day.
