# Chapter 0977 — The Outside Of That Door Where The Date Is

Somebody went past the foot of that stair and said one word out loud to nobody and went on along the row, and Adrian Vale had both his own hands flat on the outside of that door at the level of the day chalked on it.

It was Friday, the six hundred and sixty-second day of the Bare Month. The night had been hard and clear again, and the frost had been into the grit of that row and into the flags themselves and out of them before the light. The sun came over the roof line at the far end of that market after that row had woken. It came in under the awning at the store's end and lit the face of that door in one piece. The wind had gone round to the south in the night and was light. The bar lay on the top step of that stair, out of its two sockets, where it has lain every morning of that flood, and the trough on the flags beside it had ice in it about the depth of a thumb. There was the day in chalk on the outside of that door and the bare board under it stood clear of the near plank by the width of a thumb.

---

About four people a day went past the foot of that stair. That is what that is and it has not been reduced and it has not been given to anybody.

Two of them looked at the outside face of that door as they went past and two of them did not. A woman going up that row with a basket on her own arm looked at it and slowed for the length of a step and went on. A man going down that row with a barrow across his own shoulder looked at it and said nothing to the air and went on. A boy came down the middle of that row and never turned his head at it. A woman came up that row with her own hands full and looked at the doors on both sides of it and at the boards above them and not at that one.

The door stands open on its latch the whole of every morning, and Adrian Vale had his two hands flat on the outside of it at about the height of the writing on it, one on each of the two planks the door is made of, and he had been in that place since the fifth hour.

---

The mark on the bare board under that day is a single upright stroke. It is thick at the top of it and thin at the bottom of it, and it stands about two fingers in from the near edge of its own board, clear of the break in the writing above it. It came there on the morning before this flood came and nothing has gone under that date since and nothing has been scraped off that mark and nothing has been washed out of it and nothing has been changed about it.

He wanted to be able to say whether that stroke was a figure or a letter or a piece of a word.

That is three things it could be and a man cannot tell which of the three from the stroke alone. A one and an I and the first stroke of a longer mark all stand up the same, end the same, and are put down by a hand doing the same three motions in the same order. The only thing that separates them is what was on the page or in the mouth around them. There was no page and there was no mouth. He got his own finger to within the thickness of a hair of it twice without touching it and read it from that far off. Then he stood back from it and read it from further off again. Then he put his own two hands flat on the door on either side of the bare board and looked at it from the side with his own head on one side of it, which is how a man looks at a thing when he is trying to find the thickness of the chalk on it.

There is nothing on that bare board but one stroke. No chalk dust stands round it, and there is no second stroke under it, and no place on it is worn where something had been taken off. The wood around the mark is the colour of the wood at the far end of the board. Whatever that stroke is, it is one stroke, and nothing on the whole of that door, or on that stair, or in the room above it, or on any of the boards in that market would tell him which of the three things it is.

His own name was not said in that row. Nobody in it said one word to him about that mark or about his own two hands on that door, and none of them had been waiting for him to do anything at all.

---

The woman of about fifty-two came down that stair at about the ninth hour and stopped on the bottom step because he was in the frame of that door.

That bottom step is the eleventh and the last of them and the strip of ground begins at it, and it goes from there to wherever the paving gives out, and that strip of ground is about as wide as a room and nobody has ever measured it or cleared it or put a name on it. She stood on it with her own hands at the sides of her dress for about as long as it takes a man to read a thing twice, and she did not say anything to him and she did not say anything about what he was doing at the level of that writing. Then she went the rest of the way down and out under the awning and away along that row, and she was gone out of sight at the store's end of it.

That is what his own two hands on that outside face of that door did to that morning, and she did not thank him for it and nobody in that room above it knew anything about it at all.

---

The light came through the gap at the foot of that door in a stripe about the width of a hand and lay on the flags inside the room.

The door stands open on its latch, and the gap under it is where the flags inside that room have had a stripe of light standing on them for as long as the door has stood open. That stripe comes in on the flags at the bottom of the bottom step and walks across the floor as the sun goes along the row. By the time it had got to the far end of the bench it had come up the near leg of it about the height of a knee and was standing on the wood a finger's width wide. The grit in the joints of the flags on either side of it was still standing up in grains where the frost had gone out of them that morning, and the stripe had packed it flat as it went.
