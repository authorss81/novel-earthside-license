# Chapter 0953 — The Tin Up Nine Steps And Down

Two hands of a man's lay flat on the stone of the ninth step from the top of that stair and stayed there while he worked out what he was going to do with them, and then they went on doing nothing at all.

It was Tuesday, the six hundred and thirty-eighth day of the Bare Month. The sun had been up before the light and had come over the roof line at the far end of that market and along the row and in under the awning at the store's end by the fourth hour, and the room over that market stood full of it up to about the middle of the flags. The bar was on the top step where it lies, along the edge of the stone and out of its two sockets, and nobody had gone near either it or the sockets. On the outside of that door there was the day in chalk, and the bare board fixed under it carried the one mark that was on it and nothing else. The box of chalk stood on the table with its lid standing off at one corner and the two sticks inside it lying one across the other.

At the far end of the bench the woman of about twenty-nine who keeps a public register had her register open in front of her with the dry cloth along the head of her leaf, and the second book lay on the wood beside it with its cover slab off the leaf under it. The man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall two stalls along had a place in the middle of that bench, with his own board flat across his knees and the bare back of it turned out to the room. The man of about thirty-one who carries things for a living had the near wall, with the satchel held up under his arm and the flap of it hanging away from his own side. Against the far wall the woman of about fifty-two stood with her hands at the sides of her dress, and the stool she carried out of a room stood against the wood a foot off her own skirt. Adrian Vale had the near end of that bench with the ninth step of that stair behind him and nothing at all in his hands.

The chair at the far side of that bench stood under the window with dust on it, and a sheet of road folded in three lay on the chair with the fold along the middle, and the fold had been made a long time ago and had not been opened.

---

The woman of about sixty-nine with her tin came up the nine steps at about the sixth hour with the tin under her right arm and her own stick in the pocket of her skirt, and she came the whole way up and into that room and stopped about two feet short of the near end of that bench.

Adrian Vale got his own hand off the ninth step and came the last two steps. He went along that bench to the far end of it and took the chair by the back of it, with the folded sheet of road lying on the chair. He carried the chair and the sheet of road on it away from the window and out to the side of the room, and set it down against the wall that had nothing against it. That took the width of a person out of the walk between the bench and the wall.

**"I have got a tin under my arm and it is not a thing you can hold up and set down and hold up again for the length of a morning. I have been in and out of this room for a week with it. Every time I have had the end of this bench to put things down on."** said the woman of about sixty-nine with her tin, at the two feet of clear floor where the chair had been standing, with the tin still under her arm.

**"Then set it down when you come in and not when you have got to the end of something. I have had it under my arm coming up those steps and I have had it under my arm the whole way up, and I know what the arm is like by now and I did not know it this morning."** said the man of about thirty-one who carries things for a living, at the near wall with the satchel against his ribs.

She set the tin down on the wood where the chair had been standing and it stood there with the lid on it and her thumb off the lid. She put her arm back down along her own side and shook it once, from the elbow, the way a person shakes an arm that has been carrying something.

---

The tin was not opened. It stood on the wood in the light with the lid on it. The lid is a little proud of the rim on one side of it and not on the other. There is a dent in the rim at the near side about the width of two fingers. That tin has been carried under an arm a long time and has the shape of that about it. The man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall two stalls along went on along the foot of his own board with his own thumb and came to the end of it and went back to the top of it, and he did not look at the tin and did not stop on the bench to look at it. The keeper turned one leaf of her own register and squared it against the fold and did not look at the tin.

Adrian Vale came back along that bench and put his own hands down on the wood on either side of where the tin stood, flat, with the palms down and the fingers out, and the near of the two was about the width of a hand from the side of the tin and neither of them went on it. He stayed like that a while. The tin stood between his two hands. The lid stayed on it and the thing under the lid stayed under the lid. He did not put a finger on the rim of it. He did not lift the lid and he did not go round it to look at the underside of it. No mouth in that room asked him anything about it, and nobody in that room was standing about waiting to see what he would do with it.

He wanted to be told one thing about what was under that lid. Nobody in that room told him.

---

At about the tenth hour she picked the tin up off the wood and put it under her arm again and went down the nine steps and out under the awning at the store's end of that row and along the flags to where the flags give out into the side of that yard. She did not go along the row to her own place at the end of it and she did not stop at the store's end and she did not put her hand on the trestle of any of them. Adrian Vale came down the nine steps after her as far as the bottom one and stood there with his own hand on the frame of the door while she went out of sight along the flags, and nobody in that room asked him why he had come down and nobody followed him.

The light went along the sill of that window while he was standing in it and the dust on the sill stood up where the sheet of road had been sitting on the chair in front of it, and there were two shapes pressed into that dust about a hand apart. One of them was the older and had gone soft at the edges of it. The other was at the far end of the sill and had a rim round it still standing up about the depth of a grain of rice. Neither of them had been made this morning, and nothing on that sill said which hand had put either of them down or on what day, and nothing in that room said a word about it.

The chair stood against the side wall with the folded sheet of road still lying on it and the fold along the middle. The bench stood where it had stood for the whole of that flood and the nine feet of wood at the near end of it had been clear on the ground all morning.

---

By the light's going the bench was standing about a foot further along toward the wall than it had stood at the light. Under the place it had come off there were four squares of clean flags about the size of a hand where the feet of it had stood. The grit in the joints inside those four squares had been pressed flat and had not come back up. Where the bench stood then, the grit under it had not been touched at all, and it had not been touched at all because no hand in that room put the bench back where it had been, and nobody in that room was going to. The tin was not in that room and the lid of it was not off it. The two shapes in the dust of that sill stood as they had stood at the light, one of them gone soft at the edges and one of them with its rim still up about the depth of a grain of rice, and the gap between them had not altered by the width of anything that can be got at with a thumbnail.
