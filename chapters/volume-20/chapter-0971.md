# Chapter 0971 — The Sill And Two Shapes In Its Dust

The window at the side of that room stood open, and it had been standing open since before the light, and the dust along the front edge of its sill had begun to go where the air comes in over it.

It was Saturday, the six hundred and fifty-sixth day of the Bare Month. The night had been clear and hard, and the frost had been into the grit of the flags inside that room and out of it again before the light came. The sun came over the roof line at the far end of that market a good while after the row had woken. It came in under the awning at the store's end and lay down the length of that bench and stopped about the middle of the wood. The bar lay along the top step of that stair out of its two sockets, and the two sockets on the far side of the door below stood empty. There was the day in chalk on the outside of that door and the bare board under it stood clear of the near plank by the width of a thumb, with its one mark on it about two fingers in from the near edge. The stool stood a foot further out from the far wall than it had stood before she moved it with her own two hands.

---

Adrian Vale had both his own hands flat on the front edge of that sill, and the dust of it lay behind his wrists, and he had been in that place since the fourth hour.

There are two shapes pressed into that dust and they are about a hand apart. The one at the near end has gone soft at the edges of it and has lost its rim, and it has been soft a long while, and the fine stuff of that room has begun to get back down into it and take the shape away a grain at a time. The one at the far end has a rim round it still standing up about the depth of a grain of rice, and its middle gone down level with the dust either side of it. Neither was made this morning. Nothing on that sill says which hand put either of them down or on what morning, and neither is a day of the Bare Month.

He went at it the way a man goes at a lock he has no key for and has decided to be honest with instead of clever. He got his own fingers down beside the far shape and read the edge of it along about half of it. Where the rim had stood up it was standing up on three sides and had gone flat on the fourth. A rim goes flat where a hand has come down across it rather than down onto it. He read the near shape the same way and it gave him nothing at all, because a shape that has been soft that long has no rim to read and no edge to come down across. He put his own thumbnail in the gap between the two of them and it went down to the sill without touching either. He did that twice, on both sides, and got the same answer both times, and the gap was the same gap it had been for the whole of that flood.

What he wanted was to be able to say which hand put the newer one down and on what morning. There was no way on that sill or on any other surface in this city to say it. He wanted it badly enough that he kept at the window past the time the light had come far enough along that bench for anybody in the room to want it shut. The woman of about fifty-two came up that stair at about the fifth hour and went to her own far wall and stood there with her hands at the sides of her dress. He kept his own hands where they were.

---

His shadow lay across that floor and up the far wall.

The sun had not got round to the side of that window at the fifth hour. It came round to it about the seventh, and when it came round it came in on his own back. His shadow went off the sill and off his own arms and lay down the length of the room. Where it crossed the flags it went about as far as the wall, and where it got to the wall it stood up on the wood at about the height of a person's chest.

The woman of about fifty-two stayed at that wall for about the length of twenty minutes after that, and then she came off it without a word to anybody. She went the length of the room and stood at the far end of that bench, past the second book, with her own hands at the sides of her dress and her own shoulder to the wall behind the wood. From where she stood the window was at her back and the light off it, and the sill was behind her and out of it. She said nothing about why she had come off that wall, and nobody in that room said anything to her about it.

He wanted something off that sill for about as long as he could stand there and get nothing off it, and then he came away from it with his own two hands still flat on the front edge until the last moment. He did not put a finger into the dust of it at any point of that morning. That dust lay exactly as it had lain at the light, with the near shape soft and the far shape rimmed and the same gap between them that there had been at the fourth hour. The fine stuff of that room got no further into either of them in the whole of the time he had stood there looking.

---

What went through him while he was looking at it was not one thing. It was that he has spent thirty-one volumes wanting his own name on a page and cannot get at it. It was that here are two shapes on a sill, made by two hands on two mornings of this flood, by two people who are both probably still alive and both within eleven miles of where he was standing. It was that the room upstairs keeps a register with a day against a figure in it, and that neither of those two hands has ever put anything on it.

If he could have had the newer one of those two shapes dated he would have had a day on that sill with a hand behind it. That is the whole of what he wanted, and it is the size of a thumbnail of dust under the edge of a window.

He stayed in the room until the light began to go. The man of about twenty-seven carried the bucket down that stair and up it twice with the water standing flat in it, and the ice in the trough at the top of that step stayed whole about the depth of a thumb. The man of about thirty-one who carries things for a living stood at the near wall with the satchel held up against his own ribs and turned about off it twice and looked at the flags and turned about again.

**"There is a day standing against each of the six strokes in that column and every one of the six has its own day, and there is a seventh figure that wants one and has wanted one every morning this week. It will wait until it is ready and so will I," ** said the woman of about twenty-nine who keeps a public register, at the far end of that bench with her own hand flat on the page in front of her.

She said it once and went on down the column. The man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall two stalls along came up that stair at about the ninth hour and sat down in the middle of that bench with his own board across his knees. The man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board came up at the fifth hour, went along the face of his own board with the cloth, took it down that stair again before the light had got off the roof line, and came back up with nothing in his hands. There were about four people a day past the foot of that stair while the light was on the flags.

No mouth in that room said his name at any point of that day. Nobody in it was waiting for him to be useful to anybody, and he was not useful to any of them, and he went on being a man at a window with two shapes in the dust of it.

---

Clear floor about the width of a hand ran the whole length of that wall between the wood of it and the near leg of the stool, and the grit standing in the joints of the flags inside that room along that strip had not been walked on at any point of that flood. She was standing at the far end of that bench when the light went off that room, with the two books open a foot off her own elbow and her own hands still at the sides of her dress.
