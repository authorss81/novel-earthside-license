# Chapter 0985 — One Word And Then The Morning Goes On

The man who came up that stair came up it with his coat open and one sleeve of it wet to the shoulder and the other sleeve dry, and he did not take the coat off when he got into that room.

It was Saturday, the six hundred and seventieth day of the Bare Month. The night had been hard and clear for the eighth morning running, and the air stood white and close over eleven miles of flats until about the eighth hour and then thinned along the ground and let the light come in at once. The sun came over the roof line at the far end of that market and came along that row with the shade of the trestles going ahead of it, and it came in under the awning at the store's end and lay down the length of that bench.

The bar lay along the top step of that stair out of its two sockets. There was ice in the trough on the flags beside it about the depth of a thumb and the crack in its near end was quiet. About four people a day came along the walking space at the foot of that stair.

The block of writing was on the wall along the far side of that room where it had been since Wednesday morning. On the wood of that bench at the far end of it lay the sheet with three lines on it a hand's width in from its right edge, and the space below the third of them was bare.

About nine people were in that room that morning. In the hour that followed, about four of the people in it answered something that was said to them, and about four of the people in it said nothing at all.

---

Adrian Vale had both his own hands flat on the front edge of the shelf at the height of a person's shoulder. He had been in that place since the fifth hour, and the second book stood on that shelf a foot to his own left with its cover slab drawn back off the leaf under it.

The second book is a slate and it has been on that shelf since before anybody can remember. A day is cut across the head of its leaf in the middle of a stroke that was made with something hard and took the light along its length. Under that cut there is one word standing in the middle of the leaf with nothing above it and nothing below it and nothing beside it. The word has been on that leaf since before this flood came. The leaf has not been written on since the day it was cut and the crack in the column of it has taken the colour of the stone about it.

He wanted that word read out in that room by somebody who was not him.

He had been in that room before and something in it had been standing there a long while and had not been said, and he had come up that stair that morning with the whole of his own wanting set on the shelf under his own hands. He got what he wanted, and it was not what he had come for.

---

From the near wall, with the satchel up under his own arm, the man of about thirty-one who carries things for a living asked the man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall two stalls along, out loud across that room, whether the near corner of the walking space at his own trestle was still standing up on its edge.

The stallholder told him it was, and that it would go on standing up on its edge until the trestle went over.

The man of about twenty-seven told the tradesman, at the top of that step with his own hand on the rim of that trough, that the crack in the near end of it had gone a hand's width further along since the water came. The tradesman said he had not looked at it and did not intend to.

Two men said nothing at all to anybody that hour and stood at the door of that room with their own arms along their own sides, and one of them was there an hour and the other of them was gone in a quarter of it.

---

At about the ninth hour the man who came up that stair turned round from the window at the side of that room and said one word out loud.

He said it once, in that room, in daylight, with the date in chalk on the outside of the door at the foot of that stair behind his own shoulder.

**"borrowed," ** said the man who came up that stair, at the window of that room with his own wet sleeve down his side and the second book on the shelf a foot off him.

Nobody in that room asked him what it meant. Nobody in that room said it back to him. Nobody in that room improved on it, explained it, translated it or agreed with it, and nobody said it a second time before the light went or after it. Nobody in that room attached it to any day or to any person or to anything that had happened in this city, and nobody in that room asked him where it had come from or whose it was or what it had been doing on that leaf since before the water came.

That word had stood under a day cut across the head of that slate for longer than anybody in that room had been alive, and nothing had ever been entered under that day, and no mouth in that room had ever said out loud what it was doing there.

Adrian Vale got what he had come up that stair for. Somebody who was not him read it out in that room, in daylight, with a date on the door behind him. It was not the word he had expected, and it was not a longer thing, and there was nothing in it he could carry back down that stair and put somewhere.

---

The room went on.

The keeper went down the column that holds figures and came back up it, and at about the tenth hour she squared her own leaf against the fold and lifted the topmost page of it at its outer corner and put it down square again.

The tradesman went down the face of his own board with the cloth twice more, and each of those times the cloth came off the face at the foot of the middle column, where the middle of that board stands a shade proud of the two ends of it. The woman of about fifty-two stood at the far end of that bench the whole of that morning and took no part in it.

The second book was not picked up and not turned over and not written on by any hand that day. Nobody in that room said his name at any point of it, and nobody in that room was waiting for him to be of any use to anybody, and he was of no use to any of them.

At about the tenth hour the keeper reached across for the dry cloth along the head of her own leaf and found his own shoulder in the way of the light coming in at the window across that shelf. She worked at her own column from the foot of it up instead of from the head of it down for the rest of that morning, which is the wrong way round for a column of figures, and it took her about a quarter of an hour longer over it.

---

That shelf had a hand's width of dust on it at the near end of it where nothing had been standing since before the water came. His own forearm had been dragged along the front edge of that shelf twice that morning to get his hand round to the far side of the book, and the dust was gone off that hand's width of the wood in two lines about the length of a finger.
