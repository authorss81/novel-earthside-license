# Chapter 0952 — Six Joints Going Up The Row

A hand set a short stick about as long as a hand down into the grit of the flags at the store's end of that row, at a joint between two of them, and went down into it as far as the grit would take it and stopped.

It was Monday, the six hundred and thirty-seventh day of the Bare Month. The frost had gone off the roofs in the night and had left the grit between the flags at the store's end of that row standing up in grains about the depth of a nail, and where the crust had been walked on all that week the walking spaces had gone pale and hard and the joints between them had not. The light came in under the awning at that end and lay along the flags in one long stripe about the width of a man, and the shade of the trestles stood up out of it as the sun came along the row. Four hundred yards of flags ran from that store's end of that row to where the row gave out into the side of a yard, and the grit stood in every joint of that length of them.

The woman of about sixty-nine with her tin was at the store's end of that row with the tin under one arm and her own stick in her own hand. The man of about twenty-seven was at the step at the top of that row with the bucket on the stone by him and the rag folded over the rim of it. The man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall two stalls along had his own trestle two stalls along with his own board face in against the wood of it and the bare back turned out into the row behind his own shoulder. The tradesman stood at his own trestle with his own board face out and the cloth over his own shoulder.

---

She worked up the row. She did not work along it. At each joint she stopped, set the end of the stick into the grit, drew it toward her about the length of a hand, lifted it, and went on to the next joint. She made a line at every joint between the store's end of that row and the place where the flags gave out into the side of that yard. Each of them was about the length of a hand. The stick came up out of the grit with a ridge of it standing along the stroke and a hollow at the near end of it where her own hand had been.

The man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall two stalls along and the man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board watched her go up past them and neither of them said a word about it. The line went by under the trestles of the stallholder, and it went by under the trestle of the tradesman, and it went by four trestles that had nothing at them that morning, and it went on to the yard.

**"The light has not got to the far end of this row yet. The grit in the near joints is loose enough to take a mark and the grit at the far end is where the cart goes, and I do not get a second day doing this row."** said the woman of about sixty-nine with her tin, at a joint halfway up that row with the stick in her own hand and the tin still under her other arm.

**"The rag on that rim is dry and there is no water in this bucket at all. There is water standing in the low place at the side of that yard and I have not got down there with it today. That crack in the near end of that trough is going across the flags and I want to see where it goes before I put anything down on top of where it goes."** said the man of about twenty-seven, at the step with his own hands on the rim of the bucket.

She did not tell him what the lines were for. He did not ask her what the lines were for. Neither of them stood at the same joint at the same time, because she was always some flags ahead of wherever he was, and they came back down the row together at the end of it without a word between them. She put the stick away in the tin and did not shut the lid, and she put her own thumb down on the edge of the lid to hold it against her side, and the tin went along with her arm under it.

---

The row went on along the walking spaces while the light was on it. About four people a day went along that row on their own business, and some of them looked down and saw what was being made in the joints and some of them did not look down at all. Nobody stopped. A woman with a basket went over one of those lines without changing her step, and the grit of it came up on the sole of her boot and went on the flags in front of her as a pale smear about the length of a hand. A man carrying a coil went up the other side of the walking space and never looked down. A child came down the middle of the flags on the way to the store's end and put a foot on one of them and went on.

The stallholder took his own board face in against his own trestle at the time and left it face in. The tradesman put the cloth on the face of his own board and brought it down over the figures to the foot of the middle column, and it stopped where it stops every morning, and he folded it over his own shoulder and stood at his own trestle and watched the row go past.

---

By the time the light had gone off the far end of that row there was a walk across the grit at that end of the flags that had not been there at the light. The walking spaces of that row run along both sides of the flags and the joints go down the middle of the walking space and across the walking space in the same way, so that anybody who stays out of the joints can go the whole length of that row without putting a heel in one. Somebody had gone along that row on the near side, had come to the store's end, and had crossed from the near side to the far side of the flags on the diagonal instead of going round by the end of the trestles, and had gone back out again on the same line. The grit had taken the two prints and held them, and the ridge of grit along the line of the stroke of the stick had gone into the near print and come up standing out of it on the far side, and there was nothing on the flags on any other part of that row to show where the two prints had begun or where they had ended.

The lines were still standing where she had made them. The one under the basket had gone flat across its middle and stood up again on both sides of it, and the rest were as they were, and nobody had swept any of them. The stick had gone up in the tin and the tin had gone under her arm against her side, and the man of about twenty-seven was still at the step with the rag folded over the rim of the bucket and the crack in the near end of that trough going on across the flags behind him.
