# Chapter 0960 — A Thread Of Water To The Low Place

A hand came down on the rim of the trough at the top of that step and rested on the outside of it, and it did not go into the water and it did not go near the ice.

It was Tuesday, the six hundred and forty-fifth day of the Bare Month. The night had been hard again and the sun had not been up before the light. The frost had gone into the grit between the flags of that row and into the flags themselves. By the ninth hour the crust had begun to come off the walking spaces in plates about the size of a hand and to lift at the edges. There was ice in that trough about the depth of a thumb, white and hard right through, and nobody had broken it. The rag was folded over the rim of the bucket and the bucket stood on the stone at the top of that step.

The man of about twenty-seven had that step. At about the tenth hour the man of about thirty-eight at that salt wharf came up out of eleven miles of flats with the barrow and the knife in his belt and came along that row as far as that step and stopped at it.

---

The crack in the near end of that trough was running. It has been in that trough since before this flood. It runs a thread of water about the thickness of a pencil out of the stone at the near end of it and away over the lip of the step and across the flags. Where the thread crosses a joint in the flags it goes down into the joint for the width of the joint and comes up out of it on the other side and goes on. It had come about as far as the edge of the side of that yard by the tenth hour.

**"That thread has been carrying grit out of that trough with it and it has been laying it along the inside of that trough against the ice all morning, and there is a line of it standing on the ice about the width of a finger. I have got a bucket and a rag and I have not broken that ice and I am not going to, and if you want the rim of that trough wiped down I can do it this afternoon and not before."** The man of about twenty-seven said it at that step with his own hand on the outside of the rim.

**"I have had that crack since before the water came and I have watched it go across those flags every day of this flood and it has never once got to the low place at the side of that yard, and today it has got there before I have been down twice with a bucket. There is no heat in that trough and there is not going to be one, and the ice in it is not getting broken by me either."** said the man of about thirty-eight at that salt wharf, at that step with his own hands off the barrow and the thread of water going past the heel of his own boot.

---

They went along that row together to the low place in the stone at the side of that yard, which is about nine hundred paces from the top of that step, and the thread of water went with them the whole way because it runs downhill and the flags fall that way. At the low place they stood. The thread came off the flags at the last two feet of stone and went down into the low place and stood in it, and the water in that low place had been standing about the depth of two fingers all that week.

The man of about thirty-eight at that salt wharf took his own hand off the barrow shaft and put his own boot flat on the rim of the low place. He pushed his own weight onto it. The rim did not move, and the water came up over the top of his own boot about the depth of a nail and stood there.

**"Eleven miles out of that yard and in to this step and my boots are not dry and they are not going to be dry tonight, and that water has got a rim now and I have never seen that rim wet all the way round."** said the man of about thirty-eight, at the low place with the wet standing on the top of his own boot.

Nobody in that yard asked him anything and nobody offered him anything and nobody said that he had come a long way. He went back along the row and out across the flats with the barrow after him and the knife still in his belt.

---

By the light's going the water had gone over the rim of that low place all the way round and had stood on the stone inside the rim about the depth of two fingers, where for the whole of that flood it had stood in the hollow and had not once been up against the rim at any point of the ring. The grit the thread had been carrying out of that trough came down with it and settled in a fan on the near side of the low place. The crust that had lifted off the walking spaces in plates that morning had gone into the water at the near edge and gone under. The plates themselves were lying against the far rim, where they had come to rest against the edge of the stone, and they were not melting yet.

Nothing was fetched for that water and nothing was carried to that trough on that day and no hand in that row offered to carry anything. The ice in it at the top of that step was still whole at the light's going and still about the depth of a thumb, and the rag on the rim of the bucket had gone stiff and white along its own fold, and the thread was still coming out of the near end of that trough and going across those flags and would go on coming out of it tomorrow.
