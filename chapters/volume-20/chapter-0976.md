# Chapter 0976 — Carried Out And Back Up With It Shut

The bucket went down that stair at about the fifth hour with the water standing flat in it, and it came back up with the same water in it, and the man who carried it turned round at the top and went down again with it.

It was Thursday, the six hundred and sixty-first day of the Bare Month. The wind had got up off the flats in the night and had gone into the north-east by the light and was still going, and the light came sideways along the row and lay down nowhere on it. The flags had dried hard in the middle of the walking spaces and the joints of them were still black. On the top step of that stair the bar lay out of its two sockets, where it has lain all that flood. There was ice in the trough on the flags beside it about the depth of a thumb, and the crack in its near end was running its thread across the flags. There was the day in chalk on the outside of the door at the foot of the stair and the bare board under it stood clear of the near plank by the width of a thumb, with one mark on it.

The woman of about fifty-two came up at about the fifth hour and went to the far end of that bench and stood with her own hands at the sides of her dress, where she had stood the last four mornings. The keeper had the far end of that bench with her register open, the dry cloth along the head of her own leaf, and the second book open beside her with a leaf showing. The man of about thirty-one who carries things for a living had the near wall with the satchel up under his own arm. About four people a day went past that stair with the light on the flags.

---

He took it out to the low place in the stone at the side of that yard at about the seventh hour.

That is about nine hundred paces from the top of the step, out past the store's end of the row and along four hundred yards of flags to where they give out into the side of a yard. He carried the satchel under his own arm the whole way with the flap of it hanging away from his own side and his own hand shut on the strap. He went along that row past the trestle of the man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board and past four trestles with nothing at them. Neither of them said anything to him about where he was going, and he did not tell them.

At the low place in the stone he put the satchel down on the flags on the dry side of the rim. He stood over it a while with his own hands on his own knees. Then he went to the rim and looked at the water standing in it, which was about the depth of two fingers and had been so all that flood. He put no hand in it and took nothing out of it. Then he went along the stones to the far side of the yard and back again and stood at the low place a second while with his own thumb at the edge of his own thumb. Then he picked the satchel up, put it under his own arm, and came the whole way back up the row with the flap of it hanging away from his own side.

The flap of it stayed down the whole of that time. He did not open it in that yard, and he did not open it on the flags on the way back, and he did not open it going up that stair. He put his own hand on the flap of it once, flat, going past the store's end of the row, the way a man touches a thing he is carrying to find out whether it is still where it is, and the flap stayed down under his own hand. The reason it was not opened is not in that yard and not in that row and not in that room.

---

The rest of that morning went on the way that morning goes.

The man of about twenty-seven carried the bucket down and up four times. The fourth time he set it down on the flags at the top of the step and stood looking at the ice in the trough with his own hand on the outside of the rim. The thread of water out of the crack in the near end of it came across the flags and over the lip and went away on the walking space. The ice was whole about the depth of a thumb, and no heat had been fetched for it, and nobody offered.

The tradesman came up at the fifth hour with his own board under his arm and the cloth over his own shoulder. He went along the face of his board from the top of the top column to the foot of the middle column, and the cloth stopped where it stops every morning. He folded it over his own shoulder, took the board down that stair to his own trestle, and came back up with nothing in his hands. The stallholder came up at about the ninth hour, sat in the middle of that bench with his own board across his own knees, and went along the foot of it with his own thumb. The keeper went down the column that holds figures and came back up it, and lifted the topmost page of her own register at its outer corner and put it down square against the fold. The dry cloth lay along the head of that leaf and stopped at the near edge of the second book.

The woman of about fifty-two said nothing to anybody that day. The carrier put the satchel down on the flags inside that room about a foot to the left of the place he had put it down the day before. He turned about off that wall twice, looked at the flags, and turned about again. The rest of that room went on with what it was doing.

---

By the light's going the strap of that satchel had gone pale in one place about the length of two fingers.

Where his own hand closes on that strap every morning is the place it goes pale, and it has been going pale at the same place since before the water came. The leather either side of it is still the colour it came out of the shop in. He had shut his own hand on the strap about a dozen times that day without meaning to do it. The pale had come up the width of his own palm at its widest and had gone round the edge of the strap where his own middle finger lies across it, and the buckle under it had gone the same pale about the length of a nail where it is rubbed. The strap lay along the leather where his own hand had let go of it and had not settled back down, standing up about the depth of a thumb where the air was getting under it.
