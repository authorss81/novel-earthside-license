# Chapter 0997 — He Reads It Twice And Then Answers

The woman of about sixty-nine with her tin came up that stair at about the seventh hour with the tin under her right arm and her own stick in the pocket of her skirt, and she came the whole way up and stood about two feet short of the near end of that bench.

It was Thursday, the six hundred and eighty-second day of the Bare Month. There had been no cloud in the sky for two nights and the sun got up over eleven miles of flats before that door was chalked, and by six the light was already in under that awning and standing along that bench as far as the middle of the wood. The crust of that ground out past the last named house had been walked on and frosted and walked on again and stood pale and hard in the middle of the walking spaces.

Out of its two sockets on the top step of that stair the bar lay along the stone, and those two sockets stood empty in the far side of that door below. The trough on the flags at the top of that step had ice in it about the depth of a thumb, and the crack in its near end was quiet. The thread of water out of that crack came over the lip of that step and stopped in the joint of the flags a foot short of the walking space, and it had stopped in that same joint every morning of the week. The day stood out in chalk on the outside of that door, and about a finger's width of that chalk was lost down in the groove between the two planks, and the bare board under the day stood clear of the near plank by the width of a thumb with one mark on it.

The block of writing was on that wall where it has stood since the Wednesday morning, and not one word has ever been said about it. That sheet lay on the wood at the far end of that bench with three lines on it a hand's width in from its right edge and the space below the lowest of them bare.

---

The man of about fifty-seven came up that stair at about the sixth hour with his own coat buttoned and came into that room and stopped about two feet short of the middle of that bench, which is where he stood for the rest of that morning.

He stood in it where he was and looked at the bench and the two books open on it and the wall behind them and the window. He did not go over to the window and he did not go to the shelf at the height of a person's shoulder.

The man who came up that stair was in that room at the window at the side of it with his own coat open and one sleeve of it wet to the shoulder and the other sleeve dry. He stood at that window with his own hands hanging at his own sides and looked out of it at the row and the trestles and the four hundred yards of flags beyond them. He was in that room and he was not in what went on at the far end of that bench, and neither of the two people at that bench looked at him while it was going.

---

The woman of about sixty-nine with her tin put her own hand on the wood of that bench about two feet short of the near end of it and told the keeper what she meant to put to him, in her own words, standing there.

The keeper took the stub of a pencil out of the pocket of her dress and squared the sheet with her own hand and wrote on it below the first three lines. She wrote three lines and no more, a hand's width in from the right edge of that sheet, and the space below the third of the new lines was bare when she had done.

The first of the three new lines was the day. The second of them was who it was about. The third of them was the thing she meant to put to him, and it was one line long, and it said in the plainest words she had: whether the house he was born in is still standing, and what is on the shelf in the front room of it, and whether the page in the book on that shelf says what he has always said it says.

Nobody in that room said anything about the three lines while they were being written, and nobody asked her where the house was or how far off it was or how long ago it was that she had last seen it.

---

Then the keeper turned the sheet round on the wood so that those three lines faced him and put her own hand flat on the paper beside them, and nobody in that room said a word about what was on it.

The man of about fifty-seven came the length of that bench and read it. He read it from the head of it down the way a person reads a page, and he read it once and then he read it again. Between the two times he put his own thumb on the second line and left it there about as long as it takes to read a thing twice.

He read it twice and then he answered her.

What he said took about as long as the reading had. It went out of that room in his own mouth and down that stair and along that row and out past the store's end of it.

There is no leaf on either of those two books with a scratch of it on it, and there is no board in that market and no chalk anywhere in this city that carries one word of it, and nobody in that room repeated it to anybody and nobody in that row heard it.

Nothing was written down after that. There was no leaf turned and nothing added to that paper and the space below the third of those new lines stayed as blank as the rest of the sheet, and the pencil went back into the pocket of the keeper's dress and her own hands came off the wood.

Nobody in that room asked him to make it so that way. Nobody in that room thanked him for answering her. Nobody in that room told him that what he had said was right, and nobody asked him a second thing at any hour of that day.

---

That was the whole of what was put to him, and the woman of about sixty-nine with her tin had said nothing else out loud in that room on any morning before that one or on any morning after it.

She went down that column of figures and back up it. Each of the six strokes in it has a day standing against it and none of them struck out, and the seventh place at the foot of that column is bare wood the colour of the six above it. The second book lay open beside her register with its cover slab drawn back off the leaf under it and the dry cloth lying along the head of its leaf where it had gone into the crack on Tuesday and been pressed down into it. It was not picked up and not turned over and not written on by any hand that day.

The man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board came up at the fifth hour with his own board under his arm and put it down on the near end of that bench with its face out and its foot toward the door. He came down the face of it with the cloth and the cloth stopped at the foot of the middle column, and he folded the cloth back over his own shoulder and carried his own board back down that stair to his own trestle two along. The woman of about fifty-two was at the far end of that bench with her own shoulder to the wood behind the far end of it, and that stool stood against the wall along the far side of the room where she had set it down. At about the ninth hour the man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall two stalls along came up that stair and sat down in the middle of that bench with his own board across his own knees and the bare back of it to that room. The man of about thirty-one who carries things for a living stood at that near wall with the satchel held up against his own ribs and the flap of it hanging down.

The man of about fifty-seven stood about two feet short of the middle of that bench until the light had gone off that row. Nobody asked him anything else at any hour of the rest of that day, and nobody asked him anything on the two mornings that came after it either.

---

The light came along that row at about the middle of that afternoon and lay on the outside face of that door at the foot of that stair. It took the chalk of that day on it and the bare board under that day and the one mark on that board, and it stood on all three of them for about as long as it takes to read a thing twice. Then it went up above the roof line at the far end of that market and the outside of that door went back into the shade with the chalk of that day still on it.
