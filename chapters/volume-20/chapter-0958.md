# Chapter 0958 — Nobody Can Say What It Was

Both hands of a man were on the face of his own board, holding it the way he holds it at his own trestle, and they were not moving while he worked out what he was going to say about a day two hundred days gone.

It was Sunday, the six hundred and forty-third day of the Bare Month. There had been no sun at all before the light and the air in that row stood close and white until about the eighth hour, and then the cloud went off the flats and the light came along the row at once and put the damp up off the flags. The bar was where it lies, along the top step, and the two sockets on the far side of the door below had nothing in them and had not had anything in them for a long time. On the outside of that door there was a day in chalk, and the bare board under it carried the one mark that was on it.

He came up the nine steps with his own board under his own arm, the cloth folded over the top of it, and he put the board down on that bench at the near end of it and stood up and went back to it and put both hands on the face of it and kept them there. The near end of that bench is four feet from the near end of the second book and the face of his board was turned out to the room and there is nothing on the face of it that anybody in that room can read from where he is standing.

---

He said it into the middle of that floor the way a man says a thing he has been carrying and has put down.

**"Somebody stood in this room on the seven hundred and forty-eighth day of this flood and put a thing to me and I did not answer it. I have not answered it since and I am not going to answer it now. I can tell you where I was standing when it was put to me and I can tell you that it was about the fourth hour and that the light had not got past the end of the bench. I cannot tell you what it was. I have gone at it since and it is not there, and that is not because I am keeping it, because there is nothing to keep."** said the man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board, at the near end of that bench with both his own hands on the face of his own board.

The room went on. The stallholder went along the foot of his own board across his knees and did not stop at the middle of it. The carrier turned about off the near wall and looked at the stair. The woman of about fifty-two kept her own wall and said nothing. The stallholder went down the nine steps a little after that and along the row to his own trestle and stood at it.

**"I am not going to have that put down on a page, and I am not going to have it taken off a page, and I am not going to have my own page used to hold a thing that came out of a mouth this morning. That register holds a day against a figure. That is the whole of what it holds. If anybody brings me a line about a thing somebody said in this room I will put it on the floor."** said the woman of about twenty-nine who keeps a public register, with her own hand flat on the page in front of her and the dry cloth along the head of her own leaf.

---

Nobody in that room could say what the thing was, and neither could he, and between the two of them they had the whole of it.

The woman who is supposed to have asked him it was not in that room on that morning. The other woman of twenty-nine was in a room nine steps down. Nobody in that room established which of the two women of twenty-nine stood at the end of that bench on the seven hundred and forty-eighth day, and nobody in that room went down the nine steps afterwards to ask the other one. There was no page for it to be on and there was no hand that had anything on it, and nothing said in that room that morning settled whether those two are one woman or two, and nothing else on any morning since has settled it either.

The tradesman sat down on that bench at about the ninth hour with the board still flat across his knees and put his own hand on the face of it and took it off again and put it back. The keeper's hand went to the dry cloth along the head of her leaf and lifted one end of it about the width of a nail and put the end down again without moving the leaf and went back to the column that holds figures.

---

Nobody in that room said one word about the space about two fingers wide at the foot of the middle column of his own board. The cloth came down the face of that board from the top of the top column and stopped where it stops every morning, and the wood inside that space was paler than the wood either side of it, and there is no figure standing in it and nobody in that row saw one go.

---

By the light's going he took his own board up off that bench and went down the nine steps with it under his own arm and the cloth folded over the top of it. He went along the row to his own trestle and stood at it, and did not put his own hand on the face of it when he got there. The keeper turned one leaf of her register and squared it against the fold.

Where she had lifted one end of that dry cloth at about the ninth hour and put the end down again without moving the leaf, the cloth had been wet along its own edge for about the length of three fingers. It had left that wet on the wood of that bench in the shape of its own edge. By the light's going the shape had dried to a pale line, and the wood under the line stood a shade darker than the wood beside it, and it would go back inside a week. The gap at the head of her leaf, which had come away from the leaf under it by the width of a card two mornings before, had taken more damp and gone soft along its whole length, and the edge of the topmost page lay on the one under it about the width of a nail. The second book lay where it had lain all morning with its slab off the leaf and the leaf flat, and the register lay open at the far end with its own dry cloth along the head of it. What had been said at the end of that bench went out of the room and down the nine steps and along that row, and it was not in either of those two books by the light's going.
