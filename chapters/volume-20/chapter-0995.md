# Chapter 0995 — An Answer He Gave And Nobody Acted On

The dry cloth that had lain along the head of the second book's leaf since Thursday had gone down into the crack in the column of that leaf and was lying in it instead of along it, and the whole length of the cloth had taken the shape of the stone that was holding it.

It was Tuesday, the six hundred and eightieth day of the Bare Month. The night had been hard and clear for the thirteenth morning running and the sun was up before the light and came over the roof line at the far end of that market about the fourth hour. The air went thin along the ground early and by six the light was in under the awning at the store's end and standing along that bench as far as the middle of the wood. Four hundred yards of flags ran from the store's end of that row to where the row gave out into the side of a yard, and the walking spaces of them stood pale and hard in the middle with the joints black.

That bar lay where it has lain every morning of that flood, out of its two sockets on the top step of that stair, and those two sockets stand empty in the far side of the door below. There was ice in that trough on the flags about the depth of a thumb and nothing had been fetched for it. The thread of water out of the crack in its near end came over the lip of that step and stopped in the joint of the flags a foot short of the walking space, where it has stopped every morning of the week. There was a day in chalk on the outside of that door at the foot of that stair, and the chalk of it crossed the joint between the two planks and about a finger's width of that writing was lost down in the groove of it. Under the day the bare board stood clear of the near plank by the width of a thumb with one mark on it.

The block of writing was on the wall along the far side of that room, and the plaster either side of it was the colour it has been for twelve days. That sheet lay on the wood of that bench with three lines on it a hand's width in from its right edge and the space below the lowest of them bare.

---

The keeper was at the far end of that bench from the fifth hour with her own register open in front of her and her own hand on the page of it. She went down the column that holds figures and came back up it and lifted the topmost page of her own leaf at its outer corner and put it down square against the fold. The man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board came up at the fifth hour with his own board under his arm, and the cloth came off the face of that board at the foot of the middle column where it stops every morning. He took his own board back down that stair to his own trestle two along and brought it up again with nothing in his own hands.

The woman of about fifty-two came up at the fifth hour and set that stool down against the wall along the far side of the room in the place she sets it down and stood at the far end of that bench with her own shoulder to the wood behind the far end of it and her own hands at the sides of her dress. The man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall two stalls along came up about the ninth hour and sat down in the middle of that bench with his own board across his own knees and the bare back of it turned out into the room. He went along the foot of that board with his own thumb three times that day, from the top of the middle of it to the foot of it. The man of about thirty-one who carries things for a living had the near wall with the satchel against his own ribs.

---

Adrian Vale stood at the far end of that bench from the fifth hour with his own hands at his own sides and looked at the column that holds figures.

He had the whole of that hour with it. He stood there about as long as it takes to read a thing twice, and he did not put a hand on the register and did not turn a leaf of it and did not lean over the table to get at it.

There are six strokes in that column and there is a day standing against each of the six and not one of the six has been struck out. He went down that column with his own eyes from the head of it to the foot of it, and then he went at the six days standing against the six strokes one after another and read them. He could not tell from any one of them whose hand had put the stroke down beside it.

That was not because the days were hard to read. A day against a figure says when and does not say who, and that is the whole of what a day against a figure has ever said in this room or in any room. He had read those six days before, on the morning he stood in that room with the back of his own hand on a piece of paper a hand's width from this one, and there was nothing new to read in them and there was nothing in them about him.

He had asked for his own name on a page for thirty-one volumes and had never got it, and the one thing in this room that looked like a way of getting it was six strokes with a day against each of them and nothing at all saying whose figures those were.

The leaf of that register was lying open on the wood of the bench in front of him and it was exactly as he had found it: square against the fold, the topmost page of it down square, and the head of it bare. The dry cloth was lying in the crack of the other leaf and not along the head of this one. He did not touch any part of it at any hour of that day.

---

At about the eighth hour the keeper wanted to put her own register back up on the shelf behind that bench, and she could not see the near end of that shelf from where she was standing without moving the second book and the sheet to get at it.

She put one thing to him about it.

She put it to him about whether that shelf stands square with the wall behind it or hangs forward at its near end, and whether the near end of it hangs forward enough that a register put up on it would come down off it of its own weight.

He answered her. What he said went about nine feet from her own mouth and it took about as long as it takes to say a thing twice. Nobody in that room was waiting for him to have a useful answer, and nobody said a word back to him about it, and nobody thanked him for it and nobody told him that it was not wanted.

Nothing was done with what he said. The keeper put the topmost page of her own leaf down square again and carried on down the column that holds figures and came back up it.

Her own register lay on the wood of that bench at the far end of that room where it had been lying since the morning before, and the shelf behind that bench stayed as it was with nothing on it but the dust that had come up that stair on it.

Adrian Vale went on standing at that end of that bench. He was of no use to anybody in that room at any hour of that morning, and nobody in that room told him so, and nobody in that room asked him anything else.

---

The man of about twenty-seven took that step four times that morning with a full bucket and brought it back up four times, and on the last of those four he did not go on down the row afterwards. He stood on the flags with his own hand on the outside of the rim of that trough and looked at the ice in it for about as long as it takes to read a thing twice. Nothing had been fetched for that trough that day and nothing had been broken in it.

No mouth in that room said the name of the man who came up that stair on any day of that week. Nobody went and stood in front of the block of writing on that wall. No hand in that room picked that second book up or turned it over or wrote on it at any hour of that day.

In the middle of that afternoon she went out of that room and down that stair and along that row, and the stool stood in that room where she had left it.

---

The light came off that sill about the fourth hour and went down the wall under it, and the dust on that sill stood in a low ridge along the near edge of it. The two shapes pressed into that dust stood in the flat of it beyond that ridge where the light did not reach them.
