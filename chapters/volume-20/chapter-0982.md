# Chapter 0982 — What The Chalk Says To The People Going Past

Somebody went past the foot of that stair and read the day on the outside of that door without stopping, and somebody else went past about a quarter of an hour later and did not read it at all.

It was Wednesday, the six hundred and sixty-seventh day of the Bare Month. The night had been hard and clear for the seventh morning running and there had been no sun before the light for seven days, and the frost had been into the grit of that row and out of the walking spaces of it again before the sun came. The light came over the roof line at the far end of that market a good deal after that row had woken and came along it with the shade of the trestles going ahead of it.

There was ice in the trough on the flags at the top of that step about the depth of a thumb, and the crack in its near end was quiet for the first time in a week. The bar lay where it has lain every morning since before the water came, out of its two sockets on the top step. The day was chalked on the outside of that door and the chalk of it crossed the joint between the two planks the door is made of. That stair is walked past by about four people a day. The box of chalk stood inside that room on the table with the lid of it off at one corner and the two sticks in it lying one across the other.

The woman of about fifty-two came up that stair at about the fifth hour and went to the far end of that bench. The sheet lay on the wood of that bench at the far end of it with three lines on it a hand's width in from its right edge, and those three lines had been on it since yesterday morning and had been read by two people, and one of those two had answered what was on the third of them.

---

She said the second thing out loud at about the sixth hour.

**"The page carries the thing to be put, the person it is put to, and the day it is put on. It carries nothing back. Whatever she says to me goes out of this room in her own mouth and down that stair and along that row and out of this city. I am not going to write it. If she comes up these stairs in a week's time and wants to know what she said, she will not get it out of me or out of anybody else in this room. That is what the page is for and that is the whole of what it is for, and I am not going to hear anybody in this room tell me I have it right," ** said the woman of about twenty-nine who keeps a public register, at the far end of that bench with her own hand flat on the sheet in front of her and the date in chalk on the outside of that door behind her own shoulder.

She said it once and she left it where it was.

Not one voice in that room agreed with it and not one voice argued with it, and nobody improved on one word of it, and nobody was thanked for it. The woman of about fifty-two stood at the far end of that bench with her own hands at the sides of her dress and gave the whole of it back in her face and not one word of it out loud. The man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board went along the face of his own board with the cloth and the cloth stopped where it stops every morning. The man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall two stalls along had the middle of that bench with his own board lying across his own knees and the bare back of it turned out into the room.

Nothing whatever was done about it before the light went. The sheet kept its three lines. There was no leaf turned and nothing added to the paper, and the space below the third of those lines stayed as blank as the rest of the sheet.

---

Down at the foot of that stair there is a surface in that row that people walk past every morning, and it is the outside face of that door.

It carries the day in chalk, and the chalk of the day crosses the joint between the two planks and stands broken along that joint, and about a finger's width of the writing is lost down in the groove of it. Under the day, on the bare board that stands clear of the near plank by the width of a thumb, there is one stroke.

About four people a day got a thing off that door. About four people a day went past the foot of that stair and got nothing off it at all.

A man going up that row at about the sixth hour stood in front of it and read the day and read the stroke under it and went on without stopping. A woman coming down that row looked at the two doors either side of her and not at that one. A boy came down the middle of that row with his own hands in his pockets and went by it and could not have said afterwards what was chalked on it. A woman going up that row with a basket on her own arm stopped, read the day, and went on up the row and out at the store's end of it.

What that surface says to the people who walk past it is the day, and one stroke under the day, and nothing whatever about either of them. It gave the day to about four people a day and it gave that stroke to nobody at all, and the door stands open on its latch the whole of every morning with the frame of it empty.

---

The rest of that morning in that room was work, and the work was the same work.

The man of about twenty-seven carried the bucket down that stair and up it four times with the water standing flat in it, and on the fourth time he stood looking at the ice in that trough with his own hand on the outside of the rim. The man of about thirty-four went along the foot of his own board with his own thumb twice, from the top of the middle of it to the foot of it. His own palm went flat against the front of his own coat over the inside breast pocket once, stayed there, and came off again. The chalk stayed where it has been all that flood.

The keeper went down the column that holds figures and came back up it, and then she squared her own leaf against the fold with the flat of her own hand and lifted the topmost page of it at its outer corner and put it down square again. She did that twice more before the light had got off that roof line.

At about the eighth hour the man of about twenty-seven told the tradesman across that room that the thread of water out of the crack in the near end of that trough had come further across the flags that week than it had come the week before. The tradesman said that he had not looked at it and was not going to.

The second book lay open beside the register with its cover slab drawn back off the leaf under it, and that leaf lay flat. The crack in the column of it stood a hair proud of the stone about it and took the last of that morning's light along the whole length of itself.

---

The chalk on the outside of that door came off the writing on it in that cold and lay in the bottom of the groove between the two planks in a line about the length of a hand. It was the finest of that chalk and the palest of it, and it was the only dust on that door that anybody in that market ever noticed at all.
