# Chapter 0963 — The Space At The Foot Of The Column

Both hands went onto the bare back of a board and stayed there on the wood while a man worked at the foot of a trestle, and the board stood face in against that trestle with the writing of it turned away from the row.

It was Friday, the six hundred and forty-eighth day of the Bare Month. The night had been clear and hard and the frost had been into the grit between the flags of that row and out of it again before the light. The sun came over the roofs at the far end of that market a good deal after the row had woken and came along the walking spaces with the shade of the trestles going ahead of it. The board on the outside wall at the far end of that market stood on its one nail with the hole of a second nail empty above it, and the ice in the trough at the top of that step was still whole and about the depth of a thumb and no heat had been fetched for it.

Two trestles along, the man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board had taken his own board off his trestle at about the sixth hour and turned it round. He set it face in against the wood of the trestle, so that the face of it with nineteen rates in three columns was against that wood and the bare back of it stood out into the row behind his own shoulder. He was at the foot of that trestle with his own hands on the ground round the legs of it. Two stalls along the man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall two stalls along had his own board face in against the wood of his trestle with the bare back turned out and his own thumb along the foot of it, and neither of the two of them said a word to the other one.

---

He was doing a thing at the foot of that trestle which nobody in that row had asked him to do and which he did not explain. The frost had got into the ground at the foot of the two legs of it and come up the legs about the depth of a nail. He had his own hand at the near leg working the grit out of the joint where the leg met the flags, one piece at a time, the way a man picks at something that is not going to come off in one go. The grit came away and went down between the flags and he went on at it.

While he was at the foot of it he had the bare back of his own board under his hands about the height of a knee, and the wood of that back was the wood nobody in that row looks at, and there was nothing on it and there had never been anything on it.

He went on at the near leg of that trestle for about the better part of three quarters of an hour. The row went by. About four people a day went along that walking space, and some of them looked at a man at the foot of a trestle and some of them did not.

---

Then he turned his own board round and put it back face out on his own trestle in the height where a man walks, and stood at it with the cloth over his own shoulder.

The face of it carried nineteen rates in three columns. The middle one came down about the depth of a thumb further than it had come down in the spring, and at the foot of it there was a space about two fingers wide with nothing standing in it. The wood inside that space was paler than the wood either side of it, and it was the only pale place on that face. It was pale for the plainest reason there is, which is that a cloth that comes down a face and stops at a column cannot get into the foot of it. That space has not been under anything at all since that board went out.

Along the foot of that middle column, standing below the space and running the width of it and a little beyond it at each end, there was a line about the length of a hand where the wood was darker than the wood either side of it. That line was not a figure and there was nothing on it. It was where the edge of that cloth stands every morning and has stood every morning of that flood, and the wood under it has had the wet of that cloth against it and dried a hundred times and more, and the wood either side of that line has not.

---

He put his own hand flat on the face of that board and brought it down over the figures to the foot of the middle column, and the cloth stopped there the way it stops there every morning, and he lifted it and folded it over his own shoulder.

He did not write in that space. He had nothing to write in it with. The one piece of chalk in that row was in the inside breast pocket of the man two stalls along and it did not come out of that pocket that day, and this one did not go and ask for it, and nobody offered it to him and nobody told him where to get another.

**"That middle column has come down further than it came down in the spring and the space at the foot of it is as empty as it was on the day before, and there is a rate standing at the foot of that column that wants a day against it the way it wanted one yesterday. I have got a cloth and I have got two hands, and there is not one of the three of them that will put anything in that space, and I am not going to go two stalls along and stand in front of that man's pocket and ask him for the thing in it," ** said the man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board, at his own trestle with the face of the board out to the row.

He said it once. Nobody standing at that board asked him a thing about the space at the foot of it, and the row went on along the walking spaces, and no day went against that rate and nothing was entered anywhere.

---

By the light's going that board was standing face out on its own trestle where it had stood at the light, and the cloth was over his own shoulder, and the two battens at the back of that board were showing on the far side of it where the trestle did not cover them.

The wood at the near edge of it had gone dark. The near edge of that board is the edge a man puts his hand under when he lifts it off a trestle and turns it, and the damp comes out of the end grain of it and stands there. By the going of the light there was a band of that darker wood about the length of a hand along the whole of that near edge, where his own hands had been on that board three times that day and nowhere else on it.
