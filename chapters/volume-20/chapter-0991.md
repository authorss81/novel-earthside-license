# Chapter 0991 — He Puts It To Him A Second Time

The latch on that door at the foot of that stair went up and came down three times before the light and twice after it, and it was the same hand on it each time and it did not stay up.

It was Friday, the six hundred and seventy-sixth day of the Bare Month. The night had been hard and clear for the ninth morning running and the sun was not up before the light. The air over eleven miles of flats stood white and close until about the eighth hour and then thinned along the ground and let the light come in under the ridge at the far end of that market at once. Four hundred yards of flags ran from the store's end of that row to where the row gave out into the side of a yard, and the crust where the carting has been all that week stood pale and hard in the middle of the walking spaces.

The bar lay along the top step of that stair out of its two sockets, and the two of them were empty in the far side of the door below. The trough on the flags beside the bar had ice in it about the depth of a thumb and nothing had been fetched for it. The day stood out in chalk on the outside of that door, and about a finger's width of that chalk was lost down in the groove between the two planks, and under the day the bare piece of board stood clear of the near plank by the width of a thumb with one mark on it. About four people a day went past the foot of that stair with the light on the flags, and the same going past it at other hours besides.

The block of writing was on the wall along the far side of that room with the plaster either side of it the colour it had been for eleven days. On the wood of that bench at the far end of the room lay the sheet with three lines on it a hand's width in from its right edge, and the space below the third of them was bare. The two books lay open side by side beside it, and the second book had the dry cloth along the head of its leaf and its cover slab drawn back off the leaf under it.

---

The man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board came up that stair at the fifth hour with his own board under his arm and the cloth over his own shoulder, and put the board down on the near end of that bench with its face out and its foot toward the door. He took the cloth off his own shoulder and came down the face of his own board from the top of the top column. The middle column of that board stands about the depth of a thumb deeper than the two either side of it, and the cloth stopped at the foot of it where it stops every morning of that flood, and he folded the cloth back over his own shoulder and left it there.

The woman of about fifty-two came up at the fifth hour and went to the far end of that bench with her own hands at the sides of her dress. The man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall two stalls along came up about the ninth hour and took the middle of that bench with his own board flat across his own knees and the bare back of it turned out into the room. He went along the foot of that board with his own thumb from the top of the middle of it to the foot of it and back up again.

The man of about thirty-one who carries things for a living had the near wall with the satchel held up against his own ribs and the flap of it hanging away from his own side. The man of about twenty-seven carried the bucket down that stair and up it again with the water standing flat in it, twice over.

---

The man who came up that stair came up it with his coat open and one sleeve of it wet to the shoulder and the other sleeve dry.

He was at the window at the side of that room from the fifth hour. He stood with his own hands hanging at his own sides and looked out of that window at the row and the trestles and the four hundred yards of flags beyond them. Once he turned round from that window and went along the room to the shelf at the height of a person's shoulder, stood in front of it, and came back to the window. He went along that room to that shelf and back to the window again without anybody in that room moving off its own business. Nobody asked him what was on that shelf, and nobody in that room had any idea that he had stood in front of it.

---

At about the seventh hour the woman of about sixty-nine with her tin came up that stair with the tin under her right arm and her own stick in the pocket of her skirt, and she came the whole way up and stood about two feet short of the near end of that bench.

She was in that room and she was in neither of their two mornings. She had not been in that room on the morning when he first put it to anybody, and she was not in the middle of it now, and the two men at the two ends of that bench did not look at her once while it was going.

Then the man who came up that stair turned round off that window and went to the near end of that bench and put it to the man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board for the second time.

He put it to him low, to one man and not to the room, the way he had put it the first time. What he said went out of his mouth into the other man's ear and stayed there. Nobody in that room heard a word of it. There is no leaf on either of those two books with a scratch of it on it, and nothing on the sheet on the bench and nothing on the bare board on the outside of that door and no chalk anywhere in this city that carries one word of what was asked that morning or of what had been asked on the day it was asked the first time.

The man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board had his own hands on the wood of that bench and he looked down at them.

**"I have got a board in front of me and I have had it in front of me every morning since the water came, and I am telling you that I do not remember being asked anything by anybody in this room. I have not got anything in my own head about being asked anything. If somebody put something to me and I did not answer it, then that is a thing I would be carrying, and I am not carrying it, and I have been sitting at this end of this bench every morning looking at the middle of it going down my own board," ** said the man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board, at the near end of that bench with his own hands flat on the wood and the cloth over his own shoulder.

Then the man who came up that stair said one thing back, and he said it to the man of about thirty-nine and to nobody else in that room.

**"Then I am not going to say it again. That is all. I am not going to put it to you a third time and I am not going to tell you what it was, and if you are asked again by somebody it will not be by me and it will not be in this room while I am standing in it," ** said the man who came up that stair, at the near end of that bench with his own coat open and one sleeve of it wet to the shoulder.

Nobody in that room said a word back to either of them. Nobody in that room told either of them that what he had said was right or that it was wrong. Nobody asked the man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board to say it again, and nobody asked the man who came up that stair what it had been, and the woman of about sixty-nine with her tin said nothing about any part of it and did not come near that bench.

What went out of that room went down that stair in two men's mouths and along that row and out past the store's end of it. It is not written down, and it is not written down anywhere in this city, and there is no way now of showing anybody what either of those two men said about it after the light went off that row.

---

Nothing else was said out loud in that room that morning.

The woman of about fifty-two stood at the far end of that bench with her own shoulder to the wood behind the far end of it and gave the whole of that morning back in her face. The keeper went down that column of figures and came back up it. Each of the six strokes in it has a day standing against it and not one struck out, and the seventh place at the foot of that column is bare wood of the colour of the six above it. She put the flat of her own hand on that sheet, kept it there a while, and took it off again.

At about the tenth hour the man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall two stalls along went at the near foot of his own trestle with the edge of his own board and took a shaving off the near side of it. The shaving was pale the colour of the inside of that trestle, and he put it down on the walking space at the foot of it in a heap.

Nobody went and stood in front of that block on the wall. The second book was not picked up and not turned over and not written on by any hand that day, and the dry cloth lay along the head of its leaf.

At about the middle of the afternoon the man who came up that stair went to the window again and stood at it with his own hands hanging at his own sides until the light had gone off the flags. He did not go along that room to the near end of that bench a second time, and nobody in that room said one word to him about anything at all.

---

The grain of the wood at the near end of that bench stood up along about the length of a thumb where his own thumb had gone along it in the middle of that morning, and it stood up pale, and it took the light off the window and held it.
