# Chapter 0983 — A Block Of Writing On The Far Wall

There was a block of writing on the wall along the far side of that room and it was not there at the light.

It was Thursday, the six hundred and sixty-eighth day of the Bare Month. The sun was up before the light for the first time in seven days and came over the roof line at the far end of that market at about the fourth hour. The air went thin along the ground early, so that the light got in under the awning at the store's end and lay the whole length of that bench before the room had finished waking. There was ice in the trough on the flags at the top of that step about the depth of a thumb and nothing had been fetched for it. That bar was where it had been at the light, out of its two sockets on the top step of that stair, with the two of them empty in the far side of the door below. The day was chalked on the outside of that door and the bare board under it stood clear of the near plank by the width of a thumb.

There were about four people a day on that walking space, going their own way along it, while the light was on the flags.

The block stood by itself in the middle of that wall with nothing on the face of it and no mark on any part of it. It ran from about the height of a person's chest to about the height of a person's shoulder, and it was written in one piece without a break in it and without a line ruled above it or under it, and it had no head on it. The plaster either side of it was the colour it had been for eleven days.

> A question asked out loud and not written down cannot be answered again and cannot be taken back, and cannot be proved to have been asked, and two people who remember it will remember two questions, and there is no way of telling which of the two was asked.

Nobody in that room said any part of it out loud. Nobody in that room said it was right and nobody in that room said it was wrong. Nothing whatever was made of it at any hour of that day.

---

The woman of about fifty-two did not go over to that wall at once.

She stood at the far end of that bench from the fifth hour with the window at her back and the light off it, and she looked across that room at that wall the way a person looks at a thing they are going to have to stand near. Two men came up that stair behind her before she moved and went straight on to the near end of that bench, and neither of them looked at the wall.

She stood up off the far end of that bench at about the seventh hour and went across that room and stood in front of the block with her own hands at the sides of her dress.

She stood there about four minutes. The rest of that room was at the other end of it, and what they were doing was the ordinary business of a morning, and none of it came over to that wall.

What went through her while she was standing there was not about the block. It was that she had been at the far end of that bench since the morning she came off that wall and had not sat down on her own stool once in all that time. It was that the stool stands a foot further out from her own wall than it stood before she carried it out of that room. Between the foot of it and the wood of that wall there is a strip about the width of a hand the whole length of it, with grit standing in the joints inside it, and nothing has walked on any part of it. It was that she has been up that stair at the fifth hour every morning since that Thursday and has carried nothing up it at all.

It was also that a person can read a wall from six feet off, standing still, with her hands at the sides of her dress, and that it takes a wall about as long to be read as it takes to say the four things on it. She read it once from where she stood and she did not read it a second time.

She came away from that wall and went back to the far end of that bench and stood where she had been standing and put her own hands at the sides of her dress again.

The block was on that wall when she came back to it, and it had not been touched at any point of those four minutes, and there was no mark on the face of it or on any other part of it, and no hand in that room had gone near it.

---

The rest of that morning went on the way that morning goes, and the wall said what it said while it went on.

The keeper went down the column that holds figures and came back up it, and there is a day standing against each of the six strokes in that column and not one of the six struck out. The sheet lay on the wood of that bench beside the register with three lines on it a hand's width in from its right edge, and nothing was added to it that day.

The tradesman took the cloth along the face of his own board from the top of the top column, and the cloth came off at the foot of the middle column, which is where it comes off every morning. He put the cloth over his own shoulder, took his own board down that stair to his own trestle about the tenth hour, and brought it back up with nothing in his hands.

The man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall two stalls along came up that stair at about the ninth hour and sat in the middle of that bench with his own board flat across his own knees and the bare back of it turned out into the room. He worked along the foot of it with his own thumb and worked back again, twice, and then carried his own board back down that stair to his own trestle and left it standing there face in and did not come up again that morning.

The man of about thirty-one who carries things for a living stood at the near wall with the satchel held up against his own ribs and the flap of it down, and turned about off that wall twice and looked at the flags and turned about again.

The man of about twenty-seven took the bucket down that stair four times and brought it up again four times with the water standing flat in it. On the last of those times he set it down on the flags at the top of that step and stood there with his own hand on the outside of the rim of that trough, looking at the ice.

Two men came up that stair and said nothing at all to anybody, and stood at the door of that room with their own arms hanging. One of them was there an hour; the other of them was gone inside a quarter of it. Nobody went and stood in front of that block but her.

About the eleventh hour the man of about thirty-four went at the near foot of his own trestle again, with the edge of his own board under the earth round it. The earth came away in a crescent about the width of a thumbnail. The flag did not lift and the foot of that trestle did not come up out of it. He got his own hand out from under, looked at the piece of earth he had taken out, and put it down on the walking space where he had taken it from.

---

The dust off the flags of that room had come up on the stair with the woman of about fifty-two that morning. It stood in the creases at the first joints of two fingers of both her hands in a grey line about the depth of a grain of rice, and it stayed where it was when she put her hands back at the sides of her dress.
