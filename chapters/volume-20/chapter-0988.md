# Chapter 0988 — A Thing Said Across Nine Feet Of That Room

The stallholder came up that stair about the ninth hour and took the middle of that bench, his own board flat on his own knees with the bare back of it turned out into the room, and put his own thumb at the head of the middle of it.

It was Tuesday, the six hundred and seventy-third day of the Bare Month. The wind had gone round to the south-west in the night and come with rain that stopped before the light, and the air over eleven miles of flats came off that rain and stood close under the awning at the store's end until about the eighth hour. The sun was over the roof line at the far end of that market at about the fifth hour, and by six the light was in under that awning and standing along that bench as far as the middle of the wood.

The block of writing was still on the wall along the far side of that room and nobody said one word about it. The sheet lay on the wood of that bench at the far end of it with three lines on it a hand's width in from its right edge. There was ice in the trough at the top of that step about the depth of a thumb, and the bar lay out of its two sockets on the top step of that stair. About four people a day went along that walking space with the light on the flags.

---

At about the tenth hour the woman of about fifty-two said one thing out loud in that room.

She said it across about nine feet of that room to the man in the middle of that bench, who had his own thumb going along the foot of his own board at the time and did not stop and did not answer.

**"I have carried that stool out of that room every morning since the water came and I have not sat down on it once, and it is standing a foot further out from that wall than it stood before I carried it out of there. I am going to keep on carrying it up that stair. I am telling you so that you know I know what I am doing with it and that it is not a mistake," ** said the woman of about fifty-two, at the far end of that bench with her own hands at the sides of her dress.

She said it once out loud in that room in daylight and she left it where it lay. She did not say it to him as a putting-to and she did not wait on him for anything. The thing she had said was in nobody's hands. It was not on the sheet at the far end of that bench, and it was not on either of those two books, and it went down that stair in her own mouth when she went at the middle of the afternoon and out under the awning and away along that row.

Nobody in that room answered her, and nobody in that room said she was right or that she was wrong. The stallholder went on along the foot of his own board with his own thumb and did not look up.

---

Nothing else in that room was said out loud that morning.

Nobody went and stood in front of that block that day. It stood on the wall along the far side of that room with the plaster either side of it the colour it had been for eleven days, and every person who came up that stair looked at the bench and the register and the sheet on the bench at the far end of it. The wall was behind all of them, and behind the light as well.

The keeper went down the column that holds figures and came back up it, and squared her own leaf against the fold and lifted the topmost page of it at its outer corner and put it down square again. She did all three of those things once more in the afternoon, working at the head of her own column first and then at the foot of it, which is not the way round she does it.

The stallholder went along the foot of his own board with his own thumb from the top of the middle of it to the foot of it and back up again, three times that day.

The tradesman came up at the fifth hour with his own board under his arm, took the face of it to the cloth, carried it down that stair to his own trestle two along, and brought it back up again with nothing in his hands.

The man of about twenty-seven had his step at the store's end of that row all morning, with the bucket on the stone beside his own foot and the rag folded over the rim of it. He filled it once and left it standing full. He carried it up that stair four times with the water standing flat in it, and on the last of those four he stayed on the flags at the top of that step with his own hand on the outside of the rim of that trough and looked at the ice in it.

The man of about thirty-one who carries things for a living came up at about the sixth hour and stood at the near wall with the satchel held up against his own ribs. At about the middle of the morning he took the satchel down off that wall and set it on the flags against the skirting about a foot to the left of the place he had put it down on the morning before. The flap of it hung away from his own side and it stayed down. Then he turned about off that wall and looked at the flags and turned about again.

She went out at the middle of the afternoon and along that row to the store's end of it. She stood there with her own hands at the sides of her dress for about as long as it takes to say something twice, and then she went on out past the store's end and away, and the stool stood where she had left it in that room.

At about the eleventh hour the keeper wanted to lift the topmost page of her own leaf and could not get her own hand round the near edge of it because of the satchel where he had set it down. She waited about as long as it takes to read a thing twice, and then used the far edge of the bench instead and did it anyway.

---

Along that near wall there are two places in the flags about the shape of that satchel and about the length of a forearm. They are paler than the flags either side of them, and the one he had used on that morning had that satchel standing on it, and the other one had nothing at all standing on it.
