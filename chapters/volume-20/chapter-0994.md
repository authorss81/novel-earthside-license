# Chapter 0994 — What It Cost Her To Say A Name

Adrian Vale came up that stair at about the fifth hour and went the whole length of that room, and what he did at the far side of it was put the flat of his own hand on the plaster at the height of a man's shoulder, about a foot along from where the block of writing stands, and leave it there.

It was Monday, the six hundred and seventy-ninth day of the Bare Month. The night had been hard and clear for the twelfth morning running and the sun was up before the light and came over the roof line at the far end of that market about the fourth hour. The air went thin along the ground early and the light got in under the awning at the store's end and lay the whole length of that bench before that room had finished waking. There was ice in the trough on the flags at the top of that step about the depth of a thumb and nothing had been fetched for it and nothing had been broken in it.

The bar lay where it has lain every morning of that flood, out of its two sockets on the top step of that stair. There was a day in chalk on the outside of that door at the foot of that stair, and the chalk of it stood broken along the joint between the two planks, with about a finger's width of that writing gone down into the groove of it. Under the day the bare board stood clear of the near plank by the width of a thumb and carried one mark on it. The block of writing was on the wall along the far side of that room and nobody had ever said a word about it. That bench at the far end of that room had the sheet lying on it with three lines on it a hand's width in from its right edge, and bare paper below the third of them.

The woman of about fifty-two was at the far end of that bench with her own shoulder to the wood behind the far end of it and her own hands at the sides of her dress. At the near end of that bench the man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board had his own board lying face out on the wood with the cloth over his own shoulder. At about the ninth hour the man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall two stalls along came up that stair and took the middle of that bench, with his own board across his own knees and the bare back of it turned out into the room. The man of about thirty-one who carries things for a living stood at that near wall with the satchel up under his own arm and the flap of it hanging away from his own side.

---

The woman of about twenty-nine who keeps a public register said what it cost her before she said what it was.

**"I am going to say a name in this room that has not been said in it since before the flood, and she is not in it, and I am not going to be able to take it back," ** said the woman of about twenty-nine who keeps a public register, at the far end of that bench with her own hand flat on the wood and the two books open in front of her and the date on the outside of that door behind her own shoulder.

She said it once and she left it where it lay. Not one voice in that room said a word back to her about any part of it.

Then she said it out loud, and nobody in that room answered it.

**"The woman of about fifty-four," ** she said, at the far end of that bench with her own hands flat on the wood.

Then she asked one thing once.

**"What happened on the four hundred and fortieth day," ** she said, at the far end of that bench with the date on the outside of that door behind her own shoulder.

Nobody thanked her for asking it. Nobody agreed with her for asking it, and nobody argued with her, and nobody improved on one word of it.

---

She was answered, one after another, and each of them took about as long as the asking had.

The woman of about fifty-two answered first and she answered standing up at the far end of that bench with her own hands at the sides of her dress.

**"It was a morning when there was no ice in that trough at the top of that step and the rag on the stone lip of it was dry where I went out to it at about the sixth hour. The crack in the near end of it was running a thread you could follow with your eye from the lip of that step all the way across the flags, and the thread stopped in the joint of the flags a foot short of the walking space, and it has stopped in that same joint every morning since. There was a man at the near end of this bench with the cloth over his own shoulder and he did not go down the face of his own board once the whole of that morning, and the door at the foot of that stair stood open on its latch all of it and nobody stood at it," ** said the woman of about fifty-two, at the far end of that bench.

Nobody in that room asked her one word about any part of it.

---

Then the man at that wall said one thing, and it was in the middle of that morning and it was addressed to nobody in particular.

Adrian Vale took the flat of his own hand off that plaster and said it into the middle of that room, and there was nobody in that room he was saying it to.

**"A page of people is a list, and a list is how anybody gets moved off ground they were standing on, and that is the only use it has ever had," ** said Adrian Vale, at the wall along the far side of that room with his own hand coming down off the plaster at the height of a man's shoulder.

The man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board was about nine feet off from him at the near end of that bench with his own thumb going along the foot of his own board. He went on along the foot of that board from the top of the middle of it to the foot of it, and he did not look up and did not answer it. Nobody in that room answered it. Nobody in that room turned about at it.

Nobody in that room said a word about what Adrian Vale had said, and nobody said that it was right and nobody said that it was wrong, and nobody thanked him for it, and no mouth in that room said his name.

Then he put the flat of his own hand back on that plaster at the height of a man's shoulder and left it there for the rest of that day.

---

The man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board answered second, from the near end of that bench, and he answered without getting up off it.

**"It was a morning when the flags of that row were wet through and the grit in the joints of them had gone soft and took whatever anybody put into it. The ice in that trough had been in it since before the light and it was about the depth of a thumb, and I know that because I carried a pail of it up that stair in the morning and put the pail on the flags. The far end of that bench was empty at the fifth hour and she was at the near end of it and not at the far end of it, and she had the flat of her own hand on the page of that register and she did not turn a leaf of it that hour. The light did not come under that awning until well past the eighth hour," ** said the man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board, at the near end of that bench with his own hands on the wood of it.

Nobody in that room asked him one word about any part of it.

---

The man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall two stalls along answered third, from the middle of that bench, with his own board flat across his own knees.

**"It was a morning when that board on the outside wall at the far end of that market was not on its nail. It was standing on the flags at the foot of that step with the face of it against the stone and the bare back of it to the air, and it was back up on that nail before the light had got off the roof line at the far end of that market. No hand in that row would say that they had had it in their own hands that morning, and I have asked about it since and got nothing out of anybody, and that is the whole of what I have got about it," ** said the man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall two stalls along, in the middle of that bench.

Nobody in that room asked him one word about any part of it, and nobody in that room was told that anybody else's account of that morning was the right one.

---

That was the whole of what was done about it.

The keeper went down that column and came back up it, and the six strokes in it have a day standing against each of them and not one of the six struck out, and the bare place at the foot of that column is the colour of the six above it. The second book lay open beside her register with its cover slab drawn back off the leaf under it and the dry cloth along the head of that leaf, and it was not picked up and not turned over and not written on by any hand that day. The two accounts of one day in that room are two accounts of one day. The sheet on the bench kept its three lines and nothing was added to it.

The woman of about fifty-two went down that stair at the middle of the afternoon and along that row and out past the store's end of it, and the stool stood where she had left it. About four people a day went past the foot of that stair while the light was on the flags, and there were other hours of that day when about four people went past it as well.

No mouth in that room put anything to Adrian Vale at any hour of that day. He stood at that wall with the flat of his own hand on the plaster at the height of a man's shoulder from the fifth hour to the going of the light. That plaster was cold at that height, because that wall stands in the shade of that stair and has stood in the shade of that stair every morning of that flood, and the damp of it came off onto the palm of his own hand and stood there.

---

The light came in at that window about the seventh hour and came across that room and fell on the wood of that bench at its far end, where it has fallen every morning since the water came. His own shoulder and his own arm took it off that wood for the rest of that day. About the tenth hour the woman of about fifty-two came back up that stair and put the flat of her own hand on the bare wood where the light had been coming. The dust that had come up that stair on it took the print of her hand, and the print was on the wood of that bench when the light went.
