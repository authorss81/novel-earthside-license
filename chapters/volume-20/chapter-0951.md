# Chapter 0951 — Two Books And A Door Face Read Across

A hand went down the column that holds figures from the head of it to the foot of it and came back up again, and the hand did not stop anywhere on the way down and did not stop anywhere on the way up.

It was Sunday, the six hundred and thirty-sixth day of the Bare Month. The night had been hard and clear, and the frost had gone into the joints of the flags inside that room and into the grit between the flags outside, and the light came in under the awning at the store's end of that market about the fifth hour and lay down the length of that bench without a wind to push it about. The bar lay along the top step of that stair out of its two sockets with the frost standing white along the whole length of it. The two sockets on the far side of the door below stood empty and had frost in them. On the outside of that door the day stood written out in full in chalk, white on the wood, and the bare piece of board under it stood clear of the door by the width of a thumb and carried one mark and nothing else.

The woman of about twenty-nine who keeps a public register had the far end of that bench for her own, with her register open in front of her and the dry cloth laid along the head of her own leaf. The second book lay on the wood beside it with its cover slab drawn back off the leaf under it, and that leaf lying flat. At the near end of the same bench stood the man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board, his own board propped against the end of the wood and his cloth over his own shoulder. The man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall two stalls along sat in the middle of that bench with his own board across his knees and the bare back of it turned out. The man of about thirty-one who carries things for a living stood against the near wall with the satchel held up under his own arm. The woman of about fifty-two kept her far wall with her hands at the sides of her dress and the stool standing against the wood a foot off her own skirt. There was room in that room for standing and for very little else.

---

The keeper went on with her own page. The tradesman went on with the face of his own board, and the cloth came down from the top of the top column and stopped where the middle column came to an end, and he lifted it off and folded it over his own shoulder and did the same again twice. At about the seventh hour the sun came over the roof line at the far end of that market and came along the row with the shade of the trestles in front of it, and where it reached the doorway of that room it stopped short of the flags inside and never came past that.

**"There is a gap at the head of my leaf about the width of a card where the pages have come away from one another in the night, and the damp will get into that gap and sit in it, and I have got the cloth along the head of it and the cloth will not go down into it. That gap is not going to close itself and it is not going to get any worse while I am sitting here, and there is nothing in this room that puts it right."** said the woman of about twenty-nine who keeps a public register, without lifting her own hand off the page in front of her and with the day on the door behind her own shoulder.

**"It wants about the third of it off the near end before anybody can read down the middle of that column at all. It will not come off with a dry cloth. I am not going to put a wet one on that face and have it go under the middle and come up pale at the foot of the two of them."** said the man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board, at the near end with the cloth in his own hand.

The keeper put her own finger into the gap at the head of her leaf and then took it out and looked at the finger and put the finger down on the wood of the bench, and the finger left a print of itself on the wood about as long as a grain of rice. She wiped it off with the corner of her own sleeve, which took some of the frost up with it, and the wood under the print stayed a shade darker than the wood either side of it.

---

Nobody in that room said one word about the mark on the bare piece of board under the date on the outside of that door. The stallholder went down the nine steps and along the row to his own trestle with his own board under his other arm, and came back up again, and sat down in the middle of that bench and said nothing about where he had been. He and the tradesman did not say one word to each other that morning, and neither of them said anything to the keeper about the two books lying open side by side on the wood in front of her.

About four people a day went past the foot of that stair on their own business while the light was on the flags. The door at the foot of it stood open on its latch the whole of it, and not one word of anything said in that room came past the bottom step.

---

The tradesman read the outside face of that door across a second time before the light had got off the roof line, from the far end of the writing to the near end of it, the way a man reads a board across. The keeper read her own page down the column that holds figures and came back up it. The stallholder read the bare back of his own board where it lay across his knees. And the two books lay in the middle of that bench, a leaf showing on one of them and a leaf showing on the other. Neither of them was a page to be read down and neither of them was a surface to be read across. There was nothing whatever in that room that morning to say what either of them was for, or what any day on either of them was.

The four things a person in that room could read that morning did not agree with one another about anything. The column on the keeper's page held six strokes with a day standing against each of them and none of the six struck out. The face of that door held one day written out in full in chalk and one mark under it. The bare back of the stallholder's board held the grain of it and the two battens at the back of it and nothing written at all. And the two books held a day in one of them and a day in the other one. Neither of the two days was the day on the door. Neither of them was a day with a figure standing against it. No mouth in that room compared one of those two leaves with the other of them, and nobody in that room put the four of them together.

---

By the light's going the gap at the head of the keeper's leaf had taken the damp and gone soft along its whole length, so that the edge of the topmost page had come down on to the one under it and lay on it about the width of a nail. The tradesman had come away from the door about nine feet off it, and the reading he had given that face had found one thing he had not known at the light and had not told anybody in that room about it. The outside of that door is made of two planks with a joint running up it, and where the chalk of that day crossed the joint it went down into the gap and stood broken along it, so that every figure of the day is in two pieces with the width of a thumbnail between the halves. The bare board under the day stands clear of the near plank on the side the joint is not on, and the mark on it does not stand under the break in the writing either. It stands about two fingers in from the near edge of that board, which is where a stroke of chalk goes when the hand that makes it has come to the end of what it is doing and has not yet put the chalk down.

Nobody in that room came down nine steps to look at it closer, and nobody in that room asked anybody anything about where it stood or what it was for. The frost that had come out of the joints of the flags inside that room in the morning had gone out of them again by the time the light was off the roofs.
