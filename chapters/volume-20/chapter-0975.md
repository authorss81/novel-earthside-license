# Chapter 0975 — She Stood Up And Then Sat Down Again

The woman of about twenty-nine who keeps a public register came up that stair at about the fourth hour and went to the far end of that bench and stood up behind it with her own hands on the wood and did not sit down.

It was Wednesday, the six hundred and sixtieth day of the Bare Month. The sun came up before the light over eleven miles of flats and came along that row by the fourth hour, and the air had gone warm in the night and stood close under the awning at the store's end until about the seventh hour. Out of its two sockets on the top step of that stair the bar lay where it has lain every morning of that flood, and there was ice in the trough on the flags beside it about the depth of a thumb. Nothing had been fetched for it. The day stood out in chalk on the outside of the door at the foot of that stair, and below it the bare board stood a thumb's width clear of the near plank with the one mark on it that has been there since the morning before this flood came. The box of chalk stood on the table inside that room with the lid of it standing off at one corner. While the light was on the flags, about four people a day went along the foot of that stair.

---

**"I am going to say what this costs before I say what it is, and the cost is mine and it is about me, and not one of the rest of you is paying a part of it. Everything I put to anybody in this room from this morning goes in front of the asking and not behind it, and my hand does it, and I am the one who will read it out before anybody in here has opened their mouth about it. And I am going to say the worse half of it out loud as well, because the worse half wants saying in my own mouth and not in anybody else's. A person who reads himself on that page and says nothing back to it is still on it when the morning is over, and I cannot take him off it, and there is no way back from it for him and not one for me. I have known this since before I came up these stairs. Nobody has to do anything about it. I am not going to tell anybody in this room that they are right about it, and I am not going to tell anybody in this room that they are wrong about it. And if one of you can say it better than I have said it, then it will still be the same words at the end of the morning," ** said the woman of about twenty-nine who keeps a public register, at the far end of that bench with her own hands on the wood and the two books open in front of her and the date on the outside of that door behind her own shoulder.

She said it out loud in that room in daylight. She said it once and she did not say it a second time before the light went or after it, and no mouth in that room said a word back to her about any part of it.

**"It goes in first, or it does not get asked," ** said the keeper, at the far end of that bench with her own hand flat on the page in front of her.

She said that once as well. Then she sat down at the far end of the bench and put her own hand flat on the page and went on with the column that holds figures, from the head of it to the foot of it and back up again. There is a day standing against each of the six strokes in it and not one of the six struck out. The seventh has never gone into that column on any day of that flood. The second book lay open beside her with its cover slab drawn back off the leaf under it and that leaf lying flat and nothing written on it.

---

What the rest of that room did with what she had said was go on with the morning. About nine people were in that room, and in the hour that followed about four of the people in it answered something that was said to them and about four of the people in it said nothing at all. Not one voice in it said she was right and not one voice in it said she was wrong, and the morning went on past her saying the way that room takes a thing, which is by going on.

The man of about twenty-seven carried the bucket down that stair three times and up it three times, with the water standing flat in it. The man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board asked him, at the top of that step with his own two hands on the rim of the trough, whether that rim would take a hoop of the width they get at the wharf. The man of about twenty-seven told him it would take two of them and not one, and neither of those two men went up that step and looked at the rim of it.

The man of about thirty-one who carries things for a living asked the man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall two stalls along, out loud across that room, whether the near foot of that trestle was still gone into its flag.

The stallholder told him it was still gone into it and that he was not going at it in the wet. The stallholder sat in the middle of that bench with his own board flat across his own knees and the bare back of it turned out to the room. He went along the foot of that board with his own thumb twice from the top of it to the foot of it. His own hand went flat against the front of his own coat over the inside breast pocket once and came off again. Two men stood at the door of that room with their own arms along their own sides and said nothing at all to anybody, and one of them was there an hour and the other of them was gone in a quarter of it.

The keeper went down her column and came back up it. The carrier turned about off the near wall and looked at the flags and turned about again. The woman of about fifty-two stood at the far end of that bench with her own hands at the sides of her dress and said nothing to anybody and had no part in the morning at all.

What the keeper had said was still standing in that room at the going of the light, and no hand in it had reached for a chalk, and no leaf of either of those two books had been picked up, turned over, closed or written on at any point of that day.

---

The dry cloth along the head of the keeper's leaf was where she had put it back.

She had taken it up off the head of the leaf about the middle of the morning and carried it the width of her own hand to the bare wood of the bench and laid it down there. The sun came in at that window and lay across the head of the leaf, and she wanted to see the top of the column. She put her own face over the top of the column for about as long as it takes to read down three lines, and then turned the topmost page at its outer corner. Then she took the cloth off the wood of the bench and laid it back along the head of her own leaf where it had lain all that flood.

The wood of that bench under the place where the cloth had lain stood a shade paler than the wood either side of it, along about the whole length of the cloth and about a finger's width wide, and it was the only place on that bench the sun had not been on all day.
