# Chapter 0966 — Rain And Four Hundred Yards Of Flags

Two hands took hold of the two ends of a board and lifted it and set it down again on the wet flags at the far end of that market, and the near end of it went down into the standing water about the depth of a thumb.

It was Monday, the six hundred and fifty-first day of the Bare Month. The rain came in off the flats in the night and had not let up before the light and had not let up all day. Four hundred yards of flags ran from the store's end of that row to where the row gave out into the side of a yard, and by the ninth hour there was not a dry joint in the whole of that length of them. The grit that had stood up in the joints of that row for a week had gone into a paste and was beginning to come out of the joints on the walking side of the row and go away on the wet flags in a pale smear about the length of a hand.

At the store's end of that row the lines the woman of about sixty-nine with her tin had drawn down the joints had gone flat along the top of them where the rain had got into the grit and taken the ridge off. One of them was not standing at all. It had been going out and coming back since before the light on an earlier morning of that flood and was not the first of them and will not be the last.

---

The man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall two stalls along walked the length of that row three times that morning.

He walked it at the light, and again about the ninth hour when the water had come up to the walking spaces and taken the walking spaces out, and again in the middle of the afternoon. His own trestle stood in the walking space and the feet of it had been going into the flags where the grit had been taken out from under them by a week of carting. On the second of those three walks he found that the near foot of it had about two fingers of water standing round it, and had not had that the day before.

He got his own hand at the near foot of it and worked at the flags round it with his own thumb and could not lift that foot. He worked at it for the better part of an hour with his own knee in the water and it did not come. He stopped working at it before the light began to go and did not try it again.

He put the flat of his own board down along the walking space on either side of it and took his hands out of the water and wiped them down his coat. He had the middle column of his own board across his knees for a while with his own thumb along the foot of it, and the rain came down the face of that board and off the bottom of it onto his own coat where it was not.

**"That foot is going into that flag and I cannot get it out of it with a thumb, and I am not going to put my shoulder against that trestle in this rain and try to lift it out of it, because if it goes over in the wet it goes over on the walking space and it takes everything on that board down with it. It has not gone over. I have got it standing and it is standing, and that is what I came out here for."** said the man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall two stalls along, at the far end of that row with his own knee in the standing water and his own hand on the foot of that trestle.

---

At the end of that walk was the far end of that market and the outside wall there.

The board on that wall stands on one nail above its step and has stood on that nail since long before this flood, and the nail had begun to draw out of the wall a little at the head and take the top of the board with it. Above the board there is a second hole in that wall, and there is nothing in it, and no second nail has gone into that wall on any day of this flood. It is not the round hole a nail leaves, because a nail comes out of a wall along the line it went in and leaves a hole longer than it is wide, and that hole was not longer than it was wide. The wood inside it had gone hard and dark and there was a burr turned down on the near lip of it about the depth of a grain of rice.

That day the rain got into it. It stood in that hole full. It stood there because that hole goes back into the wall about the depth of two fingers and there is nothing at the back of it to let the water out at. It came out over its own lower lip and went down the wall in a thread about the thickness of a pencil, and came to the top of that board and went along the top of it and off the bottom of it onto the step below.

---

By the light's going the board on that wall had taken that thread of water down its face for the length of itself. The writing on it had gone soft at the foot of the top row and the six smaller rows under it had gone soft along their bottom edges, and the water stood in the hole above it and came out of it at the same rate.

The board was still standing on its one nail, and the nail stood in the wall it had been in, and the second hole above it was full of rain and empty of anything at all. Nothing went into that wall that day and nothing came out of it. The wood around the lip of that second hole had gone soft where the rain had been standing in it, about the width of a nail all round, and that softening had not been there at the light.
