# Chapter 0251 — A Case Comes Up The River

A case the size of a bread chest came off a barge at the salt wharf at about the sixth hour of the morning on the ninety-sixth day of the Long Month, and the barge was going on to Silt Row and would not lie alongside again that day, and the man who took the case off it put it on a handcart and has not taken his hand off it since. The light on that quay was flat, the cold came up off the water and not down out of the sky, the wind was coming across the flats and had been at the wall of the store on the upper road for twenty-two days, and what was under that man's hands all morning was an oak lid, a cold bar and a knot of oiled cloth tied twice. About four hundred people were on that quay, and the posting on the wall of that store has been up fifty days and carries one line of the nine it went up with, and the board above that door carries the figures of the sixty-ninth day of that month and has not been written on since the day after them.

---

The man of about forty who carries sacks has never in his life carried anything that was not a sack, and he said so on that quay to nobody twice, and the second time was because a docker had asked him what was in it and he had already answered the first one. The woman of about twenty-nine who keeps a public register was on that quay from the seventh hour, because a wharf is a place where things arrive, and she came down the boards, looked at the cart, then at the seal, then at the man's face, and he took his hand off the bar for about as long as it takes to wipe a palm on a coat and put it straight back.

"There is salt in it, and it is a box, and I have never in my life carried anything that was not a sack. A sack is a thing you can get an arm round. A box is a thing you put a hand on and then it owns the day."

"Whose box."

"I did not ask and I am not going to. I am paid by the load and not by the errand, and that is a different trade from the one you are thinking of, and I have said it on this quay before and nobody hears it twice because I do not come here often."

A docker asked him what was in it and went back to his work without waiting for an answer.

"You could set it down on the boards there."

"I could set it down on the boards there, and there is nobody up that road who can be given it, and I would then be a man who left a case on a quay. A man who leaves a case on a quay gets asked about it every morning after that for the rest of his life, and the asking is free and the answering is not."

Two more people came within reach of the cart and looked at the seal and did not put a hand out, and the man of about forty moved it a foot along the boards and waited for her to catch up, and she did not, and about four people on that quay have worked out that a woman who does not catch up with a cart is a woman who has not agreed with it.

"Then where."

"Up the road. On the boards of a room. In daylight, with people in it, where a thing can be put down in front of witnesses and stop being anybody's problem."

"**I have asked that question for two hundred and fifty-one days and nobody up that road has answered it once. A man paid to carry and a woman paid to write down what arrives are the same kind of person.**"

He said it to her and not to the quay, and about four people at the cart understood all of it.

"The kind being that neither of us can put anything down."

"**The kind being that neither of us can put anything down. I have had that since the seventh hour and it is the whole of what I know about a thing I have not been asked to open.**"

The man of about forty looked at the cart and not at her and said that he had not got a reason, and she said that a man who has not got a reason is the only kind of man she can put a thing in front of, and about four people on the quay heard it.

"What are you going to do with it."

"**I am going to walk it up nine miles and put it on a table in a room with a door, and I am going to stand in that room until it is dark, and I am not going to be the man who opened it.**"

"And if nobody opens it."

"**Then it stands shut for a night, and a thing shut for a night in front of nine people is a different object from a thing shut for a night in front of nobody. That is the only trade I have.**"

She wrote nothing on that quay. She looked at the knot and the seal, and then at the boards the cart stood on, which were the boards of a wharf that takes salt, and then she said the thing that decided the morning, and the man of about forty wrote it down afterward from memory and it came out the same wording both times, and about four people in this city have said that a thing written twice from memory and the same both times is worth more than a copy.

"It is going up the road, and nobody on that road can be given an instrument, because there is no office between this quay and the flats that receives one."

"That is a fact and not a rule."

"It is what is there, and I would like it entered that I did not make a rule this morning, because I make rules in a room with a date on the door and there is no date on that door yet. What is going to happen is that about nine people stand in a room and look at the outside of it and not one of them opens it, and the not-opening is the only thing done to it that day."

The cart went up the road at about the seventh hour with a hand on it. It wanted a wheel off the flat joint twice in a mile and both times the man got his shoulder into the shafts and stopped it instead of lifting the case. The case was shut when it came off the barge and shut when it came up the road, and the seal under the oiled cloth was the colour of a wet stone. Twice on that road somebody came up beside him, looked at the seal and said nothing, and both of them were people who sell by weight.

---

The room over a market has a step at the back of it and no name on the door, and somebody had put a date on that door in chalk about the fourth hour of the afternoon, and it was not a date that any of them would have put there, and the register-keeper saw it and left it alone, and it was still on that door at the seventh hour of the evening.

She took the docket off the handle in front of about nine people. She did not read it. She copied onto a second slate what was on the outside of it, in the order she saw it, and she copied the seal before she copied the writing, and she copied the place at the bottom after the writing and not before, and about four people in that room watched her hand and not the docket. She was on her knees for most of it, because the case was on a low bench, and the wet stone left its mark on one palm, and she put that hand behind her back.

"The top of it is a date and it is not a date that anybody in this city writes."

"In this city the top of a page is a day of this flood and a day of a month, and that is two lines and both of them are ours. That is one line and it is not ours, and I am going to put down what it says and not what it means."

"What does it mean."

"**I have not got to the meaning and I am not going to get to it in front of a slate. A meaning I cannot check is a thing I would be inventing, and inventing for a page is the one trade I have a rule about.**"

She asked the room then whether anybody was going to say the name of a thing that has no name yet, and nobody did, and the silence on that point lasted longer than the reading did.

"And the bottom."

"A place. It is the fourth place on the page, and it is nine hundred miles from this room, and the building it was written in is nine hundred miles from this room."

"**And there is no road between those two places that anybody in this city has been on. I have looked for it on about four walls here and I looked before anybody asked me to.**"

She did not write the place on the slate while she was saying it. She wrote it afterwards, in the fourth place, in the order she had seen it in. Then she read the slate back once, in the same words, in the same order, and stopped when she got to the bottom of it. She read it back a second time about a minute later, in exactly the same words again — the same order, the same pause, the same place where she stopped — and about four people in that room noticed the second reading and none of them said one word about it.

Somebody asked her why she had read it twice. She said the first reading had been for her and the second for the room, and that there is a difference between those two which she has never written down, and that there has been a date on that door in chalk since the fourth hour of the afternoon and it is not one of hers. Nobody asked her what the difference was, and about four people in that room have wanted to ask about nine times in a month.

A man of forty-one in a very good coat came in at about the tenth hour and stood at the head of the table with his hands behind him, and read the four lines of the docket out loud in the local language, in order, without a note. His voice did not change on any of the four, and there was nothing in that room to check any of them against, because the case was shut and the docket was the only page in the world that anybody in that room had ever seen.

"That is the whole of it. Four lines. An emergency is declared in the crossing and in the ground adjoining it. The days upon every gate between this city and the gates of the west road are to be entered in a schedule. The schedule is to be made up and kept by a company. And in every line of that schedule there are four places, and the four places are a date, a gate, a holder, and the days."

Nobody answered him. A woman of about twenty-four wrote the four places down in the order he had said them and put nothing else on the slate, and turned it round so that the room could see it. There were four short lines on it and no heading over them.

"It does not name the store."

"It does not name the store. It names no building, no yard, no door and no queue anywhere in it, and I have been in the building where it was signed, and there is a man there whose trade is knowing what a page does not say."

"You have been in that building."

"I did not ask him about this, because nobody asked me anything about this. I have been in that building and I have said two sentences about it and that is all I am going to say in this room."

About four people in that room had been waiting all morning for him to say who had written it, and he had not said.

"Nine sentences is what I have heard you say today."

"**Then you have counted. I have kept the count, and I am not going to say the ninth one in front of a room, because in nine years of keeping it I have never once found out afterwards what I said in the tenth.**"

He put his hand inside his coat, checked something there, and took his hand out again, and nobody in that room remarked on either half of that.

---

A woman of about twenty-four fetched Adrian Vale at about the seventh hour of the evening, and she was not sent by anybody, and she said so on the step at the back of the room before she said anything else. The man of about forty was still in that room because he had been asked whether he was going to take the case back down the road in the morning and had said he would sit until it was dark. He had his glove off and his other hand flat on the lid, and that hand had not moved once in three hours.

Adrian Vale came up the step, and the woman of about twenty-four said his name, and he answered it in about a second. He said one word. It was entered afterward, by the register-keeper and in her own hand, that he had answered quickly, that he had entered it himself, and that nobody had made him, and that a man of thirty-two on no roster of anything had given a person a name in one second and had not been asked for anything in exchange for it.

"You will want to know what it says."

"I have been in that room three hours and I have not opened it and I am not going to open it, and there is nothing in that for you to want. Get the room to write down what is on the outside of it and let the room argue about it tomorrow."

"You are not the first man to say that on this step and it has never once worked, and I do not say that to be unkind. I say it because you will find out on Tuesday and I would rather you found out from me."

"I will take it from you, then."

The woman of about twenty-four put her slate down face up on the table and left it there.

"**Nobody takes anything from me, and that is the trouble with me. A check that gets taken something stops being a check and starts being a person with a grievance.**"

Nobody in that room said anything for a while, and about nine people on the landing outside had gone down the stairs.

"I have written down what is on the outside of it and read it back twice in the same words, and about four people in this room heard the second reading and not one of them said a word."

The man of about forty turned the case a quarter of a turn on the bench so that the handle faced the door.

"Then you are the only person in this city who has handled a page in the ordinary way since the first of the new month."

"**I would like you to be the fifth person who says nothing about the second reading. Every other kind of help has cost somebody a day they were using for something else.**"

The man of about forty had not moved from the wall in four hours and had not said one word in any of it, and a man who has not been asked a question in four hours has been in a room and not in a conversation, and there is no instrument in four hundred miles for finding out the difference.

The man of about forty got up off the wall about a quarter of an hour later and put his hand on the case and carried it out to the cart on the stair, and he did not open it and he did not look at it again and he did not say anything else for nine miles.

"You never found out what was in it."

"No. And I have been on that road twice this month with a thing I have not opened, and the other time I knew what was in it, and I have worked out since the seventh hour that those are two different trades and only one of them pays."

"Then you are the only man in this city who has carried nine hundred miles of paper nine miles and never found out what is in it, and I have been thinking about that on that road since the seventh hour, and I am paid by the load and I have never once been paid for that."

"I would like it entered that I said that and nobody answered me, because a man who is leaving says the last thing and then the room forgets it, and it is nine words and I have had them since the sixth hour of this morning."

---

And the case stood on the boards of a room over a market with a docket tied twice to the handle of it, and about four people who had wanted it opened had gone home without saying so, and the four places on that docket were a date, a gate, a holder and the days, and about nine people in that city knew the word *holder*, and about nine of them did not know whether it was a word for a thing or a word for a person.
