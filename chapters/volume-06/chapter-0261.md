# Chapter 0261 — Which Gate Is Which

The second line of a docket from nine hundred miles away wants the days upon every gate between this city and the gates of the west road entered in a schedule, and on the sixth day of the Short Month a room over a market found out that it could not write even the first line of one because it did not know where the first gate was, and the date on the door of that room was in chalk and the day was a Wednesday. The board over the door of the store on the upper road has not been written on since the day after the sixty-ninth day of the Long Month, and the figures standing on it belong to that sixty-ninth day and are thirty-seven days old this morning, and it has stood unwritten thirty-six days, and the wind has been at that wall for thirty-two days. The case has been shut on a table in the room next door since the first day of the Short Month and it was shut then and it is shut now, and that is ten days.

---

The woman of about twenty-four had the date on the back of her slate before anybody in that room had put a coat down, and she wrote it and then she put the chalk into her apron and did not take it out again for the rest of the hour, and about four people in the room saw her do both of those things and none of them asked her what the date was for.

A man of about thirty-nine who trades on the Open Hand board was against the wall by the step at the back where the light does not reach, with his own slate on his knee, and he had not spoken since he came in and he did not speak in that first hour at all, and his right hand lay flat over the face of the slate the whole time of it, the way it does.

"You have a question with four words on it."

"**I have a question with four words on it and I have had it since the second day of that month and nobody in this room has asked it, so I have been carrying it about like a man carrying somebody else's bag and putting it down in kitchens.**"

"You have not said the four words."

"I am going to say them now. The four words are *which gate is which*, and there is a great deal underneath them, and the first of the great deal is that nobody in this city can lay a line on a floor with a finger and put a mark at the end of it and tell a clerk nine hundred miles away which gate the mark is standing for." She turned the slate round and set it on the table with the writing on the face up. "**That line is the second line of the docket and the other three are standing behind it, and it wants every gate between here and the gates of the west road, and we cannot find the first one to begin at.**"

Somebody let a door go in the passage and the draught came in under the door and moved the corner of the chalk on the sill a half inch, and about nine people in that room have said since that they had stopped watching the chalk and had started watching where the draught came from.

A smith of about sixty came in at about the third hour of the morning with a boy's hand on the bar of a cart behind him at the bottom of the step, and he was not let in by anybody and he came in, and about four people in that room have said since that a man who is sixty and has walked up a step on his own has a different standing in a room than a man who was asked to.

"My yard is the third street off this market and I have been the smith since before the flood, and I have not been in this room before and I have not come to be asked anything."

"You are the smith who hung a hinge on a gate out past the last named house."

"**I am that smith. I hung one in the second week of the dry spell and one in the fourth, and I was paid for both of them and I was not asked anything either time.**" He put a thumb and a forefinger on the edge of the table and left them there, and the chalk on the sill moved again while he was not looking at it. "I have turned that not-being-asked over about nine times since the second one and I have not got to the bottom of it. A gate wants a hinge about once in two years, and there is a road out there with the wrong ones on it, and that is my whole trade in that direction."

He said that and then stopped, and the man by the step at the back moved a boot a half inch along the boards, and about four people in that room have said since that a man who has said a true thing about his own trade and then stopped has to be waited out by whoever is sitting at the table with him.

"You are going out there this morning."

"My boy is going out there this morning with a bar of tallow and a length of chain and the hinge off the third bench." He turned and looked at the boy in the doorway, who was about thirteen and had the tallow under one arm and a coil of chain over his shoulder and was chewing. "He goes out on a Tuesday and a Wednesday and a Friday and he has gone out on about nine Tuesdays since the weather turned. **He is going because I have a hinge to hang. He is not going because a room over a market has begun to be interested in him, and if anybody here wants to be interested in him they can go out and walk.**"

The man by the step at the back lifted his hand off the face of his slate and put it back, and about four people in that room have said since that he had not moved it once in an hour and that the lifting of it was the largest thing that happened in that room before the fourth hour of the afternoon.

---

The boy got as far as the first gate at about the fifth hour of the morning. It was shut and barred, and there was a man on the far side of it who was about fifty-five and had a bar in his two hands and had been standing there a good while.

"Which gate is this."

"**That is the gate to the second named house, and it is my gate, and if you have come out here to tell me I have been told then you have walked a long way to say it.**" He did not put the bar down. "I open it for the cart and I shut it after, and nobody has ever asked me whether I am allowed to, and I have had it since before your flood and it was my father's before it was mine."

"Who asked you to put a chain on it."

"**Nobody asked me. The chain is because a cart came through in the night in the first month and went past the house, and I have not slept easier about it since, and that is the whole of the reason and it is mine and I am not putting it in a book.**"

The boy shifted the chain on his own shoulder to get the bar of tallow out from under it and the man on the far side of the gate watched him do it, and about nine people on that road have said since that a boy rearranging a load in front of a man holding a bar is a thing that changes the shape of a conversation.

"You are out past the last named house then."

"I am not out past anything. The last named house is a long way on, and you are going to find that every gate on this road has a man at it who will tell you the same thing in a different voice, and I am the first of them and I am the last one that will talk to you today, because I have a chain on and I am the wrong man to talk to." He shouldered the bar a quarter of an inch and the chain moved on its staples. "**Go on up. And do not ask them the same question, because they will not take it the same way, and the ones past the last house will not take it at all.**"

The boy went up. The light on that road was flat and white and came off low ground on both sides of it, the cold got into the iron of the chain by the time he had it over his shoulder again, and what was under his hands for the rest of that morning was a hinge and a length of chain and a bar of tallow, and he had a knotted string on the cart rail for the gates and he was knotting it by feel because he had lost the light for doing it by eye.

---

At the fourth gate a woman of about fifty said the road was kept and would not say by whom, and at the fifth a man splitting wood said his father's brother had the turn, and at the sixth a boy about as old as him said that everybody took the bar when it was their week. He had three knots in the string by then and the fourth of the gates he was sure of was the second one, because he had been through both of the first two as a child with his father pulling the tail of a rope.

"What is the turn."

"That is the turn. Everybody takes the bar when it is their week." The boy with the wood looked at him and then down at the bar and then back at him. "**Nobody writes it down and nobody asks and everybody knows, and if you want to know whose week it is you walk to the house and ask, and I will tell you now and it is not written anywhere and it is my mother's week.**"

The boy put a knot in the string for that and pulled it tight with his teeth, and he did not go past the last named house, because there was a woman at the last gate with a key on a string over her shoulder who looked at the cart and then at him and asked him whether the chain was for the gate or for the cart, and he told her it was for the gate, and she said that it was long, and he said that it was, and about four people on that road have said since that a boy with a hinge was asked a question about a chain by the last person on it and answered it and turned round, and he came about nine hundred paces back down that road with the tallow still under his arm and the four knots on a string in his hand.

Two of the four knots were for gates he was sure of and two were for gates he was sure he had passed, and those are not the same thing, and about nine hundred people have walked that road in the ten weeks since the water went out of the low ground and not one of them has written a list of anything on it, and about four of them have said out loud, on other roads and in other rooms, that the road is kept.

---

He came into that room at about the fourth hour of the afternoon and put the knotted string on the table and began taking the chain off his shoulder, and about four people in that room watched a boy of thirteen do three things at once and not one of the three of them was for the room.

"Say it in order."

"**First gate, a man, and he says it is his. Second gate, one I know from being a child. Third, new to me. Fourth, a woman, says the road is kept and stops there. Fifth, a man splitting wood, says his father's brother had the turn.**" He turned the bar of tallow over in his hands and set it down again on the sill. "**Sixth, a boy my own age, and he said everybody takes the bar when it is their week, and that is the only one of the six that came with a rule on it.**"

"And after the sixth."

"After the sixth there is the last named house and then a gate past it with a woman at it who said nothing at all, and I did not go past her. I do not know how many gates are between the sixth and hers and neither do you, and I have been trying to work it out on the way back down and I have got the number out of my head twice." He untied the string and shook it out flat on the table. "**Six answers, and two of them are about a turn, and not one of the six is a name. Four knots on this and I am sure of four and I am not sure of two, and about four people on that road told me a gate was a gate I had not been through.**"

The man by the step at the back took his hand off his slate for the second time that day and left it off, and the light in the room had by then come off the low ground outside and gone along the floor to the wall, and about four people in there have said since that the two men in that room on that day said the same number of words in the same amount of time in the opposite order. He had a slate and he had put nothing on it, and about nine people who were in that room have said since that a man who has sat for five hours with a slate on his knee and a hand on it and has not written one line is a man who came to that room to find out whether there was anything to write, and that he found out.

"So there is a turn."

"There is a turn, and there is a chain, and there is a bar, and there is a woman at a gate who would not say anything to a boy with a hinge. And not one of those four things has a place in a schedule. **The docket wants a date, and a gate, and a holder, and the days. We cannot do the second of the four, and we have been arguing about the third of the four for nine days, and the trouble was never going to be where anybody in this room thought it was.**"

"You could name the gates."

"**I could name two of them and I have never written one of them down in my life, and if I put four names on a slate in a room then in about four months somebody can be shown that this room knew which gates were which and did not send the list.**" The woman of about twenty-four put her hand flat over the knots on the string and left it there. "That is a very much worse sentence than this room not knowing. A gate is not a thing anybody in this city can put down, and the reason is not that it is hidden. The reason is that everybody who has ever been shown one of them already knows it, and nobody has ever thought of a gate as a thing anybody could want."

A man at the back of that room asked a question about the fourth place, and neither of the two people at that table answered it, and about four people in the room have said since that they noticed the two of them not answering and did not know which of the two had decided not to.

The room emptied at about the sixth hour and the string stayed on the table until the woman of about twenty-four put it in her apron, and the four knots on it were the four the boy was sure of, and about nine people in this city have said since that a boy of thirteen went out on his own errand and came back with the only list anybody in this city has of anything to do with that road, and that the list is four knots in a piece of string, and that it is in a pocket, and that nobody asked him for it.
