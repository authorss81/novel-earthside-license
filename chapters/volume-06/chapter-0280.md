# Chapter 0280 — A Keeper And A Holder

A woman of about twenty-four put a date in chalk on the door of a room over a market in a market town nine hundred paces past the last named house on the twenty-fifth day of the Short Month, a Monday, and a Hearthguard of forty-four took a slate out of inside his coat and put it on the table face down, and turned it over, and turned it back. The light came in on the north side and went off the low ground by about the second hour of the afternoon, and the cold was in the shutters and out of the wind, and what was in that room was a slate, a piece of chalk, a bar stood against the near post of the door, and people. Nobody in that room had asked to be in it and about one of the four had not been asked whether the day suited him and had not asked.

---

Nobody in that room knew how old it was, and about four people in that market town have said since that the first thing anybody said about a book on a table is that nobody can say how old it is, and that about four hundred years is what a person says when they mean a long time, and that about nine people in that room said about four hundred years and no two of them said it about the same book.

The woman of about twenty-four put her hand flat on the slate and did not turn it and did not look at it for about as long as it takes to fill a pail, and about four people in that room have said since that a woman who puts her hand flat on a page she has not read yet is a woman who is deciding whether she is going to be the one who says it out loud.

"You are going to read it."

"I am going to read it in the local language in daylight in a room with a date on the door, and that is the only way a thing gets said out loud in this flood that anybody can be shown later, and I have been the one who read a Crown page out in four kitchens by accident and I am not going to read this one by accident." She turned it round. "**It is a roll of gates. A gate, a keeper, and a set of days, and that is the whole of it, line for line, and there is nothing else on it and nothing else on the back of it.**"

"You have read it."

"I have read it in about a minute and I have not read it twice and I am going to read it out once and then I am going to stop, and people in that room have worked out that a woman who says she is not going to read it twice is saying that she does not want to be the person who has an opinion about it." She looked at the man of about thirty-six. "**A gate is a place. A keeper is the person who opens it. A set of days is the days.**" She put two fingers on the third line and did not move them. "Those are three of the four places on the Crown's page. The one that is not on this is the one that is not on that either, and I am not going to say the word out loud in a room where about four of us have said it this month, because the fifth time it comes out it is a fact instead of an argument, and I would like about nine people in this market to go on arguing about it for another month."

---

The man of about thirty-six got up off the bench and went and stood at the near post of that door where his bar was and put his hand on it and did not pick it up, and about four people in that room have said since that a man who gets up and puts a hand on a bar in a room where a roll of gates has just been read out is a man who has found out that the bar in his hand is on somebody's page and has been for about a hundred and forty days.

"It is a roll of the gates on this road, and nobody in it is a company, and nobody in it is a household, and nobody in it is a person who has been asked anything."

"It is a roll of the gates on this road and I have read it out, and I am not going to say anything about it beyond what is on it, and about four people in this room have said that a woman who reads out a page and then refuses to have a view of it is the only kind of reader anybody in four hundred miles has." She squared the slate to the edge of the table. "A description is a removal with a column for where the person is." She did not look up. "**That is a sentence somebody said out loud in a room in this city before any of the four of us had heard of a Crown case, and people in that room have heard it and about four hundred have not.**" She put two fingers on the third line again. "I am saying it here because there is a page on that table with three places on it and no fourth, and somebody in four hundred miles is going to want to know what the fourth one was."

Nobody in that room said anything about that for a while, and about four people in that room have said since that a woman who puts a sentence of somebody else's into a room on a Monday without saying whose it is has done something that about four people in four hundred miles have never done, and that it was not done out of modesty.

---

Roke turned the slate over and turned it back and did not take his hands off it, and about four people in that room have said since that a man who turns a slate over and back in a room in which it has just been read out is a man who has decided that whatever is on the other side of it is not for this room.

"That roll is the outer ward's."

"That is the ward's and the ward's is the Marches', and I keep the road out of this city gate by gate, and a body that keeps gates keeps a roll of them." He did not turn the slate. "I have not been asked for that roll in about nineteen years, and people in this room have said that is the worst sentence anybody has said in here this morning, and I have not argued with it, and I am not going to."

"Who keeps it."

"The company keeps it, and the company is four men out of a ward and I am one of them and I sign for the ward, and the roll is not a book anybody signs. It is a board with the gates on it and the days under each gate, and it has been kept that way for longer than the oldest thing any of us has."

Somebody in the doorway moved a chair about four inches and did not carry it any further, and about four people in that room have said since that a person who moves a chair as far as a door and then stops has worked out that a room with a date on the door is not a room a chair should be brought into, and that this is the third time that has happened at that door in about a month and that about nine people in that market have made the judgement and about four of them have said so out loud.

He took his hands off the slate and did not put them in his pockets. "There is no column on it for a person. There is a gate and a keeper and a set of days, and there has never been a column on it for a person." He put the slate down flat. "**I have been keeping it for nineteen years and I have never once been asked to put a name against a keeper on it, because a name against a keeper is not a keeper.** That is all I have got, and I am not going to be asked a second question about it, and about four people in this room have noticed that I said a second question and nobody has asked one."

"You are not going to be asked a second question about it and you have not been asked the first one by anybody in this room."

"**I have not been asked the first one either, and I have said the answer to it out loud in a room in daylight on a Monday because there is a date on the door and people in here will be able to be shown that I said it, and that is the whole of what I have come for and it is four minutes' worth.**"

---

The woman of about thirty-seven who had come up that road with him took her slate out of her coat and put it face down on the bench and did not open it, and about four people in that room have said since that a woman who puts a slate face down beside a page that has just been read out in daylight is a woman who has understood that there are two of them on that table and neither of them can be put beside the other, and that about nine people in four hundred miles could be shown both of them in a room and have worked out that nobody ever has.

Two rolls of the same gates. One of them had a heading on it in a word that a room in this city had said out loud once, on the eighteenth day of that month, to about four people. The other had no heading and three places on a line and had been kept by four men for longer than the oldest thing any of them had. Both of them were about the gates on this road and both of them were in a room in a market town nine hundred paces past the last named house, on the twenty-fifth day of the Short Month, a Monday, at about the eleventh hour of the morning, with a date on the door.

The man of about thirty-six took his hand off the bar at about the half hour and put it on the edge of the table instead and looked at the slate for about a minute, and people in that room have said since that a man who moves a hand off a bar and onto a table in front of a page he has not been asked about is a man who has stopped being responsible for the object in the room and started being responsible for the thing in the room, and that about nine people in four hundred miles would not know the difference and about four of them do.

"Nobody is going to say what the difference is."

"Nobody is going to say what the difference is and nobody is going to be asked, and about four people in this room have worked out that a difference which nobody will say out loud and which everybody in the room can name is a difference that belongs to a book and not to a morning, and that about four hundred years of people have been putting those in different places." He did not touch the slate. "**And I am not going to say it, and I would like it entered that I am not going to say it and not that I do not know it.**"

The woman of about thirty-seven said nothing and the woman of about twenty-four said nothing and about four people in that room have said since that two women saying nothing in a room at the same moment is not an agreement and is not a refusal, and that in a room in this flood it is a third thing, and that about nine people in that market town have watched it happen four times this month and about four of them can name what it was about and about four of them cannot.

Nobody in that room put them side by side. A woman of about twenty-four read one of them out and did not read the other, and a man of about thirty-six said that a keeper is not a holder and did not say what the difference was worth, and a Hearthguard of forty-four said that there has never been a column on it for a person and did not say why, and about four people in that market town have said since that a keeper and a holder are two words for two different arrangements and that the distance between the two arrangements is one man's life and about four hundred miles of road, and that nobody in that room worked out which man, and that about nine people in four hundred miles could not have worked it out either, and that the woman of about twenty-four turned the slate face up again and the woman of about thirty-seven left hers face down, and that about four people who have been in that room have said since that a page face up and a page face down on one table in a room in daylight with a date on the door is the only picture anybody out there has of what two Crowns did to the same gates, and that nobody in that room said that, and that neither of the two of them wrote the sentence at the front of the compact down anywhere, and that it belongs to somebody and is not a name and is not on a page anybody in four hundred miles can hold, and that about four people in that market town have said since that a Monday in a room with a date on the door is the cheapest thing this flood has produced and is the only one anybody out past the last named house can hold up.
