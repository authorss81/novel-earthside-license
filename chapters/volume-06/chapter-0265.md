# Chapter 0265 — Nine Days Of Hands

The tenth day of the Short Month was a Sunday and the light on that channel bed was flat white and did not get any warmer as the morning went on, and the cold was in the wet clay and in the iron of the barrow and out of the wind, and what was under about twenty pairs of hands at that channel from the seventh hour of the morning until the light went was a spade, a barrow, a footboard, and about four feet of clay that had not been turned in about a hundred days. The wind has been at the wall of the store on the upper road for thirty-six days, and it has been there since the two hundred and twenty-ninth day of this flood. The board over that door carries the figures of the sixty-ninth day of the Long Month and nothing has been put on it in the forty days since the day after them, so the figures a person can see up there are forty-one days old. And a case from nine hundred miles away has stood shut on a table in a room over a market for fourteen days, counting from the ninety-sixth day of the Long Month, and nobody has carried it up that road.

---

The man of about thirty-six of the market town came out at about the sixth hour of the morning with the company's barrow and did not call at any of the nine houses, and the woman of about thirty at the stall walked the nine hundred paces down the road behind him carrying a pot on her head, and about four people in that village have said since that the two of them arrived at the same time from opposite ends of the same errand, and that neither of them had been asked to come and both of them came.

"You said about nine days."

He set the barrow down in the lane and turned it round so that the handles were towards the houses and not towards the road, and about nine people in that village have said since that a man who turns a barrow round in a lane before he says anything has decided where the day's work is going to begin.

"**I said about nine days of hands and one post, and I said it yesterday at the second hour of the afternoon and I have not moved the figure since, and about four of you came out at the seventh hour this morning and about nine of the nine houses have somebody in them at that channel.**" He put his hands on the handles and did not lift it. "The post is still out at the bottom and the bar has been lying on the floor of the ninth house since the second week, and there is not one of them in that gate on the road, and the road is not going to be asked, so the only water there is going to be what nine houses can lift by hand out of the low end, and about nine hundred buckets is what nine houses lift in a day, and the fourth household needs it at the low end where the barrow cannot go."

"Then the fourth household has no water today."

"The fourth household has about four hundred buckets of water today instead of about nine hundred, and the rack at the low end is a week old and it has not been worked and a rack that has not been worked does not come off, and that sentence is about a hundred and forty years old and it is the only thing about that channel that anybody can check."

"Then it stays."

The woman of about thirty at the stall had come back up the lane with the butt empty on her head and had put it down against the fourth house, and she was standing at the man's left-hand side while the man of about thirty-six was talking, and about four people in that village have said since that she said nothing for about a minute and a half and that the minute and a half was her own arithmetic and not patience.

"It stays where it is and it does not get lifted and it does not get carried up the road to be worked, and I have watched about four people in this flood try to carry a rack up a hill and it does not work and it does not stop being a rack." He picked the barrow up and went to the edge of the bed. "**We are not going to get it back in a week. I want that said in a kitchen before the middle of the day and not at the end of it.**"

---

They dug from the seventh hour. A rack stands in a channel and a channel takes its water from a gate, and the channel at the second village takes its water from a gate in the road that has not been opened for four weeks, and what there was to dig on a Sunday morning in about four weeks of dry was a footboard, laid by somebody for a walking reason, over about four feet of clay that had gone to the consistence of a loaf.

The first two hours were barrow work and the barrow was the whole of it, and the woman of about thirty at the stall filled it from a butt she had brought down the road on her head and nine of the nine houses sent down a line of pails, and about four people in that village have said since that a pail line on a Sunday morning in a nine-house village is a thing that runs itself once four people have shown each other how, and that nobody organised it and nobody was in charge of it, and that on the sixth day of it the woman of about thirty sat down on the lip of the channel and did not get up for about an hour.

The third hour was spade work and the fourth hour was worse, because a spade into clay that has not been turned in a hundred days makes a noise that about nine people in that village have said they will hear again, and because the clay at four inches down came up in plates about the size of a hand and had to be broken with the back of the spade before it would go into the barrow at all.

The footboard went at the fourth hour of the afternoon and the man of about forty-three of the fourth household went into the hole with it.

He was the tallest of the nine houses and the oldest of about nine men in that village and he had been going down that clay on a spade for about seven hours with four of the others, and the last hour of it he was in the hole to the waist clearing the underside of the board with his hands because there was no room for a spade, and about nine people in that village have said since that a man in a hole in clay to the waist with a footboard coming down on him is the one picture this flood has made that anybody in four hundred miles will remember.

It held. The clay closed round his leg at about the knee and the board came down onto his back and he was in it for about an hour and four minutes, and about four people in that village have worked out since that the hour and four minutes was not the length of the work and that it was the length of the arguing, because four men had to agree about a barrow handle before any of them would put their weight on it.

They got him out with a barrow handle and about four men on the rope end of it, and the woman of about forty-six of that household was in the hole with him for the last twenty minutes of it with both hands on his collar, and about four people who have walked that village have said that the woman of that house put her hands on a man's collar in a hole in clay and did not once ask anybody for anything, and that nobody in that hole asked her for anything either, and that the two of them had about a hundred and forty days of that.

He came out with the clay to his chest and his left arm not doing what it was told and his right arm holding on to the barrow, and the fourth household's water went into the hole where he had been in it and did not come out, and the bed of the channel was silted to a foot in about nine places and the work of seven hours was not in the bed any more.

They got him up the lane on a door taken off its own hinges, and about four people in that village have said that a door is the only thing in a nine-house village that is both long enough and light enough, and that nobody thought of it and that about nine people thought of it at the same moment, and that the woman of about thirty at the stall was the one who thought of it and said nothing about having thought of it.

---

Nobody in that village had a word for what the loss was until the fourth hour of the afternoon, when a woman at the fourth house put a pail down outside her own door and made a noise with her mouth that was not a word, and the man of about thirty-six, who was standing in about four inches of clay at the time, said the thing out loud so that nobody else would have to be the one to say it.

"The water that was coming down this channel is in that hole. That is about nine hundred buckets and it is not coming out of it before the frost or after it, and about nine days of hands went down the hole with it, and the rack at the low end is a week older on Monday than it was yesterday morning."

The woman of about thirty at the stall wiped the clay off her hands onto her own skirt in four movements and did not look at the hole, and about nine people in that village have said since that a person cleaning their hands at the end of a thing that has gone wrong is doing a thing that can be done while they are thinking.

"I got nine houses to send down a pail line at about the eighth hour this morning and I did that on my own and I have not been asked to and I would like somebody to know it." She turned round and looked at the fourth house and at the door that was not on its own hinges any more. "**That is a thing I did. That is the last thing anybody is going to be able to do on this channel before the frost, and it took me a pot and a butt and about nine pails, and I am a woman with a stall and I am not going to be asked for the list of the nine houses because there is no list and I made the line myself.**"

"Nobody has asked you for anything."

"No, and about four people in that lane have worked out that the fastest way for a thing to happen in this village is for it to be nobody's business, and I have worked that out in about a hundred and forty days and I am not going to be the one to change it, and that is not modesty, that is a decision I made on a Tuesday and I have not broken it."

Nobody said anything to that for about as long as it takes to count nine things, and about four people in that village have said since that a loss which is said out loud by a stranger is heard differently from one that is said out loud by the house, and that the house had not said it, and that the house had been putting pails down outside its door since about the second hour of the afternoon without saying anything at all.

"Your man's arm is out."

"**His arm is out and he is forty-three and he will not have it back for nine days and I have had a hundred and forty days of hands in that clay and I am telling you what I have and not what I want.**" She had not come up out of the hole. She was sitting on the lip of it with her own hands in her lap and her sleeves rolled and about four feet of clay on her to the waist, and about nine people in that village have said since that nobody told her to come up and she did not come up. "We have this week's water in that hole. We have about four hundred buckets at the low end instead of about nine hundred. We have one rack a week old and one man who cannot lift, and the rack does not come off. And I can tell you exactly what our days are without you asking me, and I have been able to do that for about nine years, and nobody has ever asked me to and I have never once wanted to be asked."

"You can tell me what your days are."

"I can tell you what our days are, and I am not going to tell you, and the reason is not that you are a stranger. The reason is that the moment I say them out loud to a man in a good coat, they are a thing that has been said, and a thing that has been said can be written down, and a thing that has been written down about a household is a thing a clerk can put in a place where a person used to be." She got up out of the hole on her own and stood in it and then climbed the lip. "**You are the fourth person to come up this road since the water went out of the low ground and none of the three before you asked me for that, and one of the three wanted my gate.**"

The work stopped at about the fifth hour because the light had gone off the low ground and the clay was harder to see in than it was to be in. They put the barrow back on its wheels and turned it over at the top of the bed and about nine people in that village walked home up a road that has a gate on it and no bar on it, and the channel at the second village had about four hundred buckets of standing water at the low end and a hole in it with a man in it, and about four hundred miles of road between here and there, and a rack at the low end that a hundred and forty years ago somebody said does not come off, and nobody in four hundred miles has said it twice.
