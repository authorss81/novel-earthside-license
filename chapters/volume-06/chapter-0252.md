# Chapter 0252 — Read In A Room

The date on the door of the room over a market was in chalk and it was the right one, the ninety-seventh day of the Long Month, and it went on at about the eighth hour of the morning by a woman of about thirty-four who keeps a stall by a wharf, who is the person in this city who puts dates on doors, because a page that cannot be dated cannot be answered. The docket from nine hundred miles away was on the table in the same four lines as the day before, the case was shut under the table and had been shut for two nights, the board above the door of the store on the upper road has been unwritten for twenty-seven days, and the wind has been at that wall for twenty-three days.

---

The room held about nine people inside it and about two hundred and forty could hear out of the hall and the landing, and that hall has a ceiling that takes a voice and gives it back a half-beat late. About nine people in this city have worked out that a thing said in that room at the ninth hour is said to two hundred and forty and not to nine, and that it makes no difference, because the two hundred and forty are in the room.

Tamsin Quill was asked at about the ninth hour to read the four lines out loud in the local language, and she did not do it for about four seconds, and the four seconds were visible in the room.

"You have read it."

"I have read it twice, once on the step yesterday and once standing up, and I can read Crown Law, and I want one thing said in this room before I read it and I want it said by the person who sent me."

"Which is."

"**That a document nobody reads is a piece of paper. That is the whole of it and it is nine words, and I have not had anything this morning.**"

The hall gave the four sentences back to the room a half-beat late and the room waited for it, and about four people in that room have said since that a building that answers you half a beat late is a building that has decided to be heard.

"How long will the reading be."

"**About four minutes. And every one of the two hundred and forty people on that landing is going to hear all four lines, and I am telling you that now so that nobody in this room can say afterward that they did not know.**"

"Will you say it twice."

"No. Once for the words, and then once more for the four places, because the four places are what is going to be argued about, and if I do not separate them somebody will argue about the words for an hour instead."

She read it out exactly as it was on the docket, in the order of the docket, in the local language, without a note and without stopping, and she did not put any of it into anything else and she did not soften a word of it or explain a word of it. It went out into that hall in four sentences and came back a half-beat late, and there is no other page in this city that they can be checked against.

That an emergency is declared in the crossing and in the ground adjoining the crossing. That the days upon every gate between this city and the gates of the west road are to be entered in a schedule. That the schedule is to be made up and kept by a company. And that in every line of that schedule there are four places, and the four places are a date, a gate, a holder, and the days.

The room said nothing for a while. Then a woman at the back of the landing said a word that about nine people heard and about two hundred and forty did not, and the woman of about thirty-four who keeps a stall by a wharf looked at the landing and then turned back to the front, and about four people in this room have since said that she was the only person in it not looking at the man who had read it.

"You did not translate it."

"I read it in the local language, which is a translation, and I did not put it into anything else, and those are two different things, and about four people noticed the difference this morning and two of them have said so afterwards in a yard."

"What would you have put it into."

"**Into a thing I could answer. That is the whole of the difference and I have been on the other side of it for two hundred and fifty-two days.**"

The hall gave it back a half-beat late and about four people in that room counted the half-beat on their fingers without meaning to.

"That sounds like a rule."

"**It is the other side of a rule. A document in a shape you can answer is a description, and nobody in this room is going to get that out of me in the next ten minutes, and about four of them would like to.**"

"That is it."

"**That is it and I have read it. The second reading is the four places.**"

She looked at the second slate and not at the page while she said that, and a woman of about thirty-four who keeps a stall by a wharf wrote the four words on the back of her own hand and rubbed them out again.

"Say them."

About four people in that room had wanted the second reading to be the other three lines, and about nine wanted the four places, and neither of those two groups said what they wanted, and both of them stayed in their seats.

A man of about thirty-nine who trades on the Open Hand board wrote the four places out on his own hand in the margin of nothing and then put his hand flat over it, and about four people in that room saw him do it and none of them said a word.

"**There are four of them and they are in this order. A date. A gate. A holder. The days.**"

"Again."

"**No. I said them once, slowly, and I am not going to say them a third time this morning, because a thing said three times in a room is a thing being agreed to, and nothing in that docket has been agreed to by anybody in this city.**"

"Read them again."

The man of about thirty-nine who trades on the Open Hand board had not moved and had not taken his hand off the four places, and about four people in that room have said since that the two of them are the only two people in it who have heard the same thing twice and said nothing about either of the times.

"I am not reading them again. I said them once, slowly, and I have said that twice this morning and a third time is a thing being agreed to, and nothing in that docket has been agreed to by anybody in this city."

"You are not going to let anybody write them down."

"Anybody with a slate may write them down, and about four people in this room had one out before you asked. A check that stops people writing things down is not a check, it is a post, and I have refused three posts in this flood and I am not going to be the fourth by accident."

---

A man of forty-one in a very good coat was at the window of that room for most of the morning with his hand inside his coat about nine times, and about four people in that room wanted that docket to be a different kind of document. One of the four was him, and he was the only one who had been in the building where it was signed, and the other three have never said what kind they wanted it to be.

"Which building."

"Nobody in this room can say who sent it, and that is not a gap in the reading. It is signed. There is a clerk's name at the foot of it in a hand that is not the hand of anybody in this room, and the clerk works in an office nine hundred miles from this table, and the next quarter is not a date that anybody in this city holds."

A woman of about twenty-four on the second step of the stair wrote the four places out on the back of a sheet and put the sheet in her apron, and about four people in that hall have said since that a person who puts a page away in a room like that has decided the room is not the place for it.

"Then it is not from anybody."

"It is from an office. That is the whole of what anybody in this room can stand behind this morning, and I have been in that office, and I am not going to fill in the rest of the sentence in a room where it will be written down."

A woman of about twenty-four on the second step of the stair wrote nothing at that and about four people in that room have said since that she wrote nothing at that on purpose.

"Four words would do it."

"I have read it, and I find it correct." He said it without turning round from the window. About four people in that room looked at the back of a good coat and did not ask him anything else. He was in that room until about the eleventh hour and after that he was in the hall, and what he did in the hall is entered and not described.

"What is the first line."

"The first line is the crossing and the ground adjoining it, and an emergency declared in both, and the crossing is not this room and the ground adjoining it is not a street you can point at from a window, and that will not be got past today."

A woman of about thirty-four who keeps a stall by a wharf said out loud that a ground you cannot point at from a window is a ground about nine hundred people are going to be told somebody else is standing on, and the man of forty-one in a very good coat did not answer her, and about four people in that room have said since that that was the first sentence of the day with a person in it, and that it was hers.

"The second line."

"The second line is the days. Every gate between this city and the gates of the west road, and the days on it to be entered in a schedule, and that is the line the other three are for, and it is the only line of the four that anybody in this room can put a hand on. About nine people on that landing have just heard it said out loud for the first time this morning and about two hundred and forty have not, and it is entered that I read it in the local language and did not translate it."

"The third line is a company."

"The third line is a company to keep the road while the schedule is made up, and the word in front of company is the one I would not put on a wall, and nobody in this room has written it on anything, and about nine people in this city have already said it out loud in a yard and not one of them is in this room."

The light in that room went off the flats and up the far wall of it while she was standing there and nobody moved out of it.

"What is the word."

"**Two of them, in front of the other one. And a company that keeps a place is a thing this city has had for longer than anybody in it can remember.**"

"And the other one."

"**A company that holds a place pending a schedule is a thing I have read about once, and it was nine hundred miles from here.**"

The light in that room moved off her and onto the wall behind her, and about four people in that hall have said since that they watched it happen and that they could not afterwards have said what had changed.

He turned round at that and looked at the room for the first time since the ninth hour, and about four people in that room found that they had been looking at the back of a good coat for two hours and had not noticed.

"You have read about it."

"I have read about the shape of it. I have never seen one and I am not going to describe one to two hundred and forty people at the ninth hour of the morning as though I had."

---

At the other end of that hall there is a room with a door of its own, and in it the woman of about twenty-nine who keeps a public register entered the four places on a second slate in the order they were on the page, and about nine people were in that room and about two hundred and forty more could hear out of it. The two rooms had been in the same building since the eighth hour of the morning and were not going to have anything to do with each other.

Nobody gave her the docket and nobody carried it to her. What she had at the ninth hour was a woman of about twenty-four at her door with four short lines on a slate in another person's hand, a note of the seal, and the place at the bottom. She wrote them onto her own second slate in the same order, and about four people in that room have said that she did not ask for the docket once.

"You could have gone and got it."

"**I could have gone and got it, and if I had, then there would be one of me in two rooms today.**"

"That is not a virtue."

The man of about thirty-nine came back in and sat down in the same place and put his hands on his knees, and nobody asked him where he had been, and about four people in that room have said that a man who goes out of a room and comes back into it is a man who has been in the room the whole time.

"**It is not a virtue. It is a job. I have been one person in one room for two hundred and fifty-two days, and the job is the account of a thing, and the account is not the thing.**"

"You have not heard it read out."

"I have heard the four places read out. The four places came to me at the ninth hour in another person's hand in the order they were on the page, and the reading of the words is not the account of the reading of the words, it is the account of the page."

"Then who is the account of the page."

About four people in that room had wanted the four places and about nine had wanted the other three lines, and neither of the two said what it wanted.

"**Whoever is the account of it, in a room, in daylight, with a date on the door. There is one of those in this building at this hour and she is in the other room and I can hear her through the wall.**"

"You could go and stand in it."

"**I am not going to go and stand in that room and be a second pair of ears in front of two hundred and forty people, and about four people in this city have worked out that I would not and have not asked me why.**"

Four people compared the two slates in the hallway at about the fourth hour of the afternoon: the register-keeper and two of her own room, and a woman of about twenty-four from the other room who had been in there for about a quarter of an hour. The columns agreed. The register-keeper then wrote the two words *holding company* at the foot of hers, because that phrase is in the second reading and not in the first, and about four people in that hallway have said since that the whole day was in the second reading, and about nine have said nothing about either.

The man of about thirty-nine who trades on the Open Hand board carried them, one in each hand, in front of about two hundred and forty people, and his whole work in that building for eight hours was to carry two pieces of slate from one room to another and not to look at either of them on the way. It is entered that he did not look at either slate and that nobody in this city believes him.

Then Adrian Vale wrote a sentence on a slate at about the fifth hour of the afternoon and did not read it out, and he held it down on the table while the woman of about thirty-four who keeps a stall by a wharf read it upside down and then turned it round, and then let it be taken.

"That is yours."

"It is mine and I am not going to say it, because the person who is the account of this document is at the other end of this hall with a door of her own, and a thing written in a room in daylight gets read by somebody."

"You wrote it this afternoon."

"I wrote it this afternoon and I have been carrying the same sentence in my pocket since the second month of this flood without saying it, and the reason I have not said it is that every time I have said it somebody has had to hold it up afterwards, and there are about nine of them and they are all tired."

The slate went to the other end of the hall with the man of about thirty-nine, who carried it the way he had carried the other two. It was nine words long and it was in Adrian's own hand, and it said that a page with a person's name on it is a description, and a description is a removal with a column for where the person is. The woman of about twenty-four went with it and came back and said that the register-keeper had read it out in that room, that she had said a thing afterwards she was not going to repeat, and that about four people in that room had not wanted it said at all.

---

The finding of the day is not a sentence anybody said out loud. Two slates had to be carried between two rooms to be compared, because the person who reads a Crown instrument out loud and the person who enters what has been read are two people with two trades, and this city has never once put them in one room, and nobody in either room thought that was remarkable. One of the two women would have called it remarkable and did not say so, and she was in the room with the door of its own.
