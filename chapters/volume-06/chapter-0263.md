# Chapter 0263 — The Man Who Opens It

The light on that road was flat white off low ground on both sides of it and the cold had got into the iron by the fourth hour of the morning, and the wind has been at the wall of the store on the upper road for thirty-four days, and what was under a cart driver's two hands and under a gatekeeper's two hands and under about four other pairs of hands on that road that day was oak shod with iron, a chain over a pulley, cold mud and a chock. The board over the door of the store on the upper road has not been written on since the day after the sixty-ninth day of the Long Month and the figures on it belong to that sixty-ninth day and are thirty-nine days old, and it has stood unwritten thirty-eight. A case from nine hundred miles away has been shut on a table in a room over a market since the first day of the Short Month, and that is twelve days of it, and a loaded cart went up that road at about the fourth hour of the morning and came back down it at about the seventh hour of the evening, and the day was a Friday.

---

The first gate on that road is a bar of oak with an iron shoe and a chain over a pulley above it, and on the far side of it at about the fourth hour of the morning there was a man of about fifty-five who unhooked the chain himself, in the cold, with a glove off, and let the arm down onto two stones, and the man of about forty who carries sacks came through behind the cart with his own hand flat on the side of the load the whole way up.

"That arm weighs about what a man weighs."

"**It weighs what a man weighs because a man is what it is for. That is not a joke and I have never made a joke about it and I am not starting on a morning like this.**" He crouched and got both hands under the far end of it and moved it two inches clear of the stone, and the stone had a groove worn in the top of it and the groove was the shape of the shoe, and the driver got down off the shafts to look at the groove and did not ask about it.

The near wheel of the cart was four inches off the ground by then and the cart was leaning a little to the left, and the man of about forty who carries sacks had his back to both of them and was looking at the load.

"The arm is the easy part of a gate. The trouble is that a gate on this road is open for about a minute and a half a day and shut for the rest of it, and anybody who wants it open can have it by asking me, and about nine people a week ask me, and I have never charged one of them."

"You open it for anybody."

"I open it for anybody who has a wheel. That is the whole of the rule and I made it and it is not written down." He came round to the near side and put a chock under the leading wheel himself and did not ask the driver to do it, and about four people on that road have said since that a gatekeeper who puts his own chock in is a gatekeeper who has decided that a cart is a thing that happens to him.

"Can I ask you something while it is open."

The arm came the rest of the way down onto the stones while he was saying it and the chain swung against the shoe of it and stopped, and the man of about forty who carries sacks had already gone round the far side of the cart to see whether the gate would clear the load, and about nine people on that road have said since that two men talking across a gate and a third man checking a clearance is the shape of about nine hours of every week on that road.

"You can ask me anything while it is open, because in about a minute it will be shut and then you will be standing on the wrong side of a thing you have not asked about." He straightened up and put the glove back on and then took it off again because the chain was still in his hand. "**Ask it quick and ask it plain, because I have a chain to put back and the hook is the only part of this gate anybody in this city has ever asked me about.**"

"Who keeps this gate."

The man of about forty who carries sacks had come back round the near side of the cart by then and was standing at the post with a hand on the wheel of it, and the man of fifty-five looked at him and then at the man who had asked, and about four people on that road have said since that a man who is about to answer a question about ownership checks first who else is standing where the answer can be heard.

"I keep this gate. That is the first half of it and you have the second half in about four seconds if you are any good at it, and if you are not any good at it then you have come out here on a Friday morning for nothing and I am sorry for your morning." He did not say it unkindly and he did not say it twice. "**It is mine because I open it. That is the whole of the reason and nobody has ever handed me a better one and I have never asked for one, and if I was handed one I should think a very great deal about what I did with it before I used it.**"

The cart came through and the arm went back onto its stones while the wheels were still on the near side of it, and the man of fifty-five put a hand flat on the top of the arm until the last wheel was past, and the arm did not move, and about nine people on that road have said since that a man who holds a gate arm down with one hand while a loaded cart goes under it has worked out about nine things about that gate that nobody has ever asked him about.

The road climbs for about four hundred paces from that gate and then comes down again onto flat low ground, and the second of those things is where the trouble on that Friday was. The near wheel of the cart went over a stone that is not a stone and is a frozen rut, and came off the nave with a sound that about nine people on that road have described in about nine different ways, and lay down in the mud with the axle standing out of it and the load leaning.

The man of about forty who carries sacks was under the cart before the load had finished leaning. The man of fifty-five came from behind and got both arms round the axle end and lifted, and the load stopped leaning and stood there leaning at a different angle, and neither of the two men said anything at all for about as long as it takes to get a hand wet.

"The pin."

"The pin is out. It came out with the nave and it is in the mud and I have not found it and I am not going to kneel in this for it now." The man of fifty-five turned his head without taking his arms off the axle. "**There is a mallet in that cart and a spare pin in the driver's coat and the pin is the width of my thumb and if it is bent it is bent and then the wheel is not going round that gate again this month and somebody nine hundred miles away is going to hear about it.**"

They got the wheel up onto the nave and the man of about forty held it while the man of fifty-five took the pin out of the driver's coat and put it in with the heel of his hand and got it about four inches in and stopped.

"It is bent."

"It is bent. It went into the mud at an angle and it is bent about a finger's width and I have just felt it." He took his hand off the mallet. "I am not going to hit it any harder. I have been in this flood for about a hundred days and I have watched about four things get broken by a man who hit them harder than they needed, and one of them was a person's hand and I have not seen the end of that one."

The wheel was got round. They put the cart's own weight back on it and the load came down off the leaning angle and settled, and the two of them stood on either side of it in mud to the knee with their hands hanging, and about nine people who passed that way at the fourth hour of the afternoon have said since that two men standing either side of a cart in mud with their hands hanging is what this road looks like, and that nobody on that road has a word for the two hours it takes.

---

A quarter of a mile on there is a rise in that road about four feet high that a loaded cart does not want, and the whole of the day on that rise was four men, two of them under the axle with their shoulders in the mud and two of them at the shafts, and the man of fifty-five was not asked to help with it and helped with it, and about four people who passed at the second hour of the afternoon have said since that a man who has a gate and a chain and a hook and no wages had better be about nine feet from a stalled cart at that hour.

"You are not paid for this."

"**Nobody is paid for any of it. You are paid for the salt and the salt is in Aurel and the salt is not in this flood. That is the whole of the pay on that road and it has been the whole of it since before you were born.**" He took his shoulder out from under the axle and stood up and looked at the cart and not at the men. "I will tell you what I tell the carter and the boy and nobody else. A man with a wheel comes to me and asks can I get through, and I say yes, and he says thank you, and that is the whole of the transaction and it has happened about nine times a week since the first month of this flood. **Nobody has ever asked me anything else, and there is a page in a room over a market that wants the days on this gate, and it has got a place in it for a name, and I am the name, and I have not been asked and I am not going to volunteer.**"

"You have read about it."

He had the chain up in both hands by then and had not started it over the pulley, and the cart was about four hundred paces off with a bad wheel behind it, and about four people who passed that stretch of road have said since that a man standing at a gate with a chain in his hands and a question he has not answered yet is the commonest sight on that road and that nobody in four hundred miles has ever described it.

"I have not read about it. A carter told me there was a page that wanted the days on the gates, and he told me on the first Friday he came this way, and he said it in about four words, and then we both shut up and did the wheel." He put his glove back on. "You are the first person who has come out here and asked me anything at all in a hundred days and I have given you the answer in about nine sentences, and you may do what you like with it, and I would ask you not to do one thing with it."

"What thing."

"**Do not come back with it in a book.**"

Half a mile further on there is a house with a gate that is not barred at all and a woman hanging washing on a line that runs from the gatepost to a nail in the wall, and the cart went through that one with nobody in it doing anything whatever, and the woman did not stop hanging washing.

"That one is not kept."

"That one is not kept, and there is nobody to keep it, and the house is empty from the second hour to the seventh while the two who are in it are at the salt, and about four people on this road have worked out that a gate nobody keeps is cheaper than a gate somebody keeps, and that the price of it is that anything at all goes through it." He looked at the line and at the wet cloth on it. "Anything came through in the night once. At my gate. And that is why I have got a chain, and I have never told that story to anybody except you, and I am not going to tell it to anybody in a market."

---

The cart came back down that road at about the seventh hour of the evening with about half a load left on it, and the arm went back onto its two stones and the chain went over the pulley and onto the hook, and the man of fifty-five pulled the chain once to test it and then pulled it a second time, and about four people who were on that road at that hour have said since that he pulled it twice and that the second pull was not a test and that nobody has worked out what it was.

The man of about forty who carries sacks took the chock out of his own hand and handed it to the gatekeeper before he went past, and the gatekeeper took it and set it on the stone under the arm where it had not been before, and about nine people on that road have said since that a man who hands a thing back to the man who owns the gate has done the only thing anybody on that road did today that anybody in four hundred miles is going to hear about.

The gate was as it had been. It had been as it had been because about nine pairs of hands had put it that way and not one of them had been paid and not one of them had been asked.

About four people in this city have worked out since then that the third place on that docket, which is a holder, has a candidate on the first gate of a road out past the last named house, and that the candidate is a man of about fifty-five who believes the gate is his because he opens it, and that nobody in this city is going to put his name in a column and nobody has been asked to. **About nine people have worked out that a refusal to name him is a refusal that leaves the Crown free to send somebody who will name him, and that a page nine hundred miles away does not need this city to agree with it.**

Nobody has written his name down. The boy of thirteen who was on that road with a hinge and a length of chain and a bar of tallow was not on that road on Friday, and the man of about thirty-four who writes a ninth line on a wall for a purpose has not been out of this city for nine days, and about four people have said since that the only person in four hundred miles who could have written it down was not on the road and did not know it existed.
