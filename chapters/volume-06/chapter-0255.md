# Chapter 0255 — The Hundredth Day

The hundredth day of the Long Month was a Thursday and the wind came down the street that runs back from the market in a way it has not come down since before the seventh week of this flood, and the light was the hard white that comes off a hard sky, and the cold was on the forearms where the sleeves had gone back, and what was under the hands of the woman of about fifty-six who keeps a shop four streets down from that market for the whole of that day was nine measures of salt that had gone hard. The wind has been at the wall of the store on the upper road for twenty-six days. The board above that door carries the figures of the sixty-ninth day of the Long Month, which are thirty-one days old, and has not been written on since the day after them.

---

The nine measures were under the counter in the front of that shop and had been there since the thirty-ninth day of the Long Month, and in that time they had gone from something a person could put a hand into to nine separate things, and that is the whole difficulty with them. A measure of salt you can pour is a sack and a half of nothing much. A measure of salt that has gone hard is a block, and a block does not have a part of it that a shopkeeper can call a part.

She got the first one out at about the seventh hour on her knees with a flat trowel, because a hard measure has to come out of a corner as a corner and not as a heap. It came out whole. She stood it on end against the front of the counter and looked at the mark it left in the dust, and there was a mark, and the mark was the shape of a measure, and there are nine of those marks in that shop now in a line under the counter, and nobody has counted them but her and about four people in this city have worked out that a mark in a shop's dust is a record of a habit and nobody has worked out which measure the record is of.

"You are not to break them."

"Nobody has told me I may break them. Nobody has told me I may not either, and I have gone two hundred and fifty-five days in this flood without being told I may do a great many things, and I have worked out that the safest way to live is to do the ordinary day's work and to stop where the ordinary day's work stops."

"They are under your counter."

"They are under my counter and the damp is coming up the wall on that side. It came up that wall on the thirty-eighth day of that month and it has not stopped, and salt that takes damp is salt that has got about four weeks in it, and a shop that has got four weeks in its salt is a shop for about a month and then is not a shop."

The light in that shop came off the street flat and went over the top of the counter and off the front of her forearms, and for as long as it takes to boil a pail nobody in that shop moved and the second measure was where it was.

"How long is a month."

"**A hundred days. It has been a hundred days since the first day of it and it is a hundred days long and that is not a thing anybody ever had to say out loud before this week.**"

The man of about thirty-nine who trades on the Open Hand board was at the back door of that shop at about the eighth hour and had come in out of the wind and had not taken his coat off. He stood where he could see the whole of the back room and the whole of the front and he did not come further in than that, and he did not offer to carry any of the nine.

"I could shift those for you."

"You could, and you have not, and I want to say thank you for not saying it again, because a man says it twice and then it is on him."

"I did not say it twice."

"You said it once and you have not said it again and that is twice in a way, and I am not going to argue about it with a man in my back door. I know why you are not."

The woman of about twenty-four turned her slate over on the boards of that back room, wrote the date on the back of it, and left it face down, and about four people in that shop saw her do it and none of them said a word about leaving it face down.

"Do you."

"**You are going up that road to a store tomorrow for a queue, and a queue on a Tuesday does not wait for a man who is two hours late with nine measures of salt.**"

"That is the reason."

"**That is the reason and it is a good one. I have traded with you for about two hundred days and I have never once seen you late for anything, and I would rather say the reason than say thank you, and I am aware that those are not the same and I have thought about it.**"

The wind in that street had gone round the corner by about the ninth hour and come back with a different edge on it, and about nine people in that shop have said since that they had stopped noticing which way it came from and had started noticing when it stopped.

"I would have said that."

"I know you would have, and that is the second reason, and I am not going to thank you for either of them, because a man who is thanked for a thing he was going to do anyway is a man who has been thanked for nothing."

"You would be thanking a man for being honest."

"I would be thanking a man for being honest at the seventh hour of the morning while nine measures of salt are on my floor, and there is not one thing you could have done this morning that would have cost you more, and that is why I am not going to do it."

---

She carried the nine measures up onto the top of the counter in two goes and down into the back room in one, and she carried them the whole way with her arms and not on a board, because a board would have been a thing she could have set a measure down on and come back for, and she had decided at about the eighth hour that she was not going to do that nine times. Her forearms went white with the salt and then the skin of them went tight, and at about the tenth hour she could not close her right hand all the way round a trowel and she worked the rest of it out of the corner with two fingers, and the man of about thirty-nine stood in the back door and watched all of it and did not say a word.

About four people who came into that shop between the ninth hour and the eleventh hour watched it, and none of them offered, and one of them went out and came back in with a board and put it down just inside the door and left it there. She used it for the last two and it is still in that shop.

"That is a board."

"**That is a board and it is not yours and I have not asked whose it is and I am not going to. I have used it twice.**"

"It was on a wall somewhere."

"**It was on a wall somewhere, and if it was on a wall for a reason then it has been on my floor for a quarter of an hour for a reason, and I do not think the two reasons are the same one, and I would like to be wrong about that.**"

The man of about thirty-nine in the back door moved a boot about a foot to the left so that he could see past her, and then moved it back again, and neither time did he say anything.

"There is salt on the ninth of the flats in a yard nine miles up that road and nobody has been near it since the day it went down, and there is a coat over a pan in the same yard, and there is salt in this shop, and I have been trying for about two days to work out how many sets of salt there are in this city and I cannot, and I have stopped trying, and I have never said that out loud to anybody."

"I have not either. There are three that I know of and I have not compared them and neither have you and neither has anybody, and I am a shopkeeper and you are a man who writes on a board and between us we could have compared three sets of salt in about four months and we have not, and I would like that entered too."

"Nobody enters that."

"Nobody enters anything on a Thursday about salt. That is what this shop is."

She broke none of the nine and she opened none of the nine, and she did not put a thumb into any of them to see whether the middle had gone soft, which she wanted to do about nine times and did not do, because a thumb in salt is the beginning of an opening and nobody in this city has told her she may begin one.

---

At about the fourth hour of the afternoon she took out the day-book of that shop and put it on the counter and put the chalk in her hand and wrote nothing at all. The last written page in that day-book has a date at the head of it and it is the ninety-fourth day of the Long Month, which is six days ago, and the one before that is the ninety-third, and there are eleven written pages and none of them is blank. She put the chalk down about a foot to the left of the top of the page and left it there for about a quarter of an hour.

"The hundredth day of the Long Month is the date."

"The hundredth day of the Long Month is the hundredth day of it. I have written that at the head of about a hundred pages in this book and it has been true every time, and tomorrow it is a hundred and one, and there is no hundred and one of anything in this world. There is no hundred and first day. I have been writing dates in this book since the first day of the Long Month and I have run out of month."

"You could write the flood."

"I could write the flood, and then a person reading that page in about four months would not know which of the four hundred days of it it was, and I have not been doing that since the fortieth day of this flood and I am not going to start on a Thursday with nine measures of salt on the floor."

Somebody put their head in at the door of that shop and said a thing to the person beside them and neither of them came in, and the woman of about fifty-six did not stop with the chalk in her hand.

"Then what goes at the head of it."

"**Nothing goes at the head of it.**"

"That is a page with nothing on it."

"**That is a page with nothing at the head of it, and a page like that is a page a clerk nine hundred miles away can put anything at the head of, and there is a docket on a table in a room over a market in a form nobody in this city writes.**"

A chair went about four inches along that shop floor and the woman of about fifty-six did not ask who had moved it, and about four people in that shop have said since that she asks about everything else before breakfast.

"Does it match."

"**It does not match, and it is the only page in four hundred miles a clerk could put beside one of mine, and the reason it does not match is a month.**"

---

She carried the day-book to the market at about the fourth hour of the afternoon with the chalk still in her hand and set it on a stall and said three words to a woman of about twenty-four who was there, and the three words were these: there is no form.

"There is no form."

"There is no form for tomorrow. I have written a date at the head of a page in that book every day for a hundred days and I cannot write one for tomorrow, and I did not find that out by thinking about it. I found it out by putting nine measures of salt on a floor and then trying to write down what I had done, and about four people in my shop watched me not write anything and not one of them knew why until I said it."

"Who can date a page in this city."

"A notary can. There is one and she is fifty and she has been a notary for twenty-six years, and she named the month this one on its first day in a room with a date on the door, and she did it out loud in front of about nine people."

The day-book on that stall was turned round so that the blank at the head of the page faced out, and about four people in that market have said since that they have never known what to do with a page somebody has turned round on purpose.

"And she will name another one."

"She will do it in a room and not in a market, and she will say the cost out loud first, because that is what she did the last time and I am not going to argue with a woman about a month in a market on a Thursday. I have been a shopkeeper for thirty-one years and I have never once been able to argue about a month."

Then the woman of about twenty-four said a figure out loud, to the stall and not to her.

"Between this city and the gates of the west road there are ninety-four gates."

"You have counted them."

"I have not counted them. I have got ninety-four out of about nine people, and four of them have been up that road, and nobody in four hundred miles has ever counted them, and this market cannot check it, and I am saying it out loud anyway because about nine people in this city have been guessing at it in a yard for two days and I would rather one figure were on a slate than nine guesses were in mouths."

"Write it down."

Ninety-four went onto about four hands and the chalk went back into the front of the shopkeeper's coat without anybody being asked to do anything with it, and about four people in that market have said that they have each watched a figure go onto a hand that week and that not one of them has looked at it since.

"About four people have already written it down, which is what I said I wanted and I have got it inside a minute, and I would like it entered that I said I wanted it and got it inside a minute, because a number wanted in a market and a number given in a market are not the same thing and nobody in this market knows which of the two they have just watched."

Nobody checked it. About four people in that market said afterwards that a number said out loud is worth more than no number at all, and about nine said that it is the same as no number at all, and both of those are correct, and it is the first time in two hundred and fifty-five days that both of them have been said in the same market on the same afternoon.

---

And the hundredth day of a month ended without a date on the head of a page, and a day-book four streets down from a market has a chalk lying a foot to the left of a blank page, and about four people in this city have written ninety-four down and nobody in four hundred miles can say whether it is right, and a notary of fifty was told about it in that market before the sixth hour and said that she would do it in a room and not in a market, and tomorrow is the hundred and first day and there is nothing to write it in.
