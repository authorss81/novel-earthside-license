# Chapter 0383 — One Question In The Open, And A Number That Goes On

The boards of a salt wharf in this city are the only flat thing for about four streets and on the sixty-eighth day of the Bare Month, a Saturday, the light comes up off the river on them in one long sheet and does not move all morning, and there is nothing high enough anywhere near that wharf to lay a shadow across it, so that the light never goes off anything until the sixth hour, and what was under about four pairs of hands that morning was a tally-board, a knife, a board of nineteen rates with the wet coming off one end of it, a piece of chalk in the inside breast pocket of the man who keeps the first stall, and about four hundred and forty.

---

The stack at the top end of those boards is higher than a man and has been since before the water, and there is a strip of clear board about as long as a cart at the bottom of it, and a man with a tally-board has been cutting salt up there every morning of this flood and putting it onto a pile that cannot be got off that wharf by anybody. Nothing has come off those boards in a cart since before the water. He has not said so. Nobody has asked him.

The man of about thirty-four who keeps the first stall came down onto the bottom of those boards at about the seventh hour because a Saturday is a day the boards at the foot of a market are shut, and his right hand took again in the night and he opened it and shut it about nine times to find out, and he did not tell anybody that he had done that, and a man with a piece of chalk he does not use went and stood at the bottom of a wharf and did not say that his hand had been out of order the whole of the previous afternoon.

The man of about thirty-eight worked. The rain had gone in the night and left the boards with a shine on them that was worse than the rain, and a man who has worked the same boards for nine years works a wet one slower and does not say so.

The man of about thirty-nine came along the boards at about the ninth hour with his board of nineteen rates under his arm and the water coming off the river at him, and he stopped.

---

He had not stopped at these boards before. He had walked the length of them about five weeks ago and said two sentences and gone on, and the two sentences had been about a second man, and nobody in this city has sent for a second man on a wharf in this flood.

"What do you put down."

The man of about thirty-eight did not stop working. A man who works boards has a tally-board under his arm and a knife in his hand and a pile at the top end that cannot be got off them, and a man who comes and asks him a question at the ninth hour on a Saturday gets the answer at the pace of the work and not at the pace of the asking.

"**About four hundred and forty.**"

The number had been said out loud in this city about nine times over the last month, every one of them of them at the head of a stall on the boards at the foot of a market, and on this morning it was said once, on the boards, in a man's own mouth, by the man it belongs to. Everybody standing within earshot of that wharf had heard it before.

The man of about thirty-nine did not say anything for about as long as it takes to fill a pail. He had come down a hundred steps of river path with a board of nineteen rates under his arm to ask a man who works boards one question, and he had got a number, and the number was the same number he could have said himself in about a second, and he had asked it so that the man would have to be the one who said it.

"You are going to ask me whether that is the one that goes out."

"**I was going to ask you whether that is the one that goes out, and I have known that I was going to ask it since the eighth hour.**"

He had come down a hundred steps of river path for this and had got two questions' worth of answering in about a minute, and a man who has been in about nine rooms in a fortnight without being asked one question in any of them does not stop asking on a Saturday.

"**And I asked you for the number before I asked you that, and a man who has not been asked anything is not a man who has stopped wanting to be.**"

The man of about thirty-eight set his knife down flat on the boards, which he had not done in about nine years, and wiped his hands, and the light off the water came up past both of them and did not move.

"**That one goes on. And I am going to say the other half of that before you ask me for it, and then neither of us has to ask the other one anything for the rest of the morning. There is another number.**"

He stopped there, with his hands wet and the knife lying flat on the boards, and the man of about thirty-nine waited for the rest of it and did not get it.

"Then the other one."

"**It is not that one. It has never been on a page in this city and it is not going to be on one this morning, and I have never told anybody which of the two of them I believe, and I am not going to tell you either.**"

"Why not."

The knife was still lying flat on the boards where he had put it down, and the shine the rain had left on them was going off in the light, and a man who has put a number in a column for nine years was about to say the reason out loud and had already decided to say the whole of it.

"**Because I have not worked it out, and a man who has not worked it out should not be asked to say which of two numbers he would send, and there is nobody standing on this wharf who is going to make me say it, and that is the whole of the reason.**"

The man of about thirty-nine took his hand off his own board. He had got a number and a refusal in the same breath, and a man who has come down a hundred steps of river path for one answer has not got used to being given two.

"**And I would like it noticed that I said all of that out loud on a wharf and not in a room, because a thing said in a room stays in the room and a thing said on a wharf goes up the river.**"

Nobody improved on it. A man who has been putting a number in a column four times a year since the winter before the water has told another man on a wet Saturday that there are two of them and which one of the two goes out, and has said the sentence before he was asked for it, and a man with a board of nineteen rates under his arm has heard it and has not asked which one he believes. It is the first time in nine years that this man has said out loud that he has a number he is not sending, and the man he said it to is the only person in four hundred miles who would have known what to ask next and is not going to be asked it by anybody.

---

The man of about thirty-nine put his board down flat on the wharf boards, and did not sit down on it, and left it there.

"You have given me the one I had."

"**I have given you the one everybody in this city has. There are about nine people in this city who have it and none of them have it from me and all of them have it from the same three stalls, and I have been saying it at a stall for about a month and this is the first time I have said it on the boards.**"

"Then it was not worth the walk."

A hundred steps of river path is a long way to walk with a board under your arm, and the man who had walked it stood on the boards and waited, and the salt went on going up the pile at the top end of them behind him.

"**It was not worth the walk, and the reason I have said it on the boards is that you came down here and asked, and a man who has been asked for nine years by nobody deserves to be asked by somebody once.**"

He picked his knife up off the boards and went on working. The stack at the top end of them is higher than a man and there is a strip of clear board about as long as a cart at the bottom of it, and the people on eleven miles of flats make what goes up that stack, and it cannot leave this city, and the man who has been cutting it up there every morning of this flood has just told a man on a Saturday that there is a second number and has not said which one is true.

The man of about thirty-four stood at the bottom end of those boards for about half of that with his right hand open and shut in his coat pocket and his left one on the rail, and said nothing at all, and he had a piece of chalk in that pocket and had had one there since the forty-third day of the Short Month, and a man with two numbers who has just said out loud that he keeps both of them has said it about nine feet off a man with one, and neither of them knows what the other's is for.

The man of about thirty-nine took his board off the wharf boards at about the fourth hour and went back up the path with the water coming off the river at him, and he had asked one question at a stall in a month and had got a number and a sentence he did not ask for. A man who has been in about nine rooms in a fortnight without being asked anything has finally asked something in the open, where anybody standing on a river path could have heard the answer, and the man he asked has been waiting nine years to be asked by somebody and got it on the worst morning of the week. Neither of those two facts is on a page and neither of them is going to be.
