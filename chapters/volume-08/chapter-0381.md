# Chapter 0381 — Going Up Nine Steps First, And A Page In A Coat Nobody Asks About

A room over a market had the sixty-sixth day of the Bare Month in chalk on its door at about the seventh hour, a Thursday, and the light came in over a step at the back with no name on it and stopped about nine inches past the near end of the bench and did not come any further, and the cold was in the crease of a form that had been folded and unfolded more times than that, and what was under about four pairs of hands that morning was a form, a slate, a lump of chalk, a second slate on a shelf behind the table with a date on it and nothing under it, a book open face down, and a man at the far end of the bench reading a page that was not his.

---

There were about nine people in it by the eighth hour and nobody was working. The form had been on that table through four rooms' worth of mornings and had three of its four places in a condition and one of them in a decision, and on this Thursday the fourth one was still where it had been put on the day a man said out loud in this room that he had never once filled it in.

Adrian Vale had the form turned round so that it faced him and it was about nine feet off the table's middle where the woman of about twenty-nine who keeps a public register had put it, and he read it from the top down the way a man reads a thing that is not going to be lent. He had been in that room in the mornings for about a fortnight. He had a sheet folded into four in the inside of his coat with a date on the back of it and two names on the front of it and the coat was buttoned over that, and a page of his own in a coat is the one kind of page in this city that nobody can be shown.

Nobody asked him anything. There is no form in four hundred miles with a place for a man who is not asked anything, and the not-asking is the arrangement and is not a courtesy.

The man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall was at the near end of the bench with his hand flat on his own inside breast pocket, and the man of about thirty-nine was against the wall at the end of that bench nearest the step, with his own slate flat on his knee and his right hand over the face of it, and neither of them had asked the man at the far end of the bench one question in about a fortnight and neither of them was going to.

---

The woman of about twenty-nine who keeps a public register had the book face down in front of her with her forearm across it, and she was looking at the far end of the bench and not at the book, and a woman who has kept a register for nine years has a great many days in that book and almost none of them are days anybody has ever asked her about.

"**I am not going to ask you what is in the inside of your coat, and I have known there was a page in it since the first day of that month, and I would like it said out loud in a room that I have been not asking you for about a fortnight and that the not asking has cost me the same as asking would have.**"

She said it to the bench and not to him, and then she turned her forearm off the book and did not open it.

The man of about thirty-four said nothing. A man who has been at the near end of that bench for about a fortnight without a question in any of it heard a woman explain out loud, in front of about nine people, why she was not going to ask a man one thing, and the reason she gave was a price and not an excuse, and he did not look round, because a man who is right about pages does not turn round at the sound of another person being careful.

"I have not asked because a page in a coat is the only page in this city that cannot be asked for, and if I ask you for it then it stops being that, and then there is nothing in four hundred miles that a person could put a name to and not be able to produce."

The room did not make a sound. About four people in that room worked out between the seventh hour and the ninth that the woman who writes the notices in this city, the woman who has put six sheets on walls with nothing at the top of any of them, had just said out loud that she was protecting a page she had never seen by refusing to ask about it, and that nobody in the room was going to be the one to say thank you for it, and that she had not asked for that either.

She put her hand flat on the back of the book and left it there.

"You may sit there all morning. I would like it noticed that I have never once asked a man in this room what he is reading, and that today I have told about nine people that I know what is in it and not what it says, and that those are two different things and I have kept them apart for about a fortnight on purpose."

---

Then the man of about thirty-four turned round on the bench, which he had not done in about a fortnight, and he said it to the man at the wall and not to anybody else, and the two of them had never once asked each other a question about a page in a room in daylight in this flood.

"**On the day they come up those steps, you go before me.**"

The man of about thirty-nine took his hand off the face of his slate, which he had not done for a fortnight either, and looked at him.

"I am not going to be the first one up them."

"**I know you are not going to be the first one up them, and I am asking you to be, and the reason I am asking is the reason you have not been in eight rooms without a question in any of them, and I would like it said out loud that I have worked that out on my own and that you never told me.**"

He put his hand back on the slate. The man of about thirty-four sat back against the bench and did not say anything else about it for the rest of the morning, and neither did the other one, and the two of them had gone from a room in which neither of them had ever asked the other one anything to a room in which one of them had, and neither of the two things that happened had anything to do with a page.

The man of about thirty-four said the reason out loud before he was asked for it, which is a thing a man who keeps a stall does and has done about nine times in this flood, and this time the condition was that nobody was to say anything about it afterwards, and the room agreed to it without a word being said about the agreeing.

---

The woman of about twenty-one was at the bench with her hands in her apron and the pen with the nib ground narrow and cut down with a knife in the apron with them. She had been asked one question in this flood, once, in this room, on a Friday six days ago, and nobody in this room was going to be the second one to ask her anything and nobody after that morning was going to be the second one either. She did not take the pen out. She listened to two men settle a thing about nine steps and looked at the form on the table the whole time, and it was not on the form, it was on the two of them.

The man of about twenty-five had his coat on and his hands in the pockets of it, and nine words he wrote himself are in the inside of that coat, and a man with nine words of his own in his coat does not sit in a room and watch two other men work out what a stair is for. He was not asked anything either.

The second slate was on the shelf behind the table the whole morning with a date on it and nothing under it, and nobody went near the shelf, and the light stopped about nine inches past the near end of the bench and never came any further, which is where the two of them were sitting and where neither of them could be seen from the step at the back.

---

The room emptied at about the fifth hour and the form was on the table where it had been at the seventh, and the bench had not been moved, and the book was still shut. A room in this city has now spent a morning on two things and neither of them was on a form: a woman said out loud that she was not going to ask a man one question and gave the price of the not asking, and two men who have never asked each other anything in a room agreed in about four seconds that one of them would go up nine steps before the other one did.

About four people who came down that stair have worked out since that a room which has a form on it that nobody can fill in has spent a whole morning working out who walks up a stair first, and that a stair is the only thing any of them have that a return cannot be filled in from, and that two men who have said nothing to each other in a room for about a month have just divided it between them without anybody asking either of them to.
