# Chapter 0398 — A Roof That Goes Back Up On A Morning That Is Not Wet

A market in this city has about nine stalls in it under a roof of sailcloth and sacking on four poles, and the roof went down on the sixty-second day of the Bare Month, a Sunday, in a squall, and it has been lying across those boards ever since, and the eighty-third day of that month was also a Sunday and it was not wet, and what was under about four pairs of hands that morning was four poles, a coil of line, a lump of chalk on the edge of a trestle, a bucket, and about nine people who had come to put a thing back the way it was.

---

There is no rule in this city about a roof. A roof that comes down on a wet morning does not go back up on a wet morning, and this one came down on a wet morning and has been on the ground since, and a market roof is the only thing anybody in this flood has lost that cannot be got back by a person going out and looking for it.

It went up the way it went down, which is the only way it knows. The four poles went into the sill and the two ends went first, because the two ends are where the weight is, and the sacking went on over the top of it and the sailcloth went on over the sacking, and the whole of it took about as long as it took to fill a pail twice.

Nobody was in a hurry. That is what a recovery is, and there is no form in four hundred miles with a place for a market putting its own roof back up, and the reason nobody was in a hurry is that every one of them has spent about a month in a room being careful and about four hours of a Sunday holding a pole.

The woman of about thirty-four who keeps the stall nearest the water end had salt out on the boards in three heaps because the boards are swept at the end of a day, and she said it at the head of her own stall the way she does every morning, and what she said was a number.

Nobody asked her for it. A woman who has said a number out loud at the head of her own stall every morning of this flood has never once been asked where it came from, has never once written it down, is not going to be asked, and is not going to write it, and about four people on those boards worked out since that this is the cheapest thing anybody on them owns and that neither of them is going to say so.

"You are not going to tell me what you said."

"**I am not going to tell you what I said and I have not written it down and I am not going to, and you have said a number at the head of that stall every morning of this flood and nobody in this city has ever asked you for it either, and I would like it noticed that the two of us have got the same arrangement and neither of us has ever said so out loud.**"

She looked at the boards and not at him and did not repeat it.

---

The man of about thirty-four who keeps the stall took the near end of the third pole and took it in his left hand.

Nobody said anything about that. A man who has carried a stall up and down nine steps every morning of this flood has been holding things in his left hand since the sixty-seventh day of that month, a Friday, and on that Friday he took a barrow up a wet board on his right and could not close that hand again until he had put it in the cold water at the bottom of the boards about a quarter of an hour before the light went, and he told nobody, and the man of about thirty-eight who was standing at the head of those boards knew and did not say that he knew.

That was sixteen days ago. His right hand has been open and closed and open since, and it does what it is told, and there is a piece of chalk in his own inside breast pocket that he has not taken out since the forty-third day of the Short Month, and he took a pole with the other hand this morning and did not choose which one and nobody in that market worked out which one he chose because the pole was in the air by the time anybody looked.

The man of about thirty-eight worked the boards at the foot of that market and was about nine feet off, and he had his knife in his hand and he watched a man take a pole on his left, and he did not say one word about it, and there are two men in this city who have not said one word to each other about a hand and both of them have said a great deal out loud about a wall, and neither of them is going to.

---

The roof was up by about the ninth hour and the light came under it and lay along about nine stalls for the first time since the squall, and a market in this city opened on the eighty-third day of the Bare Month with a roof on it and about nine people in it, and it did not open on a market day, and a market in this city has opened on a day that is not a market day for the first time in this flood, and nobody is going to know about it in four hundred miles except the nine of them.

The man of about thirty-four put his trestles up two at a time and did not carry anything, and the man of about thirty-one who carries things for a living was not there and has not been in that market since the day he tied a line on a far corner and went up the boards, and nobody sent for him and nobody was going to.

Then the three of them who went down a road on the seventy-sixth day of the Bare Month, a Sunday, are a week out of this city, and about four people who live on that lane have worked out since that the one thing in this flood that has come back up is a roof, and that the wall at the end of a lane with nine boards gone off it is still a wall with nine boards gone off it, and that a page went out of this city on the seventy-eighth day of that month with a name at the top of it, and a book on a shelf has a day in it with nothing under the day, and that the three people on that road are on no page in four hundred miles and have not been asked about anything by anybody, and that a market is a market and a stall is a stall and a roof is a roof, and that this city has got one of the three of them back and not the other two, and that nobody put it back.
