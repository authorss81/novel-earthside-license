# Chapter 0669 — A Keeper Alone With A Book That Holds Two Hands And Cannot Be Read Out

The woman of about twenty-nine who keeps a public register had her hands on the edge of the shelf in the room over that market, beside the book and not on it, with the morning light coming off the one window. Behind the book stood her second slate, up on its edge with its written face to the plaster, and a day was cut across the head of it with nothing whatever under it. It was about the ninth hour on the three hundred and fifty-fourth day of the Bare Month, a Friday, and nobody else was in that room, and nobody came up the stair at any hour of that morning.

The book stood on its shelf with its spine to the wall, and the page of it that was open was ruled into three, and the middle one of the three held five figures put in one under another in the order they were made with a day written in beside each of them, none of the five struck out and no sixth entered. The column on the right of that page carried one word near the head of it and nothing anywhere else on that page, and it had gone in there in a hand that was not hers on a day that is not one of this run of days. The second slate had not been picked up and had not been turned over and had not been written on at any hour of this flood, and the only hand that had come near that shelf was hers.

One word went in the column on the right of that page on a day that is not one of this run of days, and one word went in the one place on a sheet on a day that is not one of this run of days, and the two places are two places and the two hands are two hands, and neither is printed here and neither is spoken here, and the two are never laid beside each other and never in one hand and never compared out loud. The instrument for establishing what a page says was a room, and a date on a door, and a person reading out in daylight, and it could not be used on a page that somebody in that room had already written in, because using it is the telling.

---

Earlier that morning the man of about thirty-one who carries things for a living had come up the stair with his satchel on his shoulder, and he knew what was in it, and he set the satchel down by the bench and left it shut. She had looked at the shut of it and he had looked at the book on its shelf, and neither had asked the other anything, and he had gone back down with the flap still down.

"**A room with nine people in it can say what a page says, and a room with one person in it can keep what a page keeps, and the keeping is not the saying and never was.**"

She said it to the shelf and not to the stair, and nobody heard it and nobody thanked her for it. The door at the foot of the stair carried its date in chalk, and nothing stood under the date, and the wood below it was bare for the width of a hand, and the page and the surface and the room did not agree about anything, and nothing in four hundred miles could put the three of them in one order.

---

She took the pail from under the bench and carried it out to the door and emptied it into the channel and brought it back and put it under the bench where it had been, and the stair stayed empty the whole time she did it, and the water made the same noise going down that it had made every morning of this flood. She dusted the edge of the shelf with the side of her hand without touching the book or the slate, and she straightened the bench against the wall, and she stood at the one window for a while with her hands in her sleeves. The book stayed where it was with its two hands in it, and the slate stayed on its edge, and the stair stayed empty.

"**I have kept this page through a flood and I will keep it through another, and the keeping is standing here while nobody asks, and standing here is the whole of it.**"

She pulled the bench a finger's width from the wall and pushed it back again, and the book did not shift on its shelf. Then she turned her back on the shelf and stood at the one window with her hands in her sleeves until the light was off the sill, and she did not turn round again.
