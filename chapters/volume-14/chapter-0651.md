# Chapter 0651 — A Hand Flat On The Lower Edge Of The Store's Board And A Bare Strip Of Wood Under A Date On A Door

The man of about twenty-seven who washes that board had the heel of his own right hand flat on the lower edge of it at the end nearest the step, and he kept it there while he looked at the face of it from about the height of his knee. He was not cleaning anything and there was nothing in his other hand. It was about the fourth hour on the three hundred and thirty-sixth day of the Bare Month, a Monday, at one end of that market, and the light had not come good on the front of that store.

That board is on the outside wall of a store at one end of that market. It carries three sets of figures of this city's own, none of the three rubbed out, and not one of the three carrying a line of words set under it. It has hung on those two nails since before the water came, and since a thin wedge of wood went under the head of the near nail its top edge has stood level with the sill of that wall.

He has a bucket and a rag and a wall he washed once in the whole of this flood on a day that is not one of this run of days, and he has not washed that wall since. The rag was on the stone lip of that step where he had put it himself the evening before, and he took it off that lip, wiped his palm along it once because there was grit on the palm, and put it back down on the same lip, and it was not inside the trough at any hour of that day. The trough was standing against the foot of that wall with about a foot of water in it out of the night and the bucket was standing beside that trough on the flags.

---

The room over that market has a stair at the back of it and a door at the foot of that stair, and on the outside of that door there is a date written in chalk, and the three hundred and thirty-sixth day of the Bare Month is on it in full. Nothing whatever stands under that date, and the wood below it is bare for the width of a hand.

About eight people go past that door in the ordinary course of a morning. On that Monday about four of them read the date on it and went on down the row, and about four of them did not look at that door at all, and nobody took the number of either of those two, and not one person in that row said a word to another person in that row about what was under that date.

About nine people were in that room over the market when the light came off the one window in it, and not one of them had been sent for anything. That book stood on its shelf behind that bench with its spine to the wall, and the page of it that is open is ruled into three, and the middle one of the three holds five figures put in one under another in the order they were made with a day written in beside each of them, and not one of the five is struck out and no sixth has been entered and none has been taken out. The column on the right of that page is the same width as the one beside it and carries one word near the head of it and nothing anywhere else on that page, and nobody in this city is going to be told what that word is.

Behind that book on the same shelf, standing up on its edge with its written face to the plaster, is a second slate, and a day is cut across the head of it and there is nothing whatever under that day. It did not move at any hour of that morning, and the only hand which came near that shelf was the keeper's.

---

The woman of about twenty-nine who keeps a public register was at the near end of that bench with her own hands flat on the wood in front of her, and a man at the step wanted to know what that door at the foot of the stair was for. He said it once, to the middle of that room, in the ordinary way, and nobody in that room told him and nobody in that room asked the woman at the near end of that bench either.

"**Every one of us walks past it twice a morning and not one of us can tell you what it is for, and that has been the case since the water came, and you are asking me because I am the one person standing still in this room.**"

She did not raise her voice to say it and she did not take her hands off that bench to say it, and nobody in that room improved on it, and nobody argued with it, and nobody thanked her.

The man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall came up that stair at about the seventh hour and stood at the far end of that bench with the board under his left arm and the palm of his right hand flat on the face of it, and he did not turn about.

"I have gone past that door every morning of this flood and I have never once had a reason to stop at it, and there is nothing on the front of it that a person has to stop at."

That went no further in that room than the end of the bench, and a woman at the one window said that it was a door and not a well and that a person can stand at a well.

He got up, put that board down against the end of that bench with the bare back of it out in the room, went and stood at the open door for the first morning since the water came, with his own hand on the frame of it, and looked down at that row for a while, and then he came back in and stood where anybody in that room could see the front of him.

The woman of about twenty-nine said one more thing out loud at about the half hour after the ninth hour, to the near end of that bench and not to him, and she did not take her own hands off that bench to say it.

"**Four of the eight that went past that door this morning read the date on it and four of the eight did not, and not one of the eight of them looked at the wood under it, and that is not a thing I have been keeping from anybody. That door is on the outside of a room that was not built by anybody in this row, and what a person may put on the back of it is not a thing this room has ever been told about.**"

He did not answer that. He stood where anybody in that room could see the front of him for a moment with his right hand down at his side, and then he said one thing back, and it was not about the wood.

"I have never asked you what that room may write on and I am not going to start on a Monday, and there is a piece of chalk in this city that has not been out of a pocket since the two hundred and ninety-eighth day of this flood and it is not mine to talk about."

Neither of those two things was argued with by anybody in that room and neither of them was improved on, and the man of about thirty-four picked that board up off the end of that bench, put it under his own arm again with his own palm flat on the face of it, and went back down that stair, and nobody in that room said one word to him about the pocket he put his own hand into on the top step.

---

Nothing on any surface in this city carries a line of words set under a figure. The one that did is a form with a heading and a place ruled under that heading, and it went out of this city early in this flood, and it is outside this city now and is not coming back, and that is a state of this city and not a silence.

The trestle that board of his own stands against is two stalls along at one end of that market, and that board was against that trestle with its bare back out in the row when he came back down that stair. The piece of chalk in his own inside breast pocket went in there on the two hundred and ninety-eighth day of this flood and came out of it once, on the six hundred and thirty-eighth day of this flood, and it is in that pocket now and it was not out of it at any hour of that day.

---

The washer came back along that row at about the ninth hour with his hands empty and the rag over his shoulder, and the thing that had changed at the end of that row since the fourth hour was that the trough and the bucket had both gone off the flags where they had been standing since before the light.

He put that rag on the stone lip of that step where it had been lying all morning, and he stood at the end of that row and looked at the bare flags where the trough had been, and he did not say one word about it to anybody in that row. Nobody in that row asked him about it either, and nobody in that row said where they had gone, and the two of them were the only things in this city that a person could have moved that morning without saying a word to anybody while doing it.

Two people at that end of that market wanted two different things out of that morning and neither of the two of them got what it was. A woman at a front wanted the trough back against the foot of that wall before the light went off it, and a man two stalls along wanted to know who had taken the two of them down that row, and neither of them got the thing they wanted and neither of them said the word please.

The woman at the front said it to that row and not to him.

"Put it back where it lives before the light goes off it. A trough that walks off on its own in the middle of a flood is a trough that comes back on its own somewhere else."

He said it to the bare flags and not to her, and it was the whole of what he said about it.

"I have not moved it and it is not mine and I am not going to stand here and say where it went, because I do not know where it went either, and I have been at that wall every morning since before the water came."

---

The colour that Monday put in front of about nine people was the colour of wood that has had nothing on it and not the colour of wood that has had a thing rubbed off it and scrubbed at. A man with the heel of his hand flat on the lower edge of a board and looking at the face of it from about the height of his knee was the one who put it there, and he did not know that he had found anything.

A thing that has been kept up and a thing that has been left alone have been standing on the same surface for the whole of this flood, and nobody in this city has ever been shown the person who decided which of the two was which.
