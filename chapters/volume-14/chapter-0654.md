# Chapter 0654 — A Hand Goes Flat On The Bare Back Of A Board That Is Standing Face In Against A Trestle And Two Men At One End Of One Market Who Have Been There Nine Years And Neither Of Them Asks The Other Anything

The man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall had the flat of his own right hand on the bare back of that board where it stood face in against his own trestle with the back of it out in the row, and he kept it there while he looked along that row at the fronts and did not take it off. It was about the seventh hour on the three hundred and thirty-ninth day of the Bare Month, a Thursday, at one end of that market, and there are four hundred yards of flags between that front and the other end of them.

That board of his own has stood face in against that trestle every morning of this flood with the bare back of it out in the row, and it is going to stand that way at the fourth hour tomorrow, and about four people a day go along that row and about four of them do not stop at it.

The piece of chalk in his own inside breast pocket went into that pocket on the two hundred and ninety-eighth day of this flood. It came out of that pocket once, on the six hundred and thirty-eighth day of this flood, and it went back into it the same afternoon without having been used, and it has not been out of that pocket since. He put the flat of his own hand over that pocket at about the seventh hour and at about the eighth and did not open it.

The other chalk in this flood is the store's own. It is in a heap on the flags at the end of that row with about a third of it gone into the channel, and its box is on the step above those flags with the lid of it lying down on the rim of it, and that lid has never shut on any day of this flood. There are two pieces of chalk in this city and they are not one chalk and no hour of that day put both of them in one hand.

---

The man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board came along that row at about the half hour after the seventh hour with his own string over his own shoulder and his own board under his arm, the face of it turned in against his own ribs. There are nineteen rates on it in three columns and one name that is not on it, and he did not put it down at that front and he did not put it up on that trestle.

He stood at about the width of two paces off that stall and said one thing and did not say anything else.

"Two stalls and nine years and neither of us has ever asked the other one thing, and it is not because we have nothing to ask. It is because the asking would be a bigger thing than the row."

He said it to the ground between his own two feet and not to the man behind that trestle, and the man behind that trestle did not turn about.

That was as far as it went in that row, and nobody along it thanked either of them for it, and about four people heard it and about four did not, and neither of those two groups said one word to the other about it at any hour of that day.

---

The woman at the front two along and the man at the front two stalls the other way both wanted that morning to come out a particular way, and neither of them got it, and neither of them said so out loud to the other.

A woman at the front two along wanted that board turned round on that trestle so that a person standing at that front could read the rates off it, and she said so out loud, across the row and to nobody in particular, and the man of about thirty-four did not turn it and told her nothing.

**"It has been that way since the day I took it down and you have been at that front since before the light, and a board nobody can read a price off is the only reason anybody in this row has ever left me alone at it.**"

Not one person in that row took it further and not one of them thanked him for it, and that woman went back to her own front and did not come back to that end of that row that day, and he did not know that and was not told.

A man at a front two stalls the other way wanted to know whether that other man came down that row on the wet mornings as well or only on the dry ones, on the grounds that a man who walks a row with a board under his arm is a man a row can plan a morning around, and nobody in that row told him and nobody in that row told the man with the board either.

---

The man of about thirty-nine said it out loud once more at about the tenth hour, to the face of his own board under his own arm, and nobody in that row had asked him for that one either.

"**Nineteen rates in four markets and nine years and one of the two of us on this row has never asked anybody what the piece of chalk in that pocket is for, and he is not going to be asked about it by me, and he is not going to be asked about it by anybody in this city, and that is not a favour I am doing him, it is nine years of not doing it.**"

He said it and then he stopped, and he did not say a word after it at any hour of that morning, and nobody in that row said one word back to him about it and nobody in that row asked him what was in that pocket, because the pocket was not his and it was two stalls along.

Nobody thanked him for the nine years and nobody thanked the man of about thirty-four for the nine years, and the man of about thirty-four put his own hand flat on his own chest over that pocket once at about the half hour after the tenth hour and did not take it out and did not say one word about it, and the lid of the store's own box of chalk was still lying down on the rim of that box on that step.

At the end of that Thursday a row of people had been standing at two fronts for nine years in the ordinary way, and neither of the two of them had ever once been the reason the other one was asked anything, and that is the longest thing anybody at that end of that market has ever been able to say for themselves.
