# Chapter 0700 — A Morning In Which Every Surface In This City Is Where It Was At The Tenth Hour And A Woman Standing With Both Her Arms In Her Sleeves

Both of the man of about twenty-seven's own hands were on the wet cloth against the face of the store's board, and he took them off it and stepped back off the stone and looked at it, and it was washed once and not twice. It was about the fourth hour on the three hundred and eighty-fifth day of the Bare Month, a Monday. The board was on its two nails on the outside wall of that store at the end of that market, and it carried three sets of figures of this city's own, none of them rubbed out, no words under any of them, and not one figure on it is printed in this city or in any other. He hung the cloth back over the rail at the side of that store's door and put the lid of the box of the store's chalk down on the rim of that box and left it there, and the lid did not shut, and the bucket went down on the flags with its own mouth up the way it stands every morning of this flood.

The trough stood on the flags at the top of the stone by the step where it stands, and the rag was on the stone lip of that step and not inside that trough, and the lid of the box of the store's chalk was lying down on the rim of that box where it lies and that lid does not shut.

The wall above the top edge of that board is a different thing from the board and was washed once in the whole of this flood and by no second person on any day of it, and no hand went near that wall at the fourth hour of that morning or at any hour of that day, and the cloth stopped at the bottom of the board every time it was run.

---

The man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall stood at the front of his own stall two stalls along with his own board leaning against his own trestle and the bare back of that board turned out into that row, and the piece of chalk in his own inside breast pocket was in that pocket and did not come out of it that day and nobody in that row said one word to him about it, and the man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board came up that row carrying his own board, and nobody in that row said one word to him.

The man of about thirty-one who carries things for a living was at that end of that market, with the strap of his bag over his own right shoulder and the flap of it down, 

"**I have carried a page up that road and I have carried a page back down it, and I have not opened either of them, and I am not going to say this morning why I have not, and there is nobody in that room over that market I would say it to if I did.**"

Nobody in that row thanked him for that and nobody said he was right and nobody said he was wrong, and he went on down that row with the bag on his own shoulder, and the rag lay on its stone lip behind him and he laid his own two hands along the top of his own trestle and left them there.

Four people went past that end of that market that morning in the ordinary course of a day, and each of the four of them got the date off the chalk on the outside of that door at the foot of those steps on the way past it, and about four more of them did not look at it at all, and not one of the eight of them went up that stair.

Eleven miles of flats go out from that end of that market in both directions with a channel of wet silt running along the low side of a raised causeway of stone, and the man of about thirty-eight who works at the salt wharf at the near end of them has a board lying face up on a stack of boards with two lines of grey grit across the figure on it that nobody has wiped at any hour of this flood, and that yard is walked past by about four people a day, and not one of the four of them has ever stopped there, and nobody in this city has ever been shown that figure and nobody has ever asked him where it came from.

The man of about thirty-eight who works there came out of his own yard door at about the ninth hour with his barrow empty and his own knife in his belt, and he stood in the doorway and looked along the top of his own stack at the board lying face up on it, and he did not go up to it and he did not wipe it and he did not turn it over, and nobody in that yard said one word to him about it and he said one word to nobody.

Out past the last named house a fence stands along the top of a shut road, sixteen willow posts and eleven withies bound with the same cord, and it has not been measured and no post in it has moved, and four of them had set out together, three of them went down that road with a barrow apiece, and the fourth of them said no, and there is nothing on any one of the three barrows and there has never been and no hand in this city has been on any one of them.

"**Three barrows stand on a road this city is not on, and there is nothing on any of them and nobody in four hundred miles can be shown one person on that road, and I have been at the top of it and I have not been asked what is at the other end of it and I am not going to stand here and guess at it for you.**"

Nobody in that row argued with him about that either. In a house out past the last named house there is a length of new rope over the back of a chair, and it has never been used, and it is not cut, and it is not taken up, and no hand has lifted it off that chair, and the man it belongs to was not asked where that fence is and was not told where that fence is and is not going to be.

---

The one line of words that ever went under a figure in this flood is under a figure on a sheet that left this city and is not coming back, and there is no figure in this city with a line of words under it, and no surface in this city has one put on it, and a place with a number in it and a place with a word in it are two different kinds of place.

The room over that market is up nine steps at the back of the store's end of that row, and the outside of the door at the foot of those steps carries a date in chalk, the three hundred and eighty-fifth day of that month in full, and below the chalk the wood of that door has been bare the whole of this flood, about as wide as a hand, and nothing whatever is on it and nothing has ever been put on it, and there is nothing on that door and nowhere else in this city that says a room over a market ever decided anything at all about that wood, and there is no person in this city that anybody could be shown who learned anything in that room.

The book is on the shelf behind the bench in that room with five figures down the middle of one page and a day entered against each of them, none struck through and no sixth entered, and near the head of the column beside it there is one word, and the hand on that word is not the keeper's hand, and nothing anywhere else on that page. The second slate stands on the shelf behind that book, a day cut across the top of it and an inch of nothing under it, and it has never once been lifted off that shelf by anybody, and it is not on a shelf of its own.

The woman of about twenty-nine who keeps a public register came up that stair at about the tenth hour, and went into that room, and stood in the middle of the floor of it with both of her own hands up in the sleeves of her own coat and her own arms crossed under them, and she stood there, and the book was on the shelf behind the bench, and the second slate was on the shelf beside it, and the door at the foot of the stair carried its date and its hand's width of bare wood, and nobody came up that stair.
