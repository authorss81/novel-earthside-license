# Chapter 0682 — What A Man Says To A Row About A Name On A Surface While Two Men At One End Of A Market Never Ask Each Other A Thing

The board was under the left arm of the man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board and the top edge of it was under the flat of his own right hand, where it had been since the seventh hour, and the nineteen rates on that board stand in three columns. It was about the eighth hour on the three hundred and sixty-seventh day of the Bare Month, a Thursday, at that end of the market, and about four people were going past the two fronts at that end and the light had gone off the flags there.

Two stalls along, the man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall stood at his own front with the bare back of his own board turned out into the row against the trestle he works off, and the face of it was to the wood of the trestle and to nobody. On the step above them sat the box of the store's chalk with its lid down flat across the mouth of it, and that lid has never once shut on that box. At the far end of the row the store had its own board on the outside wall of it with three sets of figures on it that the weather has gone the colour of and not one of them rubbed out, and nobody has put a hand on the wall above the top edge of that board since the one morning the man who washes that board washed it himself.

The foot of the middle of the three columns on that board under his arm is a bare patch about two of his fingers wide, there is nothing in it, and he has never once put his own hand on it.

---

The two of them had been at that end of that market for a long while without either of them ever asking the other one thing, and the row knew both of them, and about four people went past that front every day and the two of them never said a word to each other in it.

He came out from under his own front and stood in the open with the light on the front of him and the board under his arm, and he did not go towards the stall two stalls along, and he did not turn his face towards the man who kept it, and he said it at the volume a man uses in a row.

"**A name on a surface is for somebody who is never going to stand where you are standing, and there is not one of us in this row who has ever asked the one beside him a single thing, and I have been standing at this end of it for a long while.**"

The row took it the way the row takes everything, which is to say that about four people heard all of it and about four of those did not look up from what they were doing, and nobody said anything back and nobody came at him about it afterwards with a question. The man two stalls along heard all of it and did not turn his own board round off the trestle and did not say one word.

The man of about thirty-four went along the front of his own stall with the flat of his hand along the top of the trestle and stopped at the end of it. He turned the box of the store's chalk round on the step so that its lid was not pointing out into the row any more, and he put his own hand flat on that lid and left the hand there a while. Then he picked the box up off the step and set it down at the back of the trestle out of the weather, and his own board went on standing with the bare back of it out to the row, and he went back to the middle of his own front and took his hands off the wood.

---

A man with a sack on his shoulder came up the row, looked at the two of them standing at the front of that row with nothing being done between them, and went on past them both without stopping. The tradesman stayed where he was with his face to the open and his board against his ribs, and about four people went past him, and the two of them that stopped did not stop for long.

Nothing was written down that morning at that end of the market and there was nothing anywhere at that end of the market that a thing could have been written on except three boards, and one of them carried three sets of figures that nobody had ever been told they might put anything beside, and another of them carried nineteen rates in three columns with a bare patch at the foot of the middle one that nobody had ever stood beside him and asked him about, and the third was standing two stalls along with its bare back out to the row and its own owner had been at the front of it all morning with the flat of his hand along the top of the trestle.

He came back down that row at the ninth hour with the board still under his arm and stopped outside the front two stalls along, and he stood there long enough that a woman going past with a basket went wide of both of them.

"**This is the only thing I have got that anybody could ever be shown, and if I set it down in that room over there it stops being that and it is a board on a floor, so I am going to put it in the hands of the man two stalls along and let him be the one holding it.**"

Nobody in that row thanked him for it and nobody argued with him and nobody said he was right. He took the board out from under his arm and turned the face of it round into the daylight and put it into the other man's two hands, and the other man took the weight of it without a word, and the man who had brought it went on up the row past the store with his own arm down against his ribs where the board had been and did not look back at either of them.
