# Chapter 0668 — A Question Put Four Times Round A Room About Which Surface A Person Should Go By

The man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall had the flat of his hand on the bare back of his own board where it stood face in against his trestle at one end of that market. He kept the face of it turned in, and the piece of chalk he did not use was in his inside breast pocket, where it had been put on the two hundred and ninety-eighth day of this flood and had come out once, on the six hundred and thirty-eighth, and had gone back the same afternoon. It was about the eighth hour on the three hundred and fifty-third day of the Bare Month, a Thursday, and the row was full of the noise of fronts opening.

On the outside of the door at the foot of the stair at the back of the room over that market there is a date written in chalk, and the three hundred and fifty-third day of the Bare Month is on it in full. Nothing whatever stands under that date, and the wood below it is bare for the width of a hand. The woman of about twenty-nine who keeps a public register was at the near end of the bench, and behind her the second slate stood on its edge on the shelf, and the column on the right of the open page still carried the one word near its head that was not hers, and she was not asked about either at any hour of that day.

Out past the last named house a fence stands along the top of a road that is shut. It is sixteen willow posts with eleven withies bound on it with the same cord, and it was as it had been that morning. Four hundred miles inland is the town a page came from, and nobody in that room was sent to it, and nobody in it spoke of going. A question went round that room for about an hour that morning, and it was put four times, and it was answered four times by somebody saying they did not know, and it was not put to one person in that room.

---

The question was which of the two surfaces at the end of the market a person should go by, the board with its three sets of figures or the wall above it that was washed once and never since. The first man asked it of the bench, and a woman by the window said she did not know. The second man asked it of the window, and a man by the stair said he did not know. The third woman asked it of the stair, and a man at the far end said he did not know. The fourth man asked it of the far end, and the room said nothing for a while, and then a woman said she did not know. The keeper was not asked, and the stallholder was not asked, and the two not-askings were the only things in that room that nobody remarked on.

"**I have stood two stalls along for nine years with a board of my own, and I go by neither of those two surfaces, and a man who says he does not know which to go by is saying the one true thing in this room.**"

He said it to the bench and not to the keeper, and nobody thanked him for saying it. The keeper kept her hands on the bench, and the second slate stood on its shelf with a day across the head of it and nothing under it, and no hand but hers came near that shelf. The people who had said they did not know sat down again where they had been sitting before the question went round, and the ones who had said nothing stayed where they were, and the bench took all of it.

---

The room did not settle the question, and the question did not settle the room, and the morning went on past the tenth hour with both where they had been. The stallholder lifted his board off the trestle and set it under his arm with the face turned in, and he went up the stair and stood at the back of the room for a while without speaking, and then he came back down.

"**A room that asks four times and hears four times that nobody knows has found out what it came to find out, and the finding is that the two surfaces do not speak for each other.**"

He put his board back face in against the trestle with the bare back out in the row, and the keeper stayed at the bench, and the fence stayed on its road. He left the board where it stood and went back in behind his own front, and the question went out of that room the way a thing goes out of a room when nobody carries it.
