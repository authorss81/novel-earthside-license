# Chapter 0672 — A Handcart Turned Round On Wet Ground At The Top Of A Shut Road By Two Men Who Never Asked Each Other Anything

The man of about sixty had both hands on the two handles of his handcart at the top of the shut road, with the cart standing in the soft place where the road comes up to the fence and its left wheel sunk to the axle in the wet ground. His hazel mallet lay along the seat where he had put it before he started up, its head bound in cord, and a coil of rope lay under the mallet. It was about the sixth hour on the three hundred and fifty-seventh day of the Bare Month, a Monday, and he wanted that cart turned round on that spot and walked back down to his own door before the eighth hour, and he had not turned it yet.

The fence along the top of that road is sixteen willow posts and eleven withies bound with the same cord, and nobody has measured it, and no post stands anywhere it did not stand the day before. Three barrows stand on that road, one to each of three who went down it, and nothing is on any of them, and no hand in this city has been on any of them at any hour of this flood. A party of four is four. This city is not on that road and has sent nobody to it, and the road it goes out along is four hundred miles from the market end to the town at the other of it, and a page came up that road in this flood and a page went back down it, and the man with the cart was not on either of those two days and nobody in this city has ever asked him about them.

The ground at the top of that road had been wet since the night and had not begun to go anywhere. He took his own weight off the left handle and put it on the right and the wheel came up half an inch and went down again, and he took his hands off both handles and stood on the high side of the cart and looked at the way the ground was lying and did not say anything to anybody, because there was nobody on that road to say it to.

---

The man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall came up the row on foot with his board under his arm and the bare back of it out, and he came to the top of the road because that was where the row ends, and he stopped short of the cart and looked at the sunk wheel and the mallet on the seat and the man standing on the high side of it. He did not put his hand on the cart. He did not offer, and he was not asked, and he stood there with the board under his arm and the piece of chalk in his coat where it was that morning, and the man with the cart looked at him and at the wheel and at the two handles and said the thing that had been in his mouth for some time.

The man of about sixty said it, and then stopped, because the man with the board had gone on up the last of the row and was standing at the top of it with his back to the cart and had not answered and was not going to, and nobody on that road thanked either of them.

The man of about sixty took the two handles again and pulled, and the wheel came up and went down, and the cart went a finger's width to the left and stopped with its handle against his own knee, and he let go of it.

What the stallholder did then was not what either of them had been at that spot for. He did not put his hands on the cart and he did not put them on the handles. He came across that short stretch of wet ground to the seat, and he picked the hazel mallet up off the seat by the handle end, and set his own other hand flat on the cart's side to keep it steady while he got it clear, and he stood holding the mallet under his arm with the cord on its head against his ribs and the coil of rope still lying on the seat where the mallet had been.

---

The man of about sixty got the two handles again with nothing on the seat to shift, and he turned the cart on the spot in three goes, and the wheel came up out of the soft place and the other wheel went in and came out, and he walked it down the last of the row on the high side with a man holding his mallet a pace behind him and not touching the cart.

He got it a little way down the row and stopped it there and looked back up at the place where it had been standing, at the soft place and the ruts the wheel had made in it going round, and the mallet was still in the other man's hands and had not been given back yet.

"**A man who will not put his hands on a cart will put his hands on a mallet, and the difference between those two things is the whole of what I got out of the top of that road this morning, and I am not going to ask him which of the two he thinks he did.**"

The cart got it and the man holding the mallet did not answer it, and nobody in that row thanked either of them.

At the near end of the row he took the cart to his own door and stopped it with the handle against the jamb. He put the mallet back on the seat of it, cord head out where it had been, and put the coil of rope over the mallet where it had been under, and took his own hand off the cart and went down the row with his board under his arm. Nobody in that row said one word to either of them about the length of row the cart had just come back down, and he did not go the rest of the way to the market and did not say where he went instead.

The man of about sixty stood at his own door with the mallet in his two hands and the cart behind him in the road and the coil of rope on the seat, and he did not go in.
