# Chapter 0652 — A Satchel Behind A Bench With Its Flap Down And A Man With Nineteen Rates Under His Arm

The man of about thirty-one who carries things for a living had both hands on his own satchel at the near end of that bench, where it stood on the floor with the flap down over it, and he set his hands flat on the top of it and then took them off it again without picking it up. It was about the seventh hour on the three hundred and thirty-seventh day of the Bare Month, a Tuesday, in the room over a market, in daylight, and about nine people were in that room and not one of them had been sent for anything.

On the outside of the door at the foot of the stair at the back of that room there is a date written in chalk, and the three hundred and thirty-seventh day of the Bare Month is on it in full. Nothing whatever is under that date, and the wood below it is bare for the width of a hand, and nobody in this city is going to be told what that strip of wood is for or what it was for.

He has carried four sheets of that shape up that road in this flood and he has never opened one of them. He knows what is in one thing he has carried and he is not going to be asked what is in that satchel, and the flap of it did not go up at any hour of that morning.

That book stood on its shelf behind that bench with its spine to the wall. The page of it that is open is ruled into three, and the middle one of the three holds five figures put in one under another with a day written in beside each of them, and not one of the five is struck out and no sixth has been entered. The column on the right of that page carries one word near the head of it and nothing anywhere else on that page, and behind that book on the same shelf a second slate stands up on its edge with its written face to the plaster and a day cut across the head of it and nothing whatever under that day, and the only hand which came near that shelf that morning was the keeper's.

---

The man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board came up that stair at about the eighth hour with his own board under his arm and the face of it turned in against his own ribs, and he stood at the far end of that bench and did not put it down. There are nineteen rates on that board in three columns, and one name that is not on it, and nobody in that room asked him about either of those.

A woman with a hand-basket had come up that stair behind him and had been at the one window since about the seventh hour. She wanted that room to say one thing out loud that anybody four hundred miles off could be told by a person going up that road, on the grounds that a room with about nine people in it is a thing that can be asked to speak, and she said so twice, once to the keeper and once to the man with the board.

"Four hundred miles off, a word goes on a sheet and one person reads it and one person writes it and nobody in between gets to hold it up, and that is the whole trade of it, and this room has got nine people sitting in it who are the wrong nine to do that with."

Not one person in that room made a better thing of it and not one of them thanked her for saying it, and the woman at the one window went back to her place at that window with the basket set down on the floor between her own feet.

---

Two people in that room wanted two different things out of the rest of that morning and neither of them got what it was. The woman of about twenty-nine took that book off that shelf with both hands under it and set it down on that bench in front of her and opened it at the head of the column that holds figures, and looked down that column from the head of it to the foot of it, and shut it and put the flat of her own hand on the cover of it where it lay. She did not turn that page over and she did not rule another line on it and nothing went into the column on the right of that page at any hour of that day, and nobody in that room asked her what she had been doing for that quarter of an hour.

The man at the step wanted somebody in that room to say who may write on the door at the foot of that stair. He asked it once at about the ninth hour and he asked it of the middle of the room and not of anybody in it, and nobody answered him and nobody told him that he had been told no.

A man at the far end of that bench wanted that book taken off that shelf and put on the table under the one window where anybody could come at it. He said that he wanted a thing that could be looked at by a person who had come up that stair for a figure of this city's own, and the keeper of that book said she would put a hand on that book when there were fewer people standing round it than there were then, and nobody in that room asked her why the column on the right of that page had anything in it.

Neither of them got what it was. That book stayed on that shelf behind that bench with its spine to the wall, and the door at the foot of that stair did not have anything under the date on it at any hour of that morning, and nobody went up that stair to write on it.

---

The man of about thirty-nine said it out loud at about the tenth hour, to the underside of his own board under his own arm and not to that room, and nobody in it had asked him for it.

"**I have traded nineteen rates in four markets for nine years and every one of those nineteen is a thing two people agreed on in front of a row of people, and there is not one of the three of you who can tell me who agreed on the thing that is at the head of the column on the right of that page, and neither can I, and I have not asked her and I am not going to ask her on a Tuesday morning.**"

Nobody in that room said that he was right and nobody improved on it, and nobody in that room thanked him for saying it, and the woman at the near end of that bench did not answer him and did not defend it. The man of about thirty-one who carries things for a living stood at the near end of that bench with his own hand on the strap of his satchel and said one thing out loud at about the ninth hour, before anybody in that room had said a word about a rate.

"Four sheets of that shape have gone up that road in this flood and I have not opened one of them, and a man who trades numbers on a board under his own arm is going to be asked this morning whether he can say where one of them went, and he is not going to be able to say, and I would rather he heard it from me at the ninth hour than found it out on his own at the tenth."

Not one person in that room improved on that and not one of them argued with it and not one of them thanked the man who said it, and the man of about thirty-nine looked at the face of his own board under his own arm and did not answer him and was not asked to.

He said one more thing to the face of that board before he put it back under his arm, and it was not an answer to the man of about thirty-one, and nobody in that room had asked him for that one either.

"**Nineteen rates and one name that is not on it, and I have never once asked you what is under the date on that door, and there is a difference between a thing I have not asked and a thing nobody has asked me, and that difference is the whole of what I have got.**"

He put that board up under his arm again and went back down that stair. He did not say on which day he would trade it in that market again and nobody in that room asked him.

---

At about the half hour after the tenth hour that room held about four people, and the satchel with a flap was still standing at the near end of that bench with the flap down over it, and nobody in that room had put a hand on it at any hour of that morning.

That road out of this city is four hundred miles long and it goes past the last house in this city that has a name on it, and the sheet that went up it is on that road now and not in this city, and the one person in this city who could have told anybody what was on it chose to fold it shut along the two folds it came up that road in and pressed the bone down the length of both of them with the heel of her own hand and told nobody in that room which way the road goes after that.
