# Chapter 0659 — A Hand On The Strap Of A Satchel Behind A Bench With The Strap Pulled Round Twice And The Flap Not Opened And About Nine People In That Room Finding Out On The Morning What They Are Being Asked For

The man of about thirty-one who carries things for a living had his own hand on the strap of his satchel where it stood behind that bench, and he pulled that strap round twice and let it lie, and the flap of that satchel did not go up at any hour of that morning. It was about the seventh hour on the three hundred and forty-fourth day of the Bare Month, a Tuesday, in that room over the market, in daylight, and about nine people were in it and none of them had come up that stair for anything.

He has carried four sheets of that shape up that road in this flood and has never opened one of them. He knows what is in one thing he has carried and he is not going to be asked what is in that satchel, and nobody in that room asked him.

---

Seven notices have gone out of this city since the water came and every one of them was written by somebody in this city sitting down to write it. There is no eighth and there is not going to be one, and nobody in that room wrote one on that morning and nobody in that room said on which day the next one goes out.

The four days a notice gives a room is the cooling, and the cooling is the time a room has to find out what it is being asked, and there is no notice for this morning and there is not going to be one, and nobody in this city has said on which day the next of the seven goes out if there is a next of them, and a room with nothing in front of it finds out what it is for on the morning it is being asked and not on the morning before.

That room was finding that out at about the ninth hour on that Tuesday and about nine people in it were carrying two different things at once, and about four of them were carrying what they had found out and about four of them were carrying something else, and not one word passed between those two groups before the light went off that one window.

---

The man who carries things for a living said it out loud first, at about the seventh hour, before anybody in that room had said anything at all about anything, and nobody in that room had asked him for it.

"**I will carry one more thing up that stair and never know.**"

Take it or leave it, said that room, and nobody in it found anything to argue with in it or anything to add to it, and nobody in it thanked him for the saying of it, and the man at the step said one thing about how a man who does not know is still a man who has carried it, and he said it to the middle of that room and not to the man with the satchel, and nobody answered him.

A man at the far end of that bench said it once more at about the tenth hour, to the strap of that satchel and not to the keeper of the book at the near end of that bench.

"**Nobody in this room is going to ask me and nobody in this city is going to tell me, and I would rather be a man who carried it and never knew than a man who asked at the bottom of that stair, and that is what it costs me and I am not going to be thanked for it because I have not told anybody what it was.**"

Not one person in that room agreed with him and not one of them improved on it, and the keeper of that book did not answer him and did not thank him, and nobody in that room asked her one thing about that satchel.

---

The woman at the one window and the man at the step wanted two different things out of the rest of that morning and not one of the two of them got the thing it wanted.

A woman at the one window wanted that satchel opened on that table so that about nine people in that room could see what had come up that road, and she said so once in front of the middle of that room, and the man with the strap said it to the bench and not to her.

"That flap does not go up in this room and it is not going to go up for the sake of a window, and there is nobody in this room who could tell you what was on it if I opened it, including me."

That was the end of it in that room, and the woman at the window went back to the window with the basket on the floor at her own feet and did not come at that table again that day.

A man at the step wanted somebody in that room to say out loud what that room was being asked for, on the grounds that a thing nobody has said out loud cannot be refused, and nobody in that room said it. Nobody in that room told him he was wrong and nobody in that room walked to that stair with him. He put his own hand flat on the top of that rail at about the eleventh hour and went back down that stair one step at a time with his hand going down the rail the whole way of it. He was still not knowing at the bottom of it, and he went out through that room and down the row, and the strap behind that bench was still round twice and the flap was still down after he had gone.

---

From the light the woman of about twenty-nine who keeps a public register stood at the near end of that bench with the flat of her own hands on the wood in front of her, and she said one thing out loud at about the half hour after the tenth hour, to that room and not to anybody in it.

"Seven have gone out of this city in this flood and every one of them was written by somebody sitting down to write it. There is no more of them, and the price of that is that a room like this one finds out what it is for on the morning, and it has found out on this morning, and nobody has been thanked for it and nobody is going to be."

Nothing in that room argued with that and nothing in it improved on it and nobody in it thanked her for saying it, and the man it fell on was in that room at the ninth hour and was not thanked for it either.

That satchel with a flap went down that stair with its strap round twice and the flap down over it, and seven notices went out of this city in this flood and this one is not the eighth of anything and never was going to be, and a thing a city stops doing does not get better at it by being said out loud in a room by the person it costs.
