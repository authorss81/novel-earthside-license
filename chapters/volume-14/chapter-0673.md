# Chapter 0673 — A Page Left Open Beside A Bench While The Light Comes Off The One Window Onto The Head Of A Column Nobody In That Room Had Been Told About

The woman of about twenty-nine who keeps a public register had her two hands flat on the bench in the room over that market with the book open in front of them at the angle she always opens it to, and the light off the one window lay across the open page and stopped short of her wrists. It was about the eighth hour on the three hundred and fifty-eighth day of the Bare Month, a Tuesday, and about nine people were in that room, every one of them up that stair on some business of their own.

The page in front of her was ruled into three. The middle one of the three held five figures, put in one under another in the order they were made, a day written in beside each of them, and not one of the five struck out and no sixth entered. The column on the right of that page carried one word near the head of it and nothing anywhere else on that page, and it had gone in there in a hand that is not hers on a day that is not one of this run of days, and she had not stopped it and had not been asked about it and has not been asked about it since either. On the shelf behind the bench stood the second slate, up on its edge with its written face turned to the plaster, a day cut across the head of it and nothing whatever under that day; the book was on the bench in front of her and not on that shelf, and the slate was not picked up, not turned over, not written on, and no hand but hers came near that shelf.

The one other word that went out of this city in this flood went into the one place under a heading on a sheet that went back up the road on a day that is not one of this run of days, and it went in a different hand, and it is not in this city, and nobody in this city has been told what it was. The two of them are in two places and in two hands and on two days and she does not put them beside each other in her head while she is working, because the day she does that is the day the room finds out that she has been putting them beside each other, and the room will not be told by her.

She wanted the book shut and back on its shelf before the man who kept the stall two stalls along was at the top of that stair. The light off that window came across the bench at that hour and the head of the column on the right of that page sat in the middle of it, and a man coming into that room with a board under his arm would have that head of column in his eye before he had taken the board out from under it, and she had been thinking about it for about an hour without doing anything about it, which is a thing she is good at.

---

Four people were standing at the far end of that room and about four more were sitting or moving between the bench and the door at the back. A woman at the front said the row was thin and a man beside her said the row was the same as any other day, and the two of them wanted different things out of the morning and neither of them said what they wanted. A man with a crate on his shoulder came to the near end of that bench to put the crate down out of the aisle, and the crate was about as far from the shelf the book stands on as a person can reach and no further, and he put it down and did not put it on the shelf, and the shelf stayed clear.

A woman at the back said that if the room wanted a thing done with a page it could have a slate and write it out and be done with it, and about four people in that room looked at the shelf behind the bench at the second slate standing on its edge with a day cut across the head of it, and nobody went and got it, and nobody said no, and the woman at the back went back to where she had been standing.

"**A room that writes a thing down has to have something to write it on, and that slate has been standing behind that book since before the book was on that shelf, and nobody has ever turned it round, and I have not turned it round, and that is not a thing I am going to do at the eighth hour on a Tuesday.**"

The crate got it and the woman at the back did not, and nobody in that room thanked her for it, and nobody improved on it, and the woman at the back did not ask her why not.

The date in chalk on the outside of that door at the foot of the stair had the three hundred and fifty-eighth day of that month on it in full and the board under the date bare to a hand's width with nothing whatever standing on it, and about four people went past before the tenth hour, went up, came down again, and did not stop at it.

---

At the eighth hour the man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall came up that stair, and the bare back of his own board was out in the room under his arm, and the piece of chalk in his coat did not come out of his coat, and he came into that room and did not go to the far end of it. He came to the near end of the bench and stopped on the side of it where the light was, and he stood there and looked down at the open page, and the head of the column on the right of that page was in the middle of the light and he looked at it.

He did not ask what the column was for. She did not tell him. Her two hands stayed flat on the bench on either side of the book and did not go near it, and the page stayed open at the angle she opens it to, and the light lay across the head of that column in front of about nine people who were all doing something else at the same time and about four of whom were not.

"**I keep that book open at that angle every morning of this flood and I have never once changed it for anybody, and a book open on a bench is a different thing from a book shut on a shelf, and you have just been looking at one of those without being asked to, and I am not going to say the rest of it.**"

It went into the open page and not to him. Nobody in that room thanked her for it, and he did not ask her what the rest of it was.

He said three sentences at that bench after that. The middle one of them was that he had come up that stair every morning of this flood and had never once looked at that page. The last one of them was that he would like it known that he did not learn anything and that he looked. Nobody in that room thanked him for any of the three, and she did not look at him while he was saying them and looked at him afterwards.

Then he took his own board from under his arm, and held it flat in both hands, and looked at the blank back of it for as long as it takes to walk the length of that room, and put it back under his arm with its face still turned in, and went to the door at the back and down that stair.

The man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board had stood at the far end of that room through all of it with his own board under his arm and its face turned in against his ribs, and nobody in that room had asked him one thing.

The man who had come in at the eighth hour with a crate on his shoulder and had put it down out of the aisle came back at the ninth hour and put his own shoulder under the crate and took it up off the floor of that room and went out with it, and did not look at the shelf on his way past it.
