# Chapter 0671 — A Trough Left Brimming At The Head Of A Run Cut Down Four Hundred Yards Of Flags

The man of about twenty-seven who washes that board came down that row before Adrian did, and looked at the flags from the high side of them and put nothing on them, and Adrian came on at the fourth hour with a trough at his feet and a bucket in his hand and the rag off the stone lip and over the bucket's rim, and he went down on his knees at the near end of four hundred yards of flag stone and worked his two fingers into the joint between the first flag and the second and lifted out of it a hand's breadth of wet silt and laid it along the lip of the trough. It was about the fourth hour on the three hundred and fifty-sixth day of the Bare Month, a Sunday, and the light had not come onto the front of the store at that end of the market, and the water in those joints had been standing since the night before.

"**Water in the joints of a stone row gets out on its own by the middle of the day, and the morning that anybody puts a bucket to it is the morning that row stops being a row and starts being a job.**"

Nobody in that row heard it, and nobody in that row thanked him for it, and he went on down the row to the end of it and left the flags alone at any hour of that morning.

He wanted those flags dry by the fourth hour. The water came up out of the bed under the flags faster than a man could lay it into a trough, and by the time he had got the length of his own arm along the first row of joints the trough was heavier than it had been, and the water he had moved had found the next low place and was standing in it. He went on down the row anyway, the same way, a bucket at a time, and wherever he set the trough down he put the rag back on the stone lip of the step, which is where the rag lives and is not inside the trough at any hour of any day of this flood.

At the foot of the stair at the back of the room over that market the date in chalk was on the outside of the door, and the three hundred and fifty-sixth day of that month was on it in full, and nothing whatever stood under that date, and the wood below it was bare for the width of a hand, and about four people went past before the eighth hour and got nothing off it but a date. The store's chalk stood in a box on the step with a lid that did not shut, and the grit under the near side of that rim was about a thumb's width, and the man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall went past the end of the row with his own piece of chalk in his inside breast pocket, where it had gone in on the two hundred and ninety-eighth day of this flood and had come out once since, on the six hundred and thirty-eighth, and had gone back the same afternoon.

---

Adrian came back up the row with the bucket in his right hand and the silt packed down in it, and he emptied it into the trough, and the trough took it and the water in the trough rose to the level of the lip and stayed there. He set the bucket down on the flat stone at the head of the run he had cut, and the rag over its rim went wet through on the side that was facing the water, and he left it there and went back down the row with his hands empty to the near end to start the second run.

A woman with a crate on her shoulder came through that row and went round the trough on the high side of it, the way a person goes round a thing in a road, and she did not say anything to Adrian and Adrian did not look up. A man with a barrow came the other way and stopped for as long as it takes to decide whether a thing in a road is somebody's, and he decided that it was, and he took his barrow up onto the stones above the run and went on, and his barrow wheel left a print in the silt Adrian had laid along the lip of the trough, and Adrian saw the print and did not lift the silt out of it.

Then the stallholder came down the row with his board under his arm and the bare back of it out, and he stopped at the head of the run and looked down the length of the four hundred yards of it, at the trough, at the bucket with the rag over its rim, and at the water standing in the joints, and he did not ask Adrian one thing about any of it, and he stepped over the run on the stones above it and went on down the row with his board under his arm and the chalk in his pocket where it had been all morning.

A woman at the next front said the row was the same as any other day and a man beside her said the row was busy, and the stallholder said nothing to either of them and went on down the row with his face to himself.

---

He came back down that row at the seventh hour with nothing in his hands, because his trough, his bucket and his rag were all at the near end of it and the man on the flags had them. He came to the head of the run and stopped, and what he had to deal with was not what he had left there: the trough was up to its lip and standing over it, and the bucket was on the flat stone with its foot in the water, and the rag over that bucket's rim was black on one side.

He put his own two hands under the lip of the trough and lifted, and the trough came up off the flags with a sound and a weight that a man does not expect of a thing that has stood in the same place every morning of this flood, and he carried it a few paces and set it down on higher stone, and the water that came out of it went across the run and found the bucket and stopped at the bucket.

"**I leave a trough and a bucket and a rag, and I come back to a trough that is full and a bucket standing in its own water, and neither of those two things is a thing a man does on purpose and both of them are a thing a man has to lift.**"

The trough got it and the man at the far end of the four hundred yards did not, and Adrian had his back to both of them and had his fingers in another joint, and he heard it and did not turn round and was not told anything about it.

The washer stood with his hands on his hips for a while and then bent and picked the bucket up out of the run and carried it to the flat stone and set it down on the stone and not in the run, and the rag came off the rim of it in his hands and he wrung the rag out over the bucket and hung it on the lip where it lives.

The water that came up out of the flags had nowhere left to go by then and came back into the run the way it had come into it, and Adrian's bucket stood on the flat stone with its foot in it, and the washer put his own hand flat on the top of his own trough and looked at the bucket and at the water standing round the foot of it, and he set his bucket down in the low place at the head of the run where the water was deepest, and the water came up over the base of it and stood round it, and he left it where it was.
