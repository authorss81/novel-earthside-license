# Chapter 0657 — A Strip Of Wood About As Wide As Two Fingers And Four People Who Are All Right

The man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board had a hand on the strap of that board where it was under his own arm, and he took his other hand off it, turned the board over once with the face of it going down and the back of it coming up, and put it back under his arm the way it had been. It was about the seventh hour on the three hundred and forty-second day of the Bare Month, a Sunday, in the room over a market, in daylight, and about four people were in that room and not one of them had been sent for anything.

The back of that board is not ruled and has nothing written on it. It came out of a room in this city with the face of it ruled in three columns and nineteen rates on it, and at the foot of the column between the other two there is a space about as wide as two of his fingers with nothing whatever in it. One name is not on that board. It was taken off it in his own hand and it has never been said to anybody, and he has been asked about it by nobody in this city at any hour of this flood.

The piece of chalk in the inside breast pocket of the man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall went in there on the two hundred and ninety-eighth day of this flood and came out of it once, on the six hundred and thirty-eighth day of this flood, and went back the same afternoon without having been used, and it is in that pocket now and it was not out of it at any hour of that Sunday.

---

That board is an ordinary surface in this city and anybody in that room could put something on it if the man carrying it let them, and about four people in that room meant four different things about that strip of wood and not one of the four of them was wrong.

A man at the step meant that it should carry a name, because a strip of wood at the foot of a column is where the name of a board goes and he could not think of one good reason for a board to be an exception to that.

A woman at the far end of that bench meant that it should carry nothing at all, because a strip left open on a board is how anybody standing in front of it knows the man holding it wrote the whole of it himself and had nobody else put a word on it.

A man at the end of that row meant that it should carry a figure, and a plain one, because a number in a strip at the foot of a column is a thing a person can do arithmetic on and a name is a thing a person can only ask about. He came in out of that row at about the seventh hour with his own board under his arm. He stood at the far end of that bench and set the lower end of that board down on the boards of that floor and leaned it back against his own leg. He turned the face of it out into the room and held it there with the flat of his own hand on the top of it for as long as it took him to say what he had come to say. Then he turned it back in against his own ribs again and picked it up off the floor.

He said that last one out loud at about the ninth hour, to the middle of that room and not to the man with the board, and not one person in that room improved on it and not one of them argued with it and not one of them thanked him for saying it.

---

The man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall was at that bench with his own board propped face in against the end of it and its bare back out in the room, and he meant that it should carry nothing whatever and stay that way, and he gave his reason for it out loud to the bare back of his own board and did not turn about.

"**A name in a strip at the foot of a column is a thing a person can be asked about at a front on a wet morning, and I have watched a row find out things about a man without ever once asking him, and a man who has nothing written in a strip is a man nobody has to ask anything at all.**"

Not one person in that room said that he was right, and none of them made anything better out of it, and none of them thanked him for the one sentence he said. Nobody in that room asked the man with the board under his arm about that strip at the foot of the column between the other two.

The man of about thirty-nine said one thing out loud at about the half hour after the ninth hour, to his own board and not to anybody in that room, and it was the only thing he said that morning.

"I have traded these nineteen rates in four markets for nine years and every one of the four of you has just told me what belongs in a strip of wood about as wide as two of my fingers, and not one of the four of you has asked me what is not in it, and that is the reason I have never put anything in it, and I am not going to start on a Sunday."

He stopped there and he did not say another word at any hour of that morning, and nobody in that room asked him one thing about that strip, and nobody in that room is going to be told what was taken off it.

---

The man of about thirty-four said one more thing to the bare back of his own board and then he said nothing else at any hour of that Sunday, and nobody in that room asked him which of the four of them was wrong, because none of the four of them was wrong.

"**Four of you have told a stranger in about a minute what belongs in a strip of wood about as wide as two of his fingers, and none of you has ever been told it, and I have not asked on this row in nine years and I am not going to start on a Sunday, and there is not one of the four of you that is wrong about it and there is not going to be anybody standing here who says which of you is.**"

He put his hand flat on the top edge of that board of his own, lifted it off the end of that bench and set it under his arm with the face of it turned in against his own ribs, and he did not say a word after that one at any hour of that Sunday.
