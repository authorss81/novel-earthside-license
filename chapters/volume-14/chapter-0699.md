# Chapter 0699 — A Sunday In A Room With Nobody In It But Her And A Book Taken Down Off The Shelf Behind Her And Opened On The Bench

Nothing at all was on that bench this morning except the woman of about twenty-nine who keeps a public register, who had her own two hands flat on the near edge of the wood, and the bench was bare from end to end, and the second slate stood on the shelf behind it, its day cut across the head of it and an inch of nothing under it. It was about the fourth hour on the three hundred and eighty-fourth day of the Bare Month, a Sunday, and that room was empty.

Nobody came up that stair. Nobody came up it at the fifth hour, and nobody came up it at the sixth, and nobody came up it at the seventh or the eighth or the ninth, and nobody came up it at the tenth, and that room had nobody in it but her from the fourth hour to the tenth hour, and the chapter does not say why, because nothing in that room knows and there is nobody to ask.

The light off the one window came into that room and lay on the floor and came up the leg of the bench, and there were five figures standing one under another down the middle of the page in that book with a day entered against each of the five and none of the five struck through, and there is one word near the head of the column on the right of that page, and it is in somebody else's hand.

---

She stood at the shelf behind that bench and put her own two hands flat on the wood of it, one at each end, and did not touch the slate that was standing on it. It has never been picked up. It has never been turned over. Nothing has ever been written on it, and nothing was written on it that day either, and no hand but her own had gone near that shelf since the day it was put there.

She took that book down off that shelf and held it in her two hands and carried it the two steps to that bench and set it down on the wood and opened it, and it opened at the page it opens at every morning of this flood, and she turned no other page and she wrote nothing in it.

One word stands near the head of the column on the right of that page. Whoever put it there wrote in a hand that is not hers. It has been there since a day that is not one of this run of days. It was put there by somebody else, in that room, in front of about nine people, and she stood at that bench and did not stop it and was not asked why she did not, and she has not been asked since and she is not going to be asked this morning either, in a room with nobody in it but her.

"**There is a word at the head of that column and it is not mine, and I keep the book, and I have been at this bench every morning of this flood, and not one person in this city has ever asked me what that column is for, and I have never once written down a single thing about who was standing in that room.**"

Nobody in that room heard her say that. She said it to the open page and to the bare bench and to the light coming off the one window, and she did not say it twice, and there was nobody in the row below that window who could have heard it either.

---

At about the sixth hour she read down the middle of that page, five figures with a day against each of them, and she put her own right forefinger on the wood at the side of the page and not on the paper and went down it, and then she put her own forefinger on the wood beside the head of that column on the right of that page and stopped there, and she did not go any further along that page, and she did not touch the word that is at the head of that column.

She got up off that bench, and went to the shelf behind it, and stood square in front of that slate with her own two hands down at her own sides, and looked at the day cut across the head of it, and did not put her own hand near it at all.

"**That slate has a day cut across the head of it, and under that day there is nothing, and it has stood on that shelf since before I came to that room, and nobody in this city has ever asked me where it came from or what it is for, and I am not going to turn it over this morning to find out what is on the other side of it, and I am not going to write on it either.**"

There was nobody in that room to hear that. The light off the one window had come up the leg of that bench by then and was lying across the wood at the near end of it, and she stood in it for a while with her own two hands at her own sides and the slate behind her where it stands, and then she came back and sat down at the near end of that bench and opened that book again at the page it opens at.

At about the eighth hour she went out of that room and down that stair and out into the row at the foot of it, and she stood at the foot of that stair on the side of it that the door is on. The chalk on the outside of that door carries the three hundred and eighty-fourth day of that month written out in full, and it is the only mark on that door, and the wood under it is bare for the width of a hand and has been bare the whole of this flood, and she put no hand on that door and she did not touch the chalk and she did not touch the wood under it, and she stood there looking at it for a while and then she went back up that stair.

Nobody else came past that door that morning while she was standing at the foot of it, and nobody came up the stair behind her, and about four people went past that door in the ordinary course while she was inside that room with her own two hands on that book. When she got back into that room the book was where she had left it on the bench, open, and the second slate was where it stands on the shelf behind her, with nothing under the day on it and nothing on the shelf beside it either.

She put the flat of her own right hand on the open page at the head of that column on the right of it, and she did not put a finger on the word that is there, and she shut the book on the heel of that hand, and she left the hand on the cover.
