# Chapter 0656 — Two Hands On A Table Under One Window And A Slate Turned Over Onto Its Face With Nothing Written On It And About Nine People In That Room Working Out In About An Hour That Not One Of Them Was Ever Told

The woman of about twenty-nine who keeps a public register had both hands flat on the table under the one window of that room, and she had been holding them there for a while without doing anything with them. It was about the seventh hour on the three hundred and forty-first day of the Bare Month, a Saturday, in that room, in daylight, and about nine people were in it and not one of them had been sent for anything.

At about the eighth hour she picked up a slate off that table and turned it over onto its face and put it back down on the wood, and there was nothing written on that face and nothing got put on it by putting it down, and the back of it was the same. She ran the flat of her own hand across that face from the near edge of it to the far edge of it, and then she did the same to the back of it. She set that slate down on the wood with its face up and squared to the edge of that table with her own two fingers. It lay there for the rest of that morning with nobody's hand on it. She did not rule a line on it and she did not take a piece of chalk to it and she did not carry it to the shelf behind that bench, and the second slate on that shelf was not moved at any hour of that day and the only hand which came near that shelf was hers.

Behind that bench the book stood on its shelf with its spine to the wall. The page of it that is open is ruled into three, and the middle one of the three holds five figures put in one under another with a day written in beside each of them, and not one of the five is struck out and no sixth has been entered and none has been taken out. The column on the right of that page carries one word near the head of it and nothing anywhere else on that page. Behind that book on the same shelf a second slate stands up on its edge with its written face turned to the plaster, and the only hand which came near that shelf that morning was the keeper's.

---

On the outside of the door at the foot of the stair at the back of that room there is a date written in chalk, and the three hundred and forty-first day of the Bare Month is on it in full. Nothing whatever stands under that date, and the wood below it is bare for the width of a hand, and about four people go past that door in the ordinary course of a morning and about four of them do not look down at the wood at all.

At about the ninth hour somebody in that room asked somebody else in that room what may be written on that door. It was said in the ordinary way, across that room, at about the volume of a person talking about a door, and nobody in that room said it was a foolish thing to ask and nobody in that room looked round at the woman at the near end of that bench while it was being asked.

About four people in that room said they did not know. About four said nothing at all. Nobody in that room answered it, nobody in that room gave a second version of it, and the woman who keeps that book was not asked it at any hour of that morning.

---

The woman of about twenty-nine said one thing out loud at about the tenth hour, to the middle of that room and not to anybody in it, and she did not put her hands in her sleeves to say it.

"**I have been in this room every morning since the water came and about nine of you have looked at me every one of those mornings instead of at that door, and not one of you has ever asked me, and I am telling you now that I would answer it if I could, and I cannot, and that is the first thing I have said in this room in a fortnight that is about that door.**"

Nothing in that room made a better thing of what she said and nothing in that room thanked her for saying it, and nobody in that room asked her anything else about that door, and about four people in that room had it in their own heads before the light came off that window and about four did not.

At the far end of that bench the man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall stood with his own board standing face in against the end of it and the bare back of that board out in the room, and he had been at that end of that bench since about the ninth hour.

He said it to the bare back of his own board and did not turn about to say it.

"**I have said for years that that door belongs to this room and there is not one person in this city I could point at and say that person told me so, and it has taken me until this morning to find out that I have been saying a thing nobody ever said to me, and I am not going to say it in this room again.**"

Nobody in that room agreed with him and nobody in that room made a better thing of it and nobody in that room thanked him for a morning he spent finding that out, and a man at the step said one thing about how a thing nobody can account for usually turns out to have been written down somewhere, and nobody answered him.

---

The slate was still lying face up on the table under that one window at about the eleventh hour with nobody's hand on it, and nothing had been put on it and nothing had been ruled on it and it had not been turned over a second time.

What that room worked out on that Saturday is not that nobody may write on that door. It is that not one person in this city can name one other person who was ever told, and a thing that about nine people have each been certain of for years and that not one of the nine of them can account for is a different sort of thing from a thing that is not allowed.
