# Chapter 0680 — A Bench Wiped Where No Page Had Been While A Room With A Decision In It And Nothing Written Down Looks For Something To Put It On

Adrian Vale had both hands flat on the bench in the room over that market with the wet cloth under the fingers of his right hand, and he was wiping the bare wood on the near side of it from the front edge back towards the middle, and no page had ever been on that part of that bench. It was about the tenth hour on the three hundred and sixty-fifth day of the Bare Month, a Tuesday, and the light off the one window had gone off the bench by then and there were still about nine people in that room.

The cloth came out of the bucket that stands at the end of that room, and the bench has been wiped at the front of it every morning of this flood and never at the middle, and the middle of it is where the keeper's elbows go when she is at that bench, and the middle of it is bare and has been bare every morning of this flood, and there has never been a page on it and there is not one this morning.

The table stands against the wall under the shelf the register is on, and the register stood on that shelf with its five figures one under another in the middle of the page, a day written in beside each of the five, and one word near the head of the column on the right of it that is not the keeper's. The cloth had been wrung out over the bucket and hung over the edge of that table to drip, and it was wet in the middle of it and dry at the two corners, and the man of about thirty-nine who trades on a board was standing at the near end of that room, where the light was, with his own board standing on the floor against the leg of the bench and its bare back to the room, and he had been standing there over the board since the seventh hour.

The door at the foot of that stair had a date in chalk on the outside of it and the three hundred and sixty-fifth day of that month on the date in full, and about four people had gone past that door that day and got the date off it, because the board under the date is bare to a hand's width and carries nothing whatever and has carried nothing the whole of this flood.

---

Seven notices have gone out of this city in this flood. They went out on seven different mornings on seven different sheets and every one of them went out of this city to an office nine hundred miles off that reads a page and puts a name on a paper, and not one of them came back, and the last of the seven went out on a day that is not one of this run of days, and this city has written no notice since, and is not going to write an eighth, and there is nobody in that room who was told not to and nobody in that room who was told to.

He wanted a paper out of that room that morning, and he had wanted it since the day before, and he had it in his head the way a man carries a thing he has carried every working day of his life, and he had not said so to anybody until the tenth hour.

"**A notice is a thing that goes out of a room on a morning, and every one of the seven went out on a morning, and this room has not had one for days, and a room that has decided a thing on a Thursday and cannot get a paper out of itself is a room that decided a thing on a Thursday.**"

The far end of that room got it and nobody in it did, and nobody in that room thanked him for it, and about four people in that room said they did not know what he was talking about and four more of them said nothing, and the man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall had come up that stair at the eighth hour with his board under his arm and gone down again at the ninth, and was not in that room and did not hear a word of it.

---

The woman at the front of that room said out loud that the door at the foot of the stair was the only surface in this city that a person could put a thing on. She said it plainly, in the ordinary voice, and she pointed at nothing while she said it, and about nine people heard her say it, and not one of them said yes, and not one of them said no, and nobody went to the door and looked at it and nobody got a piece of chalk out of anywhere.

Adrian wrung the cloth out over the bucket a second time and carried it to the table and spread it flat on the top of the table, and the water that was still in the middle of it ran to the near edge of the table and came over it onto the floorboards, and the water came across the boards to the bottom edge of that board and stopped there and did not go any further, and the man of about thirty-nine looked down at the water at the foot of his own board and did not move the board.

He came back to that bench with the cloth and put it down on the wood at the near side of it and worked it along from the front edge back towards the middle, and the wood under the cloth went darker and then went back to the colour it had been before he came. He laid the flat of his own hand on the place he had just come off and it was cold there, and the place his hand had been on before that was colder, and he did not lay it on a third place. He took the cloth off the wood and folded it once across the middle and dropped it into the bucket at the end of that room, and it went down slowly and the water in the bucket came up over the fold and stopped there, and the bench was as bare and as wet at the middle of it as the front of it and no part of that bench was any cleaner than another part.

"**I can tell you what a paper does and I cannot tell you where a paper would go, and a man who can do the first and not the second is a man who has been carrying things for a very long time without being asked what a thing is for.**"

It went to the floor. Nobody in that room improved on it and nobody in that room said he was right, and the cloth lay flat on the table with its two dry corners showing and its wet middle pressed down into the wood.

A man who had left a thing on a chair in that room came up the stair at about the tenth hour, and saw the bench and the cloth on the table, and said nothing whatever about either of them, and took his own thing off the chair and went back down, and the chair stayed in that room with nothing on it.
