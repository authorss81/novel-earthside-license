# Chapter 0684 — Both Hands Under The Near Lip Of A Trough Going Out Onto Eleven Miles Of Flats To Make A Fifth Count And Not Making It

The trough stood on the flags by the stone step at that end of the market with water in it, and the man of about twenty-seven who washes that board put his fingers under the near lip of the trough and took it up off the ground, and the water in it went over one side and stopped against the other. It was about the fourth hour on the three hundred and sixty-ninth day of the Bare Month, a Saturday, and the rag was lying on the stone lip of that step and the bucket was standing on the flags at the end of the trough with its own mouth up.

He had washed the outside wall at one end of that market in the whole of this flood and he had not washed it a second time and he was not going to on that morning, and the board on that wall carries three sets of figures of this city's own that he does not touch and that no hand in this city has ever been told they might put a line of words under.

Eleven miles of flats went out from the end of that row in both directions with the water coming off them in sheets, and the row itself was a raised causeway of stone with a channel of wet silt running along the low side of it, and the channel was where the channel was on the day before and on the day before that.

---

Four mornings, somebody in this city walked that row on foot and came back with a count of one kind of thing out of it, and the four counts are all entered somewhere and the four of them do not agree with each other and are not going to be got to agree.

He went out along the row at the fifth hour with the trough on his right hip and the bucket in his left hand and the rag over his right shoulder, and the light came off the flats in a sheet every time the wind got under it, and he put his own face down into the collar of his coat when it did.

"**Four mornings, somebody has walked this row and got a different thing out of it each time and all four of them are written in, and it has fallen to me to make the fifth, and I am not going to make it, because a fifth that agrees with one of the other four is a lie and a fifth that agrees with none of them is a fifth.**"

He said that to the water coming off the flats and not to anybody, and there was nobody else on that row at that hour but about four people walking at the far end of it going the other way, and none of them came near enough to hear a word of it.

Twice on that row the wind got under the water and put it across the stone in a sheet that came up over the toe of his own boot, and twice he stood still until it went down again, and he did not go round any of the four times and he did not turn back at any of them. He put the trough down on the high side of the stone at the two places where the wall of a house came out to the causeway and picked it up again at each of them, and the bucket in his other hand went along at his own side and the rag on his shoulder was wet through by the time he stopped at the near end of that row and had dried on the top of it again.

---

The channel along the low side of that row takes a boot to the ankle in the middle of it and is about as wide as two flat stones laid end to end, and he got to the near end of that row at the seventh hour and stood in it up to the middle of his boot with the trough set down on the high side of the stone and the bucket standing in the water at his own feet.

The man of about thirty-eight at the salt wharf came out of his own yard door at that hour and stood in the doorway and looked at him standing in there. Neither of them said one word to the other, and the other one did not come out of the doorway, and he did not turn round and look at him again. He put his own hand flat on the rim of the trough and lifted it and set it down again further along the stone, and the water that came off the flats filled it and went over the rim and he let it.

---

About four people passed him going back up that row and none of them said anything to him and he said nothing to any of them, and one of them was a woman with a hand-basket and she went past him on the high side of the stone and did not go down into the water at all.

He came back up that row at the ninth hour with the trough lighter than it went out and the bucket in his hand and the rag still over his shoulder where it had been, and he had not taken a figure out of eleven miles of flats and he had not said a word to anybody about what was in the channel at the near end of it, and the four counts that are entered somewhere are still four.

"**I washed that wall at the end of the market once in this flood and I have not washed it a second time and I am not going to, and a man who has done a thing once and stopped is not a man who has failed at it twice.**"

Nobody in that row asked him why he had stopped at it the first time and nobody thanked him for stopping at it, and the man with the barrow had gone back inside by then and his own yard door was shut.

In the low ground where the water comes off the stone between the near end of that row and the market he stopped, and set the trough down in it and left it standing there with the water going over the rim of it. He drew the bucket up off his own hip and took the handle of it in both hands and pulled the rag back over his own shoulder where it had been all the way out and all the way back, and he went on up that row towards the market with the trough behind him in the water and his own two hands on the bucket.
