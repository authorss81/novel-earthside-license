# Chapter 0687 — Two Hands At Each End Of A Bench In A Room With Nine People In It And A Stool Standing In The Dark End

The woman of about twenty-nine who keeps a public register had one hand flat on the top of that bench at the end nearest the window and the other flat on the top of it at the end nearest the stair, and she was clearing it. It was about the fourth hour on the three hundred and seventy-second day of the Bare Month, a Tuesday, and about four people were in the room over that market and the light off the one window had not come onto the bench yet.

The register was on the shelf behind her with its five figures one under another in the middle of the page and a day written in beside each of the five and none of them struck out, and the one word near the head of the column on the right of that page was where it had been put. On that same shelf the second slate stood turned to the plaster with a day cut across the top of it and nothing under that day, and it was not picked up on that morning and no hand but hers came near that shelf.

Against the far wall of that room, in the dark end where the light off the one window does not reach, a stool was standing that had not been standing there before the Saturday, and nobody in that room knew who had put it there or why it was there.

The door at the foot of that stair had the three hundred and seventy-second day of that month on it in chalk in full, and the wood under the date was bare for the width of a hand and had nothing on it, and about four people went past that door in the ordinary course of that morning.

---

Seven notices have gone out of this city in this flood. They went out on seven different mornings on seven separate sheets, and every one of them left this city for an office that reads what it is given and writes a name against it, and the office is nine hundred miles off, and not one of them has come back, and this city has written nothing since and is not going to write an eighth.

"**I have cleared this bench because a room with nine people in it needs the whole length of it, and I am not doing it for a reason anybody could put down anywhere, because there is no page in this room and there is not going to be one.**"

Nobody in that room thanked her for clearing a bench and nobody improved on it, and about four people went on doing what they had been doing and about four moved their own things off the bench because she had cleared it.

She took the two things that had been standing at the middle of it off and put them on the shelf beside the second slate without touching the slate, and she went along the length of that bench with the cloth in her hand and wiped the wood where the book had stood every morning of this flood, and she did not wipe the middle of it.

---

A sheet of floor paper had been rolled up tight and stood against the leg of that bench at the far end and it had been standing there since before anybody in this city began keeping anything. She got her own hand round the roll and lifted it off the stone where it had been standing against the leg of the bench. She carried it the length of that bench and put it down on the wood and unrolled it along its own length until the roll was gone out of it. She laid it in the position it had been rolled at, which was with the near edge of it short of the front edge of the bench and the far edge of it square with the back of it.

Nothing was written on it. It was not a form and it had never been one, and it had not been near a book all morning and it was not going to be near one after that, and the four of them that were about nine people looked at it and about four did not.

By the eighth hour there were about nine people in that room and the sheet was still lying on that bench where she had put it, and a man at the back of the room put his own hand flat on it to lean past somebody and left a print of his hand on the paper and did not notice he had done it, and the paper carried that print for the rest of that morning and nobody wiped it off.

---

"**That is floor paper and it came off this floor and it is going back where it was rolled, and it is not going to carry a single thing on it this morning, and I would rather it carried nothing than carried one thing that was wrong.**"

Nobody in that room asked her what she would have said was right and nobody argued with her about the paper, and the room went on with the morning it had.

A man who had left a thing on a chair in that room came up that stair at about the tenth hour and saw the cleared bench and the sheet lying on it and said nothing whatever about either of them. He took his own thing off the chair, and then he picked the chair up off the floor and turned it on its side and carried it out of that room and down that stair with his own thing under his other arm, and what the room heard last was the chair going down.
