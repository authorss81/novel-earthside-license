# Chapter 0653 — A Length Of Cord On A Gate-Peg That Was There Yesterday And The Face Of A Board In A Bucket

Adrian Vale had both hands on the handle of a barrow at the near end of eleven miles of flats and he was not going to take that barrow any further. It was standing on the beaten ground with its shafts down and there was nothing on it, and the other end of it was against a trough with about a foot of water in it, and the trough was not empty and the bucket was in his own right hand and the rag was over his own shoulder. It was about the fourth hour on the three hundred and thirty-eighth day of the Bare Month, a Wednesday, in weather, out along those flats, and the light had not come good on the tops of them.

At the near gate there is a peg driven into the post, and a length of cord is coiled on that peg and the loop of it goes over the top of the peg and under itself. He put his own fingers under the top of the loop and lifted, and the cord came up off the wood about as far as the thickness of two of his fingers and then stopped, and it stopped because the cord had gone flat and had gone round the peg twice underneath itself and had not been lifted off that peg by anybody in this flood. He put it back where it had been and wound it down again in the same order and it was on that peg at the fourth hour of that day and it had been on that peg at the fourth hour of the day before.

He wanted it off that peg and coiled on the step where a person could find it, and it did not come off, and he did not say why, and nobody at that gate said a word about it.

---

A man came out along that row to fetch a thing and stopped about ten paces short of that barrow and stood there with his own hands at his own sides. He had no business at that gate and he did not say what he had come out for.

Adrian Vale did not put the trough down and turn it over and he did not tip it, and the water in it went on doing what water does in a trough that nobody has tipped out, and he did not answer that at any hour of that morning.

He carried the trough the other way up that row with his own two hands under one end of it and the other end down in the wet, and the bucket came along in his own right hand and the rag stayed over his own shoulder and it was not inside the trough at any hour of that day. A trough, a bucket and a rag are one of each in this city and there is no second set of any of them in it.

---

There are four hundred yards of flags at one end of that market. A person can walk them and stop where they like and nobody in that row is going to be told what the walk is for, and the store's board is on the outside wall at one end of them with three sets of figures of this city's own on it, none of the three rubbed out, and not one of the three carrying a line of words set under it.

Adrian Vale set that trough down on the high ground of that row at about the seventh hour, above where the low ground of it stands under, and set the bucket down beside it, and put the rag down on the stone lip of the step at that end of the row, and went back up that row.

The man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall was behind his own trestle two stalls along at that hour with a board standing face in against it and the bare back of that board out in the row. The piece of chalk in his own inside breast pocket went in there on the two hundred and ninety-eighth day of this flood and came out of it once, on the six hundred and thirty-eighth day of this flood, and it was in that pocket at every hour of that day and it was not out of it.

He wanted to know whose trough that was and whose bucket that was, on the grounds that a trough standing on the high ground of a row a person works in is not a trough, it is a wall.

"That trough is in the middle of the way a person walks to my own front and it was put there by somebody else this morning and I have not moved it because it is not mine."

That went no further in that row than the front of that stall, and the man at the front two along said one thing across the row about a wall of water in a wet morning and was not answered by anybody.

---

At about the ninth hour the man of about twenty-seven who washes that board came back up that end of that row with nothing in either hand. The rag was on the stone lip of the step where Adrian Vale had put it at the seventh hour and it was still there, and he did not pick it up. He stopped where the flags were still dry and looked a while at a trough and a bucket standing on the high ground of that row. Then he crouched down and put his own hand into that bucket where it stood beside that trough and took it out again.

He said it out loud to the bottom of that bucket and not to anybody in that row, and nobody in that row had asked him for it.

"**There is board in the bottom of this bucket. That is washed stone off the face of a board and it has come eleven miles in a bucket and it has gone back again, and that is not water that came out of eleven miles of flats, that is water that has been at the face of a board, and nobody in this city has a second bucket for a person to go and fetch.**"

Nobody in that row said that he was right and nobody improved on it and nobody thanked him, and the man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall did not turn about, and that rag lay on the stone lip of that step until the light came off those flags and after it.

---

What the man who came out along that row to fetch a thing was never told is what was under the water in that trough. What the man who washes that board found out at the ninth hour, with his own hand in a bucket that had stood in the sun on the high ground of that row since the seventh hour, is that the water in it was at the face of a board before it was in the trough. He took his hand out of it and turned it over and looked at what was on the palm of it, and that was the colour of the face of a board, and he had been at that face every morning since the water came. He wiped that hand down the leg of his trousers where the flags were dry and did not say a word about it to anybody in that row.
