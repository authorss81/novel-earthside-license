# Chapter 0157 — The Gate Board

A bowl of hot water and something in it was put in front of a man of thirty-two at about the tenth hour of the morning on the sixth day of the Long Month, eleven miles from the last room he had been in, and he was not asked a question for about forty minutes, and the day is about the bowl and not about the man.

**It is a Monday and there are about nine days of racks to save before the weather turns, and a fire, and a boy of eight, and a woman who did not invite anybody and has not said anybody's name in four hours, including her own, and that last part is not an oversight and it is entered that about four people who were in the room noticed and nobody asked.**

---

He came up the road at the sixth hour with nothing in his hands, which is the first thing anybody from Saltwake has done on that road in a hundred and sixty-one days, and the woman of about thirty-four who keeps a stall by the wharf had walked with him to the bottom of the flats and had stopped there and had said four words and gone back down.

"**She does not want you there.**"

"You have said that before."

"**I have said it in four rooms and I have been wrong in one of them and I am not going to be wrong twice on the same road.**"

---

The kitchen is one room with a fire and a door and a bench by the door and a table with four legs of unequal length. There is a barrow in it. There is a boy of eight in it who is doing something to a rope with a knife and is doing it wrong. And there is a woman at a bench with a board in her left hand, and the board is the gate board, and it is about two feet by one and it is old and it has been repainted so many times that the grain has gone, and there are chalk marks on it that are not this week's and some that are.

She did not look up. She had a knife and a stick of something that was not a knife and she was cutting a length off a length.

"Sit down," she said. "**There is a bowl. It is not for you, it is for whoever is standing in my kitchen, and if you send it back empty my boy will be told that it was the man's fault and it will be true and it will be the first time.**"

He sat down and ate it. It was water and a root and salt, and the salt was from a rack a quarter of a mile away that did not belong to her, and he said so, and she said it was the Reach's and the Reach had not come to argue about a quarter of an acre in a hundred and sixty-one days.

**And then, without looking at him and without stopping, she put a second piece of the same root in the bowl, and did not say a word about it, and did not look up to see whether he ate it, and it is entered by a woman of twenty-four that this was the single most careful act in the whole of the sixth day of the Long Month and that nobody in that kitchen thanked anybody for it, including the person who did it, and that the reason it was careful is that a woman who gives a visitor something she cannot afford is making a claim on him and this woman did not make a claim on him, and that she has been on nothing for nine years herself and knows exactly what it is to have to be given something.**

---

**And for about forty minutes nobody asked him anything, and it is entered by a woman of twenty-four, who was told about it on the Tuesday, that this was the first time in a hundred and sixty-one days that a person in this city had been in a room for forty minutes with nothing asked of him, and that she entered it as a fact and not as a kindness, and that she did not improve on it.**

He watched her work. She did four things and each of them was faster than the one before. She put the boy's rope right in about nine seconds without stopping what she was doing, and she did not explain it and he did not ask. She set a straight edge with her thumb and her eye and no chalk. She counted nine lengths along a rack rail with her fingers — and stopped, and counted them again with the other hand, and said nothing about having done it twice.

**And the board in her left hand had nine chalk marks on it that were not this week's, and four of them were rubbed almost out and five were bright, and he looked at them for about a minute and worked out that they were days and that somebody had been keeping this board long enough for five of the days to still be on it, and he wanted very much to ask, and did not ask, and the not-asking was the only thing he did in that kitchen that he had any practice at.**

**And she used him, once, for about six minutes, without asking him and without thanking him, which is the way of a kitchen.** She put a rail on the end of the bench and said *hold that* and went back to her cutting, and he held it for six minutes and his arms went from fine to bad, and she looked at the rail once, at the end, and said "**Aye**", and nothing else, and that was the whole of what he got in four hours from a woman who has a boy of eight and nine days of racks and a weather turning on Thursday.

**And in the eleventh minute he understood that the board in her left hand was not a thing she was carrying and that she had been holding it since before he came in and would hold it until he left, and that the reason was not that it was valuable.**

"That is a gate board," he said.

"Aye."

"Mine is a slate. It is the ordinary kind."

"Then you have not kept a gate." She went on cutting. "**Nobody has ever held that board in the wrong hand. It is the left, because the right is for the work, and it is not a rule anybody taught me, it is what my arm does after nine years.**"

**And about forty minutes in, the boy of eight asked him a question, which made two in that kitchen, and it was the only one he was asked all day and it was not about him.** He asked whether the man on the upper road was the one who cut the causeway. The woman said no before the man of thirty-two could answer, and the boy said *course not* and went back to his rope, and about four people who were told about this afterwards understood that she had told the boy a lie for the boy's own good and had told him a true one about the man in the room, and that she had not troubled to separate them.

---

**And at about the half of the eleventh hour he said the thing he came eleven miles to say, and he got nine words of it out.**

"There is a question about a term of the—"

He stopped. There was a fire and a boy and nine days of racks and the weather turning on Thursday, and the woman had not asked him a single question in eleven minutes, and he had no right to a twelfth one and he knew it.

"**No,**" said the woman of about thirty-four who keeps a stall by the wharf, later, in a market, to a man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall. "**He did not ask. I want that said, because he came eleven miles and he stood in a kitchen and he did not ask me one thing, and about four people in this city have been telling each other for a year that he asks everybody everything, and he does not, and it is the single most annoying thing about him.**"

"You were there."

"I was at the bottom of the flats. She told me at the gate on the Tuesday and she said the words he said and she said how many there were, and she said *nine*, and she counted them twice, which she does."

---

**And the sentence came in the middle of other work, and it came out of nowhere, and she did not know she had said anything, and it is the best sentence anybody says on the sixth day of the Long Month.**

He was asking, carefully, badly, with his hands, whether the settlement had put anybody's name anywhere near the flats, and whether anybody from the upper road had come down with a slate.

"**There is no order to it,**" she said, and she was cutting, and she was not looking at him. "**And I have been letting water through this channel since before you were tall enough to be trouble, and it will go on doing it whether you can write a page about it or not.**"

She went on cutting. The boy asked her whether the rope was right. She said it was wrong and told him which end.

**And the word in that sentence is *order*, and it is a word a woman of fifty-eight said in a yard in a town nine hundred miles from here on the tenth day of the new month, in a sentence about nine men and a list with no shift attached to it, and that sentence is on the page four times in four hands, and a man from another world arrived at the same word from the other end in the sixth week of this flood and told a hall of about two hundred and forty people that a list of people with no shift attached to it is an order, and neither of them has ever heard of the other, and no person in this kitchen has any idea that the word is older than both of them or that it is going to be in a room in about four months in a form that has a date on it.**

---

**And he did not get what he came for. He did not improve on the nine words. He did not ask a second question, and the reason is a rule he set himself in his own hand with a date on it, and the rule is that he is not to ask one person one question, and the count is a day and a day plus one, and it is a hundred and sixty-two today.**

"I have not asked one person one question in a hundred and sixty-two days," he said, to the boy of eight, at the door, which is not a question, and which the boy of eight did not understand and did not answer and which is entered by a woman of twenty-four in a book in her own hand as the first time he has said a figure out loud to a child.

**And the one sentence she gave him is not a page and it does not travel and it cannot be carried to any office in four hundred miles or nine hundred, and the woman of twenty-four entered it and did not copy it, and the entry says that it is entered and not copied and why.**

---

**And he walked eleven miles back and it is the longest eleven miles anybody has walked in this book, and the reason is not the rain and it is that he was worse off at the end of it than at the start of it, and it is entered by a woman of twenty-four in her own words as a fault in him and not as a virtue, and she did not improve on it: a man with no office has very little practice at being in a room where he is not needed, and he has spent a hundred and sixty-one days being needed, and he did not know until the eleventh minute of a kitchen at Redroot what it costs to be handed a bowl you are only given because you are standing there.**

**And at about the ninth hour of the evening the boy of eight carried the gate board in off the yard and put it down on the bench by the door and went to bed, and it is not on a list of anything, and it has nine chalk marks on it that are not this week's, and nobody in that kitchen saw him do it.**
