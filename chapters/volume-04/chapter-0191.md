# Chapter 0191 — A Yard With Nothing In It

The rota went up on the wall of the yard on the upper road on the evening of the thirty-ninth day of the Long Month, in four hands, as it goes up every evening, and about a hundred and forty people read it standing in front of it before they went to their beds, and not one of them could tell from the paper whether the store was shut in the morning, because the shut was not on the rota and was never going to be on the rota.

It is a Saturday. The store shuts at first light on the fortieth day of the Long Month, and it is a hundred and forty-five days, counted from the seventh week of this flood, since about a hundred and forty people have had a day with nothing on it, and nobody has been told why, and nobody sent for the man who shut it.

---

A rota is a page, and it is a page in four hands, and a thing copied twice is two things, and about nine people in this city have said that since the thirty-first day of the Long Month, and a man of about twenty-nine has written it down in a book of his own that goes nowhere.

He had copied the Monday one and the Tuesday one. He had copied the Thursday one badly, and a woman of about thirty-four in the store had taken it out of his hand and put the right one up without saying a word about it, and he had said thank you out loud, and she had said there was nothing to thank him for.

"**You have copied it four times this month and you are the only one of the four who has not asked what it is for.**"

"**I asked what it was for on the seventh day of the Long Month and you said *does it go*, and I said yes, and that was the end of it.**"

"**That was the end of it and I have been sorry about it every evening since. There is no way to be sorry about a thing for four days without turning into a person who is sorry about things, and I would rather you went back to copying.**"

He went back to copying. The board came off the wall at about the fifth hour of the evening and was washed in a bucket and put up with nothing on it, and the day was not on it.

---

**And the day was in chalk on the door of the store, in the hand that has been at the front of that queue since the seventh week of this flood, and that is the first thing this settlement has put on a door since the day it opened, and a settlement is a settlement made out of pages, and about four people in that yard worked that out in about four minutes and said it out loud.**

"**You have put the one thing that matters on a door.**"

"**I have put it on a door because a door is not a page. A page gets copied and a door does not, and if four people write the shut down onto four pieces of paper then in about four days there is a shut that some of them got wrong, and I have watched a rota do that twice this month and I am not going to let a shut do it.**"

"**And nobody sent for you and nobody asked you and you shut it anyway, and I would like it entered that you did that whether you asked for it or not.**"

"**Then enter it, and enter this beside it, and both halves are mine and neither of them is for you. I have shut this store three times since it opened and twice of them were for a day and once was for two hours because of weather, and this is the first one I have shut because there is a thing on the Reach that I do not know what is going to happen on, and I am shutting it anyway, and I have not made a better reason up on the way here.**"

"**Then say the worse half of that out loud.**"

"**The worse half is that a day with nothing on it is the only thing this store has ever handed anybody that I did not have to pay for, and about a hundred and forty people are going to find that out in the morning, and about nine of them are going to walk eleven miles, and I am not going to tell one of them not to.**"

Nobody said anything to that for about four seconds, and a man of about twenty-nine in the workroom said the only sentence anybody said about it, and it is nine words.

"**That is the first free thing this store has ever given anybody.**"

---

**And a girl of seventeen who is on no roll of anything was in that yard, having come up the road in the afternoon with a slate she wanted to have in her hand in the morning, and she said the number, and it is the only time anybody has said it on the upper road.**

"Ninety-eight."

"Ninety-eight what."

"**Days. The count of days this city has got nothing out of a wall. It is ninety-eight and it was ninety-five on the Wednesday, and it does not go in one on a day the wall says something on. The wall said something on the Wednesday and repeated the number before it and the count did not move, and then it said nothing for three days, and it is ninety-eight.**"

"And the wall."

"**The wall has said nothing since the Wednesday. I am not going to sit here and wait for it to say something, and if it does say something in the morning then the number is ninety-eight in the morning and I would like that written down somewhere, so that whichever of the two of us is wrong can be found.**"

A man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall wrote it on the back of his own hand in pencil and did not wash it off, and said that he was not going to be the third person in this city to wash a thing off his own hand and be wrong about it.

---

**And at about the sixth hour of the evening, nine hundred miles from that yard, in a room with a window that does not open, a man of forty-one in a very good coat was told a figure he had not asked for.**

It came on a sheet with a heading and a date on it and a line that said the district assented, and the figure was not this city's, and the man had asked a different question in the spring and had been told the answer was not available, and the sheet was on the desk in front of him at about six in the evening and nobody in that room would say where it had come from except that it had come in the ordinary course, which is the only way anything has ever come to anywhere.

He read it twice and put it down and asked one question, which was whether anybody was going to do anything about it, and the person who brought it said that it was not his to say, and that a return was a return, and that a return is not a change.

There is no road in this world that reaches that room. Nothing can be sent to it and nothing can be prepared for it. About four hundred miles the other way there is a city where a woman of about thirty-six is a public official and is on nobody's side, and she has been here twice, and nobody has asked her about that sheet, and she has not offered it.

---

**And at about the seventh hour of the evening a woman of about thirty-four who keeps a stall by the wharf started walking, and she had four sentences in her hand and not on anything, and it is eleven miles.**

She had said, in a room over a market, that she would carry it in her hand and not on a slate, and the reason she gave was that a question has to go somewhere and a slate is the wrong shape for a walk, and that she has carried a slate for nine years that she has never written on and was not going to put four sentences on it at the last mile.

A man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall came out of the market behind her and did not say a word for about a mile, and then said nine of them.

"**I am going as far as the top of the road and no further, and I am not going to be asked to come back, and I am not going to be thanked.**"

"**Then walk behind me and do not keep up, and if anybody stops you on that road do not say what you are carrying, because there is no rule in four hundred miles that says a question has to be put on a thing in order to count, and there is no rule in four hundred miles that says a person carrying one has to be a person anybody can find.**"

"**I have been trying to work out what you said to me on the thirty-eighth for two days,**" he said, "**and I have got as far as a woman who is useful is a woman who can be found, and I have not got past it, and I am not going to say the rest of it on a road at the eighth hour.**"

"**Good. And I will say the rest of it on Monday in a room in daylight with a date on the door, and I will say it before anybody asks me anything, and you are not going to like it and you will not be able to argue with it, and I have not decided what it is yet, which is the first sentence about myself I have said in nine years.**"

She walked. The road past the flats is four miles of causeway and then eleven miles of Reach road, and she was on the Reach road by about the eighth hour, and the salt was on four hundred and forty racks on her left the whole way and did not stop drying, because salt does not stop drying, and about nine people were working the racks by lantern and one of them looked up at her and did not ask and she did not stop.

---

**And at about the ninth hour of the evening she came off the road at the bottom of a long cut and onto a low place where the red root grows in the bank, and there was a woman standing on that bank with a stick in her hand who had been standing there since before dark, and about four people in this city have said since that a water-count of the Reed Reach knew at about the sixth hour of the evening that a question was coming up that road and stood on a bank in the cold for three hours to be at the bottom of it.**

"You are late."

"**I walked it. I did not want to get there before whoever it is wants me there.**"

"You have it on a thing."

"**I have it in my hand and it is four sentences and I copied it out of a man's own hand, and I carried it eleven miles, and I did not put it on the slate, and I would like it entered that I could have.**"

The water-count held out her left hand and said nothing, and the four sentences went onto it and not onto anything else, and it is the fourth time in nine years that anything in this city anybody could be stopped from has been put on a person rather than on a page, and the person was the one carrying it.

"You are not going up to the house."

"**I am not going up to the house. And the reason is hers and not mine, and the reason is that she has said every day since the twenty-fourth of the Long Month that she is not going to be asked about it again, and a raft comes up that channel in the morning and a raft cannot be turned round, and if a woman of thirty-four is standing on a step at the ninth hour of a Saturday night then whatever the raft hears on Monday is going to be about a woman on a step and not about four sentences.**"

"And if she is not there in the morning."

"**Then the raft will have come to a low place with about nine people standing on it, and the rule will do what a rule does, and it will take the whole morning, and it will find her, and it will find her because she lives at the bottom of that cut and not because anybody carried her there.**"

The water-count put her hand flat against her own coat and did not write anything down on anything.

"**Twenty sentences is the most that has ever been on my hand and it is four and a half lines and it is in a wet hand. I will be able to read it at first light and I will be able to read it at noon and I will not be able to read it after that, and I am telling you that now so that nobody in this city can say in four days that the question was never on the Reach.**"

**Four sentences on the flat of a wet left hand, held up against a coat on a bank at the bottom of a long cut, and nobody in four hundred miles able to check them in about four days.**
