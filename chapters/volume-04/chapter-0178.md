# Chapter 0178 — Nine Hundred Measures

Nine hundred measures of salt went out of the Reach on a barrow and a sledge and about four people's backs on the morning of the twenty-seventh day of the Long Month, and they went up a causeway of four miles that is four feet wide at the top and took forty days to build, and about nine households on the flats stood at the bottom of it and watched the whole of it go.

**It is a Monday. This is the first load. There has been a store and a queue and a wage and a rota since the seventh week of this flood and there has never been anything in four hundred miles that anybody could buy, and there is one thing on the Reach that a person can sell to somebody nine hundred miles away, and it went up a causeway this morning on a Tuesday's worth of somebody's back.**

---

The salt comes off the shelf in slabs the size of a floorboard and it has to be levered and not lifted, and about nine people have hands that will do it after a week and about twenty-one do not, and it took three hours of about nine people's morning to get nine hundred measures off four hundred and forty racks and onto a barrow and a sledge and off the flats, and the third hour was the worst hour and went wrong twice.

The first wrong thing was that about nine people from the store tried to lever at the speed of about nine people who have been doing it for nine days, and about twenty-one hands on that flat cannot do it at all this week and about nine of them found out at about the eighth hour in front of about forty people.

The second wrong thing was at the causeway, at about the tenth hour, where a sledge went over on the low side about a hundred yards up, and about nine measures went into the water, and nobody said one word about it for about four minutes, and then a man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall said the sentence that about nine people have written down since.

"**That is nine hundred and nine measures instead of nine hundred and about nine measures, and nobody is going to be paid less for it and nobody is going to be paid more for it, and I want it said now in front of about nine people that the loss went into the water and not into anybody's share.**"

"You cannot say that," said a man of about forty-two who had walked eleven miles to be there. "**The share is a share of what comes off the racks. If the line is nine hundred measures and the line is short, the share is short.**"

"Then write nine hundred and nine on the page and let the wage be the same, and I will carry the sledge up the rest of it myself, and you can tell me afterwards that you cannot do that, and I would like to find out today whether you can."

And the man of forty-two could not do it, and said so in about four seconds, and wrote nine hundred and nine on a page in a hand that is not a clerk's with a date on it, and the wage on that page is the wage for nine hundred.

---

**And nine households watched it, and the watching is the chapter, and it was not watching a triumph and it was not watching a defeat, and about four people in this city have said that the reason is that nobody on that flat knows what to do with the sight of nine hundred measures of salt going away, because the thing it does is the only thing anybody has ever been able to do with salt.**

There are about nine hundred measures a week and one buyer nine hundred miles away who has never been on this side of the water, and the settlement is not a trading post and says so itself, and there is no other way out of the Reach flats with a load on it, and any other route is four miles of causeway and forty days.

"So that is the profit," said a woman of about thirty-four who keeps a stall by the wharf, standing at the bottom of a causeway at about the eleventh hour with about nine people around her. "**That is what everything is. Every wage on the upper road, every ration, every bed, the roof over ninety racks, the nine people who came down on Saturday with bars, my slate, the workway, the days, the nine days a week, all of it is nine hundred measures a week going up a road four feet wide to one man nine hundred miles away.**"

"Nobody has said the word."

"**Nobody has said the word because the word is *export* and an export is a thing a place does, and this is a place, and I have been standing here for about an hour and I have worked out that I have been angry at the wrong thing since the first day of this flood and I am not going to be told that by anybody and I have said it out loud so that about four people have to sit with it.**"

"You are not going to be told it," said a man of about thirty-nine who trades on the Open Hand board, from about nine feet away, where he had been for the whole hour without going near the causeway. "**I have been refused by name four times in four years and I stood on a quay for four days in case anything came, and what came was salt, and it came in a sack, and it came on a Tuesday. And I have said what I am about to say four times in four years and I am not going to say it a fifth time in a better place, and if anybody wants it they can go and look at the quay.**"

Nobody went and looked at the quay.

---

**And the load went up the causeway and through the gate, and the gate is at the bottom of the channel, and the days on it belong to a household of three, and about nine hundred measures went through it between the tenth hour and about the fourth hour of the afternoon, and a woman of about thirty-eight who keeps that gate stood at it for the whole of that time and did not stop one barrow and did not say one word to anybody.**

The board was in her left hand. It was in her left hand all afternoon. About four people on that flat have said that the board was in her left hand for the whole of the twenty-seventh day of the Long Month and that the tell is nine years old and that nobody improved on it, and that a barrow of nine measures went past her about four hundred times and that she counted none of them and that this is the only thing she did all day.

A man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall came past at about the fifth hour of the afternoon and stopped, and did not say anything about the day, and she said one thing to him, and it is the only thing she said between the seventh hour of the morning and the seventh hour of the evening.

"**Nine of them came down on Saturday with bars and none of them was sent.**"

"They were not."

"**I know they were not. That is what I have been standing here thinking about, and I have been thinking about it for seven hours, and I have worked out that the whole of the difference between Friday and today is that on Friday nine people came with a sheet and a chalk and a cost and today nine people came with bars, and that neither of them was sent, and that I have no word for the second thing and I am not going to borrow the first one.**"

"You are going to be able to say it again in a year."

"**No,**" she said, and went down and opened the gate, "**I am not going to be able to say it again in a year and you knew that on Friday and you have not improved on it, and now you have said it to me and it is a sentence you said and not one I said, and I would like that entered.**"

---

**And a man of about thirty-nine who trades on the Open Hand board walked the whole of that causeway on the twenty-seventh day of the Long Month, up and back, four miles each way, alone, on foot, on a Monday, and came down off the top of it at about the second hour of the afternoon and said the thing that about four people in this city have said is the sharpest thing anybody has found in a hundred and eighty-two days, and he said it in a yard with about nine people in it and it took him about four minutes.**

He had walked it before. He said so, and he said how many times, and he did not say what for.

"**I have walked it nine times since the seventh week of this flood and I have never once said anything about it, and I have not said anything about it nine times because I could not get past the first nine feet, and on Monday I got past the first nine feet.**"

"Which are the first nine feet."

"**The first nine feet of that causeway are on the low ground at the bottom of a long channel that a man of forty-two took in the seventh week of this flood without asking anybody, and he says so himself in front of about nine people and does not pretend it is a lease, and about four hundred and forty racks of salt stand on it, and there are households on it, and the causeway is four miles long and four feet wide at the top and it was built out of that ground by about forty people who were not from here, and it took forty days.**"

"That is a road on somebody's ground."

"**That is a road on somebody's ground, and it is the only road out of these flats with a load on it, and the ground was taken in the seventh week without asking, and the road was built on it by strangers, and the road is the reason the salt is going anywhere at all, and nobody on this flat has ever put those four sentences in that order before this afternoon.**"

"**And you could close it,**" said a man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall, from a doorway, and everybody in that yard turned round.

"**No,**" said the man of about thirty-nine.

"You built it. You could—"

"**I could put a barrow across it. I could put nine barrows across it and I could do it on a Tuesday and nobody would stop me, and it would be the easiest thing anybody has done on this water in a hundred and eighty-two days, and it would be wrong, and I have known that since the seventh week and I have not said it out loud until today because I have been waiting to find out whether it was wrong, and it is.**"

"Then what is the difference between a road and a gate."

And that is the sentence, and it is in a book in this city in his own words, and he said it once on the twenty-seventh day of the Long Month and has not improved on it and nobody has.

"**A gate is not owned. A household has the days on a gate, and a household gives the days, and a household that gives the days still has the gate, and if you cannot get the days back from the people you can ask the community that grants them and the community can take them back. I have read a page about that twice this week and I have not been in a room about it and I have not been asked anything and I have not wanted to be asked anything.**"

"And the causeway."

"**The causeway is four feet wide and it is on ground that was taken in the seventh week, and the only instrument in four hundred miles that can stop a road is the ground it is on, and the ground belongs to about nine households and a woman of thirty-eight, and a woman of thirty-eight does not own her ground either, she has days, and days can be given back.**"

"So the causeway can be stopped."

"**The causeway can be asked about. That is not the same as stopped, and I have been walking it since the seventh week of this flood to find out the difference and today I have not found it and I am going to go on walking it, and I would like it entered that I did not find it.**"

---

**And at about the seventh hour of the evening, in a yard in this city eleven miles away, a man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall wrote nine hundred and nine on a slate and did not read it back, and about nine people in that yard said that the first thing anybody in four hundred miles has ever been able to buy from the Reach is on a slate in a yard in a market, and the second thing is that the payer's name is on it and the paid man's is not, and that both of those things came from this city and neither of them was told to anybody.**

**And the last thing on the page is a sledge at the top of a causeway of four miles with nine hundred measures gone up it and about nine measures in the water at the bottom, and a gate at the bottom of a channel that is open, and a board in a woman's left hand.**
