# Chapter 0151 — The Nine Miles

The rain came down the upper road for six hours and a man of forty-two walked all of it, and when he got to the step at the back of a room over a market he put a printed sheet into a hand before he said a word.

He had been on the road since the fourth hour of the morning and before that on a cart that would not take him the last two miles because of the water, and his coat was not good enough for any of that and he had not tried to make it look as though it was. The sheet was dry. He had it inside the coat against his chest, and he took it out and put it into the hand of a woman of about thirty-four who keeps a stall by the wharf, because she was nearest the step, and then he said his name and his age and the trade he had last held, in that order, and a room with nothing on the walls got very quiet.

"Jonah Pike," he said. "**Forty-two. Warehouse supervisor, in Leeds, until the sixth day of this flood.** I have been building a settlement on the upper road since the seventh week and it has a name, and the name is on that sheet, and I am going to hand the sheet about before I say anything else, and I want it understood that I am doing that on purpose and not because I am nervous."

"You were in this room on the eighty-seventh day of this flood," said the woman of about thirty-four who keeps a stall by the wharf, "**and then you were not on anything for fifty-eight days, and I counted, and I am not going to pretend I did not count.**"

"I know that you counted," he said.

---

**The sheet is one page and it is printed, and about nine people read it in a room in daylight, and it has a date on it and a figure at the bottom of every line and a hand at the end of every figure, and it is the first page anybody in this city has been handed by a person who came nine miles in the rain to hand it over.**

> **THE WHITE LEDGER, UPPER ROAD. The sixty-fifth day of the month after this one.**
>
> **People: one hundred and forty, counted this morning, and it is counted every morning and the count is on the wall and the wall is outside where anybody in the road can read it.**
>
> **A store, and a queue that begins at the store at the first hour and ends when it ends. A rota, copied by hand, four copies, hung where the work is. A drying line, and salt on it, and the salt is not ours.**
>
> **Wages: a share, a ration, a dry bed, and a place at the front of a queue that works. Paid on the day it is due. The paper money is worth nothing here. It was worth nothing here on the first day and it has never been worth anything and everybody in the store knows it, including the people who took it, and we do not say otherwise in front of anybody.**
>
> **Ground: one hundred and forty acres of the low ground at the bottom end of a long channel, taken in the seventh week of this flood. We did not ask. We are saying so in front of nine people on a Tuesday and we are not going to pretend it is a lease.**

**And the name of the ground is on the sheet, in the fourth line, printed, and it is a name this city has never had in a room, and about four people in that room could not say afterwards whether they had heard it before.**

"I can," said the woman of about thirty-four who keeps a stall by the wharf. "**I can say I heard it, and I heard it about a quarter of an hour ago, off a piece of paper, standing on a step, and that is all I have got.**"

"Then you are the only person in this room who can," said Jonah Pike, "**and I would like it noticed that I put it on the sheet, and that a sheet is a thing a person can be found in, and that I did it anyway.**"

"Why," said a man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall.

"**Because I am a man with a printed sheet and I am not going to stand in a room and describe ground I have taken without naming it, and if that costs me the room then it costs me the room.**"

---

**And the thing that a room could not get past was not the ground and not the wage. It was the fourth line of the sheet, and it took the rest of the afternoon.**

"A man who takes your paper money," said a woman of twenty-four, who is the first public record in this city and is on no roll of anything, "**has been paid in something he cannot spend, cannot carry to a market nine miles away, and cannot leave this world with, and you have said so yourself, unprompted, on a printed sheet, in a room.**"

"I have."

"**Why.**"

"**Because the first man I paid was ashamed of it.**" Jonah Pike put both hands flat on the step at the back, which is not a table. "**He took it in front of nine people and went out of the room and did not come back for a day and a half, and when he came back he asked me whether anybody knew. And the answer was no, because it is worth nothing and it does not weigh anything and nobody in that room could tell you by looking at him. And I decided then that I would say it out loud before anybody took it and not after, and I have said it out loud every day since, and I have never once had a man not come back.**"

"And that is the whole of your honesty," said a man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall.

"**That is the whole of my honesty, and it is a technique, and I am not going to be praised for it in a room, because I worked it out from a man going out of a door.**"

---

**And then the figures went on a board on two trestles in the hall below, for one day, and a girl of seventeen who is on no roll of anything wrote them in a narrow column in her own hand, and the shape of what she wrote was not given to her by anybody.**

She put the payer's name at the top of every line.

**Not one of the names of the people paid went on it.** There were six lines and every one of them ended in a figure and a hand and a date, and the hand was the store's, and above the six figures was one word — *owed* — and there was no name at the top of the page and no name in the sixth column and nobody in the hall asked her for either.

Jonah Pike looked at it for a while and then he said a thing that about four people in that hall understood and two hundred and thirty did not.

"**We did not think of that.**"

"You did not think of it," said the girl of seventeen.

"**We have been writing wages for a hundred days and we have been putting the worker's name on them because we did not think of anything else, and a man in this city worked that out on his own in a market and did not have a page for it.**" He put a finger on the top of the column. "**And we have got it right, and we got it right late, and I would like it entered that we got it right late.**"

"It is entered," said a woman of twenty-four.

---

**And the day went on, and about nine people were in and out of the room from the second hour of the afternoon to the ninth hour of the evening, and there was no table in it and nothing on the walls, and bread was brought and did not go on anything, and the woman of about thirty-four who keeps a stall by the wharf sat on the step at the back with the printed sheet in her lap the whole time and did not let go of it, and at about the fifth hour somebody came up the hill who was not expected, and it was a man of about thirty-nine who trades on the Open Hand board, and he was let in and stood against the shutter with his hat in his hands.**

Nobody asked what the settlement wanted. It came out in pieces over five hours, the way water comes out of a crack, and nobody arranged it and nobody improved on it. **The queue is a queue and it works and it works on a Tuesday. There is water in it. There is a dry place to sit that is not a bed and a bed that is not a favour. The rota is a rota and it is copied by hand. The salt on the drying line is not theirs. And the money is salt, except that nobody in that room had worked out where the salt goes until a man of about thirty-four asked, twice.**

"Nine hundred miles," he said.

"**Nine hundred miles, and there is one buyer, and he has never been to this side of the water and he does not know whether the racks are on the Reach or on the Lock, and he does not care, and I have never met him and I write to him every fortnight.**"

"Then you are not a trading post," said a man of about thirty-nine who trades on the Open Hand board. "**You are an export.**"

"**We are an export,**" said Jonah Pike, "**and it is the only thing anybody in four hundred miles can sell to somebody who is not in four hundred miles, and I did not find that out and neither did you, and about nine people in this room have been standing next to it for a hundred and nine days and did not know it.**"

---

**And at about the ninth hour of the evening, in front of about nine people, with the trestle board still standing in the hall and the bread gone and the light off the west shutter, a man of forty-two answered the ninth sentence.**

Nobody had asked him to. A girl of seventeen had it written out on her own slate in her own hand because she had been in the yard when it was said, and she read the date of it out before she read the sentence, and the date is the thirty-fourth day of the new month and it is the fifty-ninth day of the flood and it is a Thursday, and it is a hundred and thirty-four words long and it is the longest single thing anybody has said in a yard in this city.

**It is about the seventeen. It is that a man of thirty-two has spent a hundred and fifty-nine days making sure that not one of them has a job, that the page listing what they may not have is the longest page in this city, that there is not one thing in it they may do, and that in a month with no money in it one of them is going to be a person with no work in a city with no money.**

"Ninety-six days," said a woman of twenty-four.

"Ninety-six days," said Jonah Pike.

**And he answered it in nine sentences, and eight of them were the best arguments anybody had made in this city in a hundred and fifty-five days, and the ninth one was not an argument at all.**

"**Nine. This is what I have got instead of an answer, and I am giving it to you because it is a figure and a date and a hand and not a feeling.**"

He put his hand flat on the step.

"**We paid nine people this week out of a store, and they were paid on the day, and one of them is one of yours and has been since the day he walked up the road, and he is standing in this hall with his hat in his hands and he is not the only one. The page you wrote is the longest page in this city. It is also the only page in this city that has nothing on it that a person may do, and I have not come here to tell you that you were wrong to write it, because you were not wrong to write it, and if I were you I would write it again tomorrow. And the seventeen are not the people I built this for, and I have never once pretended they were, and the reason is that a person who is on nothing can be sent anywhere, and I have needed that four times this month and I have used it four times, and one of the four was a woman who did not want to go and went.**"

The hall did not make a sound.

"**Ten. And the thing I have not got, and I am not going to pretend to, is a reason why a person who has been on nothing for a hundred and fifty-five days should be glad about it. Your man was right about that in a yard and he is right about it now, and I have not answered him, and I have had a hundred and fifty-five days to think of something and what I have got is a store.**"

---

**A man of thirty-two stood at the back of a room with his hands behind him and did not answer it.**

He did not answer it in four seconds and he did not answer it in four minutes. He looked at the trestle board in the hall, at the six lines with a figure and a hand and a date on each of them and no name on any of them and no name at the top, and then he looked at the printed sheet, which was still in the lap of a woman of about thirty-four who keeps a stall by the wharf and who had not let go of it since the second hour of the afternoon, and he said nothing at all until about a quarter past the ninth hour, when he said one thing to nobody in particular and it was not to the man of forty-two.

"**The names were never on it.**"

"They were never on it," said Jonah Pike.

"That is the ninth sentence answered," said a woman of twenty-four, "**and it is the first time in ninety-six days that anybody has answered it in a figure instead of a feeling, and it is not a good answer, and it is the only one anybody has, and I am entering that a man of thirty-two said nothing for five hours on the sixty-fifth day of the month after this one and that I noticed and that nobody improved on it.**"

**And a man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall, who has paid one day of a wage out of a stall in this city before and who put his own name on the slate and the paid man's name off it, looked at the trestle board for a long time afterwards and did not say anything either, and the reason he did not say anything is entered by a woman of twenty-four and it is that he has been beaten at his own shape by a store and there is no column in this city for that.**

---

**And the woman of about thirty-four who keeps a stall by the wharf went down the hill at about the tenth hour with the printed sheet folded in four inside her coat, and a girl of seventeen on no roll went with her as far as the wharf because nobody had asked her to and she wanted to know what a sheet was worth.**

"It is nothing," said the woman of about thirty-four. "**A sheet is a place a person can be found in and I have been unfindable for nine years and it took a man nine miles in the rain to undo it in one Tuesday.**"

**And a woman of twenty-four wrote the count of this city's silences on the board under the trestle figures, because she keeps one and nobody else in four hundred miles does, and the figure is sixty-one, which is one more than it was on the sixty-fourth day of the month after this one, and a day the wall says nothing on is a silence and a day it says something on is not one however hard the day was.**

**And a form ran out at midnight, and a woman of twenty-four has to write a date on the morrow, and there is no month left to write it in.**
