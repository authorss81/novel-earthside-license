# Chapter 0173 — Nine Years, Three People

A woman of about thirty-four who keeps a stall by the wharf came down the flats on the morning of the twenty-second day of the Long Month and found about thirty people in a rope queue at a gate and a boy of eight sitting on a rail with a length of rope across his knees, and she stood at the back of that queue for two hours without saying anything to anybody, and about four people who saw her do it have said since that it was the first honest thing anybody did on that flat that week.

**It is a Wednesday. The weather is still holding. The board at the bottom of the channel carries the second day of the Long Month in one chalk and the nineteenth in another, and a rail, and a set of knife marks above the rail that somebody cut on Sunday, and the twenty-fourth is not on it.**

---

**And here is the whole of the twenty-second day of the Long Month, and it is three-quarters of a list of chores, and the list is nine years old and it is on the page because nobody in this book is going to be able to find a more interesting thing to write about a household that has kept a channel by itself since before the flood.**

The channel is a foot and a half wide at the top and four feet at the bottom and it goes four hundred yards up off the flats, and it has not been cleared since before the flood, and it is the only thing that carries the water off them, and on the twenty-second day of the Long Month the woman who keeps it cleared about forty yards of it between the sixth hour and the second hour of the afternoon, alone, with a bar, because about nine people were on the racks and a rule that came from a store up the road past this city says a person does not work a queue and a rota on the same morning.

"She could have had the forty yards," said a man of about thirty-nine who trades on the Open Hand board, on the road at the top of the flats, to nobody in particular. "**She could have had four people's worth of it. There are nine people on those racks and there are nine people on this flat who are not on them. And the rule says she is not allowed to have them, and the rule is not wrong, and I have walked four miles this week trying to find the person who wrote it and everybody knows who wrote it.**"

"She is not allowed to have them because she is on the rota," said the woman of about thirty-four who keeps a stall by the wharf. "**And the rota is nine names and four of the nine are not on this flat and two of the four are nine miles up a road and one of those two is a man who cannot read. And she is on it. That is the whole of the answer and I have been arguing about it for two days and I have not got past it.**"

---

**And the rope is the queue's, and the queue is about thirty people long on a Wednesday, and the queue has worked every morning since the fifth day of the Long Month, and the bell on the post is rung by a boy of eight who has been told to ring it and does not know why, and on the twenty-second day of the Long Month the boy was not ringing it because he had been given the day off.**

**And the gate has been on no list for nine years, and the woman who keeps it has never asked to be put on one, and about four people in this city have understood that about her and one of them has understood it in the wrong direction.**

He had been given the day off by a woman of about fifty-nine who keeps a house and a boy of eight at Redroot and who is his grandmother, and she gave it to him on the Tuesday night, and she had not asked anybody.

"He is eight," she said, to a woman of about thirty-four who kept a stall by the wharf and had come about four hundred yards up off the flats on a Wednesday morning for no reason anybody established. "**He has had a day off on Thursday and he does not know yet and I am not going to tell him until the morning, because if I tell him on Tuesday he will spend Wednesday thinking about Thursday and Thursday is the day and that is most of a day off to a person of eight.**"

"And tomorrow is the night before," said the woman of about thirty-four who keeps a stall by the wharf, and then stopped, and put her hand flat on the rail of the gate, which is the tell that a woman of thirty-eight has and that a woman of thirty-four has borrowed from her by standing next to her on those flats for about eight days, and neither of them has ever said so.

"**Tomorrow is the night before,**" said the woman of about fifty-nine. "**And I am not going to pretend that is a different thing from what it is. I gave him the day off because he is eight and because in about nine days there is going to be a great deal less of him in this house, and I did it in advance on purpose and I would like it entered that I did it in advance and that I did not ask anybody.**"

Nobody entered it. It is entered here.

---

**And the boy of eight spent that Wednesday exactly the way a boy of eight spends a Wednesday, and nobody in this chapter improved on him and about nine adults arranged a great deal of their own morning around it, and here is all of it.**

He had the rope. He had had the rope since Sunday. It was about nine feet of it and it was not the queue's rope and it was not anybody's, and he was doing something to it that four different people asked him about on that Wednesday and that he answered identically four times, and about four adults on that flat found out what it was in the first minute and let him get on with it.

"**Knots,**" he said.

"What sort."

"**A sort where it does not come undone.**"

He was at the rail, sitting on it, from about the sixth hour to about the fourth hour of the afternoon, in the sun, with the gate behind him and about thirty people waiting in a rope, and he made eleven knots in nine feet of rope in about ten hours, and one of them came undone about the third hour of the afternoon and he did it again, and about a woman of about forty-four who cannot get a rack above her shoulder sat down on the ground beside him at about the ninth hour because her back was finished and did not say one word about the rope for three hours and then said one thing about it, and it is the only thing anybody said to him all day that was about him.

"**That is not the sort that never comes undone,**" she said. "**That is the sort that comes undone if somebody is in a hurry. I have seen the four Elms make that knot for a hundred years and I have untied it for about forty of them and I am not going to tell you which end you start at because you have four more days to work it out.**"

"**I have got tomorrow,**" said the boy of eight.

And that is the whole of what he said that was about himself, and it is on the page, and about four adults on that flat heard it and about nine did not, and the woman of about thirty-four who keeps a stall by the wharf heard it and stood still for a while and then went away down the flats without saying anything to anybody, and the finding of the twenty-second day of the Long Month is in nine words and it is hers and it is nobody else's, and it is this: **a household organised around a gate has no hour at which anybody is off, and the only person in it who has ever had an afternoon off is eight years old and has been given one by his grandmother, in advance, in secret, and nobody thought about it for nine years.**

---

**And the household has three people in it and two of them have been on a wage for about a fortnight, and the third has a bed, and the arithmetic of that household has not been done by anybody including the three of them, and a woman of about fifty-nine who keeps the house did it out loud on a Wednesday afternoon at about the fourth hour because she cannot read and has been keeping a household's figures in her head since before the flood.**

"**There are nine years of racks in this household's history and a woman of thirty-eight in it who has been paid nothing for any of them. Since the seventh day of the Long Month there has been a wage on a Tuesday out of a store and it comes to this house and it is enough, and I know it is enough because we have not gone anywhere, and we have not gone anywhere because a woman of fifty-eight who cannot read worked out in about four minutes on the seventh day that a wage which arrives on a day is worth more to a household than a wage which is owed for nine years, and she said it out loud in a kitchen, and nobody argued with her, and she is not in this room and she has not been in this house.**"

"And the boy."

"**The boy has a bed because that same wage pays for it, and he has had a bed since the second month of this flood and I have not once asked anybody where it came from, because I did not want to know, and I have worked out since that not wanting to know was the most convenient thing I have ever done in nine years of keeping a house.**"

"You want to know now."

"**I want to know on Friday at about the ninth hour, and I am not going to ask anybody before then, and I would like it entered that I have gone nine weeks not knowing on purpose and that I am not asking a single person in the meantime.**"

---

**And at about the fifth hour of the afternoon about nine people came to that gate who had not been in the queue, and every one of them wanted the same thing, and none of them said it, and she came out of the channel with a bar in her hand and put herself at the gate and answered about nine of them in about four minutes.**

They wanted to know what she was going to say on Friday.

"**On Friday somebody from a store up the road past this city is going to ask me for the days on this gate, in a room, in daylight, with a date on it, and about nine people will be in it, and I am going to answer them.**"

"Are you going to say yes."

"**I have not decided and I am not going to tell nine people on a Wednesday what I am going to decide on a Friday, and there is one of me and there are nine of you, and if I tell you now you will go away and tell somebody and I will have decided in about nine households instead of in one.**"

Somebody at the back said *everybody knows*.

"**Everybody knows what a man of twenty-nine read out in a kitchen yesterday. Everybody does not know what I am going to say, and the difference between those two things is the whole of what is on this flat today.**"

---

**And the last thing on the page is the gate board, and the woman who keeps the gate picked it up at about the seventh hour of the evening because the rain had come up off the water and would take chalk, and she carried it in and put it on the bench by the door, and it is not on a list of anything, and it carries two dates in two hands and a set of knife marks, and the twenty-fourth is not on it, and it will still be on that bench on Friday morning when about nine people are going to be standing in that kitchen.**

**And there is one more thing on the page and it is nine years old and nobody improved on it, and it is this. There is a record kept by the Reed Reach of who may use a water gate. It has been kept for nine years. Every name on it is alive. It is the only list in four hundred miles that has never once been wrong about a person, and a woman of about thirty-four who keeps a stall by the wharf has read a copy of it and has said out loud, in a yard, on a Wednesday, that she does not know whether that is a good answer and has stopped waiting to find out. And the woman who keeps the Redroot gate is not on it, and has never been on it, and did not ask to be, and a woman of about thirty-nine who trades on the Open Hand board said the reason out loud on the road at the top of the flats at about the seventh hour of the evening and nobody improved on him either, and it is this: *the day her name goes on that list the gate stops being hers and becomes the Reach's, and she has been keeping it for nothing for nine years on purpose, and nobody has ever once asked her to explain that, and I am not going to be the first.***

**And on the back of that board, on the bench by the door, there is a boy's handprint, and the print has been there since the ninth day of the Long Month, and nobody has washed it off, and he put the board down on the bench and went to bed and nobody saw him do it, and he has never been asked about it and is not going to be.**
