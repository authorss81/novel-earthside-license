# Chapter 0156 — Salt

There are four hundred and forty racks on the flats at the bottom of the Reach and they belong to about nine households, and about nine of them have been drying salt for a man from another world since the seventh week of this flood, and on the fifth day of the Long Month, which is a Sunday, a man of about thirty-nine who trades on the Open Hand board walked out onto them at the fifth hour of the morning because nobody in this city had ever gone and looked at what that store actually sells.

**He had been standing on a quay since the ninth hour on the Wednesday before, in case anything came. Four days. Nothing came. He has been refused by name four times in four years and he did not tell anybody he was standing there and a girl of seventeen on no roll found out on the fourth day and entered that she had found out and had not said anything for a day and a half.**

---

The racks go in rows down to the water and they are two hundred years old or they are made in the shape of two hundred years old, and the wood is silver, and each one is about the height of a tall man and the width of two, and they stand on two legs in the silt with the salt on a shelf between them, and the salt on the shelves is white and hard and comes off in flakes like something that has been burned.

**The thing that undoes a person standing on them is not the scale. It is that every rack is full and has been full for eleven weeks and the man who owns it is not poor.**

"That is a good year," said a man of about sixty-two who owns eleven of them and whose name is not in that room.

"It is a good year everywhere on the flats."

"It is not a good year. It is an ordinary year and I have had four of them and two of them were worse." He put his thumb on a rack leg. "**The salt is not better. It is the same salt I have been drying since I was nine. What is different is that somebody nine hundred miles off has written to me twice this year and has paid on a day.**"

---

**And the finding of the fifth hour of the morning is the finding of the day and it was made on foot and it took four hours and it is dull, and dull is the problem.**

The salt goes out on a causeway. The causeway is four miles. It took forty days to build. It is four feet wide at the top and it is shored on the low side with about nine hundredweight of stone that came up the road in a barrow by hand, and it goes from the flats up off the bottom of the channel to a hard standing above the flood line, and there is a cart on the hard standing and the cart is not a cart of this world and it has two wheels of a kind nobody here makes, and there is a sledge for the last half mile because the last half mile is not a road.

**There is no other way out of the Reach flats with a load on it.** The Reach is water on three sides and mud on the fourth for nine miles in a wet season. There is a channel, and the channel goes to a gate, and the gate belongs to the Reach, and the Reach is not a road and cannot be walked with a barrow.

"So the salt goes out through a gate," said the man of about thirty-nine who trades on the Open Hand board.

"**The salt goes out through a gate and any other route is four miles of causeway and forty days of building, and about four hundred and forty racks is the whole of the reason that causeway exists.**"

---

**And he walked the causeway, all four miles of it, on the sixth hour of the morning, alone, and it is the least dramatic thing in that room and the reason the day works.**

The first mile is bank and gravel and it is wide enough for two barrows. The second mile is shored on the left with stone and the shoring is good work and not this world's work, and the stone is squared and the joints are tight and somebody who is not from here was taught to square stone, and he has not said who. The third mile crosses a soft place where the fill has settled about a foot and the causeway has been raised once already and the raising is visible from both sides as a long low shoulder. **The fourth mile is the sledge**, and the sledge is a set of two runners on a platform and it is pulled by about nine people and it takes about four hours for half a mile because it is loaded to a limit that a person from this world would not have chosen and he asked why and nobody knew.

At the top there is a hard standing of about four square yards and a cart on it that is not of this world, and a stack of woven things the size of a loaf, and about nine people, and a slate on a post with a date on it and a figure at the end of it and a hand.

**The slate on the post has a date on it. It is the fourth day of the Long Month and it is a Saturday and it says what is loaded and what is owed for loading, and it says the word *owed*, and it has the settlement's name at the top of it, which is the first time in four hundred miles that anybody has put the name of a body at the top of a wage page and the finding of that is entered separately and is not this day.**

---

**And then he did the second thing, which is the thing he came for, and it took him from the flats to a room over a market in about six hours and it is on the page because he said it in daylight with a date on it and did not describe anybody.**

Nine people were in it and a man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall had put the table back in and a man of forty-two from another world was not in it and had not been in a room since the sixty-fifth day of the month after this one.

**He said the nine sentences. A girl of seventeen on no roll of anything counted them on her fingers and did not write the number at the top of anything, and it is entered that she counted them twice and got nine both times.**

> **One. I will tell you where the money comes from in nine sentences and I am not going to describe anybody while I do it.**
>
> **Two. Four hundred and forty racks on the flats, and about nine households own them, and they have been drying on that ground since before the flood.**
>
> **Three. About nine of those racks are drying salt for a man from another world, for a wage, and the wage is a share and a ration and a dry bed and a place at the front of a queue that works.**
>
> **Four. The salt goes out on a causeway of four miles which took forty days to build and is four feet wide at the top, and it was built by the settlement and not by the Reach.**
>
> **Five. It goes to one buyer and he is nine hundred miles away and he has never been on this side of the water.**
>
> **Six. That is the whole of the money. There is no other money in it, and I have been standing on a quay for four days in case anything came and this is what came.**
>
> **Seven. It is lawful, and nobody has broken a rule to dry it, and I want the room to notice that I went looking for a rule for two days and there is not one.**
>
> **Eight. The racks stand in the channel and they drain it, and the channel takes its water from a gate.**
>
> **Nine. And there is one set of racks that dries, and I am not going to say which gate, and I know what that costs this room and I am going to come back to it.**

---

**And he was asked by a man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall, before he had got the ninth sentence out of his mouth, why he had not said which gate, and he gave the reason and the reason is his own and it is the reason he did not name a household either, and it is the reason he has not described a single person in nine sentences.**

"**Because a description is a removal with a column for where the person is.** I have been on a board that goes down a river four times a year since I was thirty-one and I know exactly what a paragraph about nine people does to those nine people in about four months. **And there are about nine households on these flats and about nine of them have never been to a market in this city and have no business in this city and have not been asked about anything, and I have been asked nothing and I am not going to describe them to a room to make a room feel better.**"

"You have just told this city where its money comes from," said a man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall.

"**I have told this city where a store's money comes from. That is a road and a buyer. It is not anybody's name.**"

"Then what have you told us," said the man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall, and he asked it the second time, because the first answer had not been an answer.

"I have told you the shape of it," said the man of about thirty-nine who trades on the board. "**And the shape is worse than the story. The story was that a man from another world was taking ground off a wetland. It is not a story. It is a man from another world paying a wage on a day to nine people, and there is no rule against him doing that, and I have looked.**"

---

**And then a woman of twenty-four said the thing that the whole of this winter turns on and she said it without stopping for breath and she was not the person anybody expected to say it.**

"**Then he cannot own it.**"

"He is not trying to own it," said the man of about thirty-nine who trades on the board.

"**He does not have to. A gate is not a thing anybody can own, and I have known that since I was a child and so has everybody in this room. And a settlement that needs water has to be given it. And being given it is a thing that can be asked for.**"

The room did not make a sound.

"**That is the whole of the fifth day of the Long Month. Not that he stole a gate. That there is a thing he cannot take and has not tried to take, and it is the only thing on the flats that is not a matter of work, and it can be asked for.**"

---

**And the man of about thirty-nine who trades on the board did not improve on the nine sentences, and he did not take any of them back, and he went back up the nine miles on the Sunday evening with a blister on his heel and said nothing to anybody about what he had said in the room for two days, and on the second of those a woman of about thirty-four who keeps a stall by the wharf asked him at a gate, and did not wait for the answer to the first half.**

"You did not say which gate."

"I did not say which gate."

"**Then somebody has to go and find out, and it is going to be a woman who keeps a gate at the bottom of a channel and who is not going to be told anything by anybody from Saltwake, and I am telling you that now so that you do not come up the road with a question and a page.**"

**And the last thing on the fifth day of the Long Month is four hundred and forty racks with salt on them and a causeway of four miles standing empty in the dark, and about nine hundred measures of it dry and paid for and stacked, and a settlement on the upper road that has not been told that any of this is now known.**
