# Chapter 0159 — A Warden's Duty

A woman with a board in her left hand was putting a line in the ground at the bottom end of a channel at the sixth hour of the morning on the eighth day of the Long Month, and by the seventh hour there were about thirty people on the wrong side of it and a settlement workroom behind her.

**The ground has been held since the seventh week of this flood. The line went in on Sunday morning in about eleven minutes with a chalk and two pegs and it is now three days old and it has to be re-cut every day or it goes, because this ground is silt and the water comes up under the chalk and not over it.**

---

The line is not a wall. It is a scratch about a foot long in nine places, and nine pegs, and a board at the end of it with a date on it, and the whole of it took eleven minutes and about four people.

The first hour was the best hour of the day and about nine people have told people since that it was the best hour of the day, and nobody has improved on it, and it is entered that this is the first time in a hundred and sixty-three days that a thing done in this city has been called the best hour of the day by more than one person, and that the men who were on the near gate of the Lock of Salt heard about it two days later and did not believe it and that a man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall told them it was true.

**And at about the seventh hour there were about thirty people on the wrong side of the line, and the trader who was standing eight feet back with his hat in his hands worked out how they had got there, and it took him the rest of the morning, and it is the whole of what this day is for.**

About nine of the thirty were the settlement's. They had a rota, a slate with their names off it, and a wage at the end of the day, and a man of about twenty-nine from the workroom who came down on the Tuesday and had read a cost out to nine people and had been stopped four times and had told them that a Warden must defend the place.

About twenty-one of the thirty were the Reach. They had been told there would be a queue and a wage at the end of it. They came at the sixth hour because somebody had said sixth hour. **And the two groups stood in the same line, and the line was not a roll, and there was no column anywhere in it with the settlement's name in it and the settlement's name in it, and at about the ninth hour a man of about twenty-nine from the workroom came down the line with a slate and could not tell which nine of the thirty were his, and did not ask, and went back up, and a man of about thirty-nine who trades on the Open Hand board saw him do it and has not described it to anybody since and is not going to.**

---

**And the people the line went through were not the settlement's and not the trader and did not know there was a line at all until it was in the ground, and that is the part of the day that the settlement's workroom never heard about and that the trader thinks about more than the rest of it.**

There are about nine households on these flats. The line is a hundred and forty yards and it goes across the working end of two of them, which is where the salt is scraped off the boards and stacked, and a hundred and forty yards of a hundred-and-forty-acre holding is a scratch. A scratch is a scratch. And a household of about sixty-one came out at the seventh hour to ask what it was, and was told by a man holding a spade that it was a line, and asked who had drawn it, and was told that, and asked what the line was for, and was told that too, and said nothing at all and went back to the house and came out again at about the eighth hour with a barrow and put herself on the end of it and dug for two hours beside the settlement's people without saying another word.

She was still there at the seventh hour of the evening. Nobody asked her to be and nobody entered her and a man of about thirty-nine who trades on the Open Hand board dug four feet away from her for nine hours because he could not think of a reason not to, and his hands are cut and that is on the page.

**It is entered that nobody in four hundred miles can say whether that household agrees with the line, is indifferent to it, is afraid of it, or has not worked it out, and that asking her is the only thing that would establish any of the four, and that about nine people wanted to ask her and that a woman of about thirty-four who keeps a stall by the wharf said in a gateway that the asking would be a removal with a column for where the person is, and that she is right, and that it also means the answer is not going to be had.**

**And the fourth hour's failure was a real failure and it lasted as long as a bank takes to fill, and it is the reason the rest of the day went the way it did.**

The line runs along the top of the cut bank and at the fourth hour a man of about forty-one who had put his spade in at the wrong angle took a foot of the bank out and about nine feet of the new line went into the channel with it, and the water came up through the hole in about a minute and a half and there was silt in nine people's eyes inside ten seconds.

**A man of about thirty-one with a hand that will not close put his boot in the hole and stood on the edge and did not take it out, and about nine people shouted and one woman swore and the man of thirty-one stood on the bank at the fourth hour of the afternoon on the eighth day of the Long Month with his boot in a hole that was taking the channel and he did not move for about a minute and a half, and it was a woman of about sixty-one with a barrow who came along the line afterwards and got him off it, and he has not made a fist since the twenty-fifth day of this flood and the hand was open the whole time and it is on the page because it was on the page.**

"The bank takes the channel," he said, afterwards, to nobody. "**Not the other way. A bank does not hold water. A bank holds what is behind it and that is all, and nobody has ever taught that to anybody because everybody who knows it is dead or is on the other side of a rope.**"

---

**And the day went on, and it was hot for the eighth day of the Long Month, and about thirty people dug a line about a hundred and forty yards long out of silt with nine spades and a barrow and a bucket, and the work was badly done and it got done, and by the seventh hour of the evening the line was four inches deep in nine places and a foot deep in one, and a woman with a board in her left hand walked it and did not say anything about the one that was a foot deep, and chalked a mark on the board beside it, and chalked nothing on the ground.**

**And here is what nine people discovered on the eighth day of the Long Month and about thirty-one people did not discover, and no instrument was used, and nobody lied, and there is no form anywhere in four hundred miles or nine hundred.**

A person who has taken a groundkeeper's workway can be made to hold ground by nothing more than the taking of it. Nobody compels her. Nobody pays her to stand on a line. Nobody asks her to refuse to move. **A duty to a claimed place does all of that, and it did all of that in a day, on silt, with a chalk, and the settlement did not take the ground. The settlement gave away a duty, and the duty took the ground.**

---

**And the man of about thirty-nine who trades on the Open Hand board did not put it on a board, and did not describe it, and did not report it to anybody, and there is a reason and the reason is his and it is the only thing in that room he actually decided.**

He told one person, at a door, in daylight, on the Wednesday evening. It was four miles down the Reach road and it took him two hours to walk and he arrived at about the tenth hour with his hands cut and a blister on his heel and a woman of about thirty-four who keeps a stall by the wharf was on the step and looked at his hands first and asked him about the blister before she asked him anything else.

He said it in about nine seconds. **A person can be a wall, he said. That is the whole of what I came to say and I am not going to improve on it, and you are going to ask me who and I am not going to tell you, and there are about nine of them and about twenty-one more and I cannot tell you which is which, and neither can the woman who came down the line with a slate.**

"Put it on a board," said a man of thirty-two who is on no roster of anything.

"**No.**"

"That is a fact about nine people—"

"**A board goes down a river,**" said the man of about thirty-nine who trades on the Open Hand board. "**It has gone down a river four times a year since I was thirty-one and I have never once been able to find out what a clerk did with one, and I am not putting a woman who is standing on a line in a piece of paper that goes down a river, and if you write it down it goes down a river too, and I would rather it was in your head where it cannot travel.**"

"**Then it is a fact nobody can check.**"

"**Then it is a fact nobody can check,**" said the man of thirty-nine, "**and a fact nobody can check is a page nobody can be angry at, and that is the best protection either of us has ever heard of. And I want you to notice what it is the same as. It is the third sentence of that exchange of ours from the seventh day of the new month, the one nobody can be bought out of, and that sentence is also a thing nobody can check and nobody can be angry at. And it is not a duty. And I have been trying all day to work out why those two are the same and I have not worked it out and I am not going to pretend to.**"

---

**And a man of thirty-two who holds nothing did not write it down either, and the reason is his own and he gave it without stopping and then did not improve on it.**

"Because I have been putting things on pages for a hundred and sixty-three days," he said, "**and every single one of them has travelled, and I know that because eight of them came back wrong.**"

---

**And at about the seventh hour of the evening on the eighth day of the Long Month a man of forty-two came out of a workroom door on the upper road, onto the line, and looked at it.**

He looked at it the whole time it takes to look at a thing like that. He did not say anything about it. He did not touch the chalk and he did not speak to the woman with the board and he did not go down the line. He looked at about thirty people's worth of work and then he went back inside, and the door shut, and a man of about twenty-nine from the workroom stood in the yard for a while afterwards and then went in too.

**And a girl of seventeen who is on no roll of anything was standing out on the flats with a slate under her arm and the settlement's mark cut into the corner of the base of rack nine, and she had the chalk in her other hand, and she did not write it down.**

"I am not going to write this down," she said, to nobody.

**And a woman of twenty-four entered that she noticed, on the flats, on the evening of the eighth day of the Long Month, that a girl of seventeen had a chalk in her hand and a slate under her arm and chose the slate, and she entered that she noticed it, and she entered that she did not improve on it, and she entered that she has noticed about nine things in a hundred and sixty-three days and has not improved on any of them, and that the count of nine is the same as it was a year ago.**

**And the last thing on the page is a line a foot deep in one place out of nine, cut into silt at the bottom end of a long channel, held by a duty that nobody was compelled to accept, and a hundred and forty yards of it, and a chalk mark on a board in a woman's left hand, and a man of forty-two who looked at it from a doorway and went back inside.**
