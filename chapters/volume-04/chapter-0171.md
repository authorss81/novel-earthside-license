# Chapter 0171 — A Gate With a Household Behind It

About nine people were standing at the bottom of a channel on the morning of the twentieth day of the Long Month who had not been standing there on Sunday, and not one of them had come up the road past this city, and one of them was a woman of about fifty-two who digs and had come four hundred yards up off the flats and could not say afterwards why she had come.

The weather is still holding, and it is going to stop holding on Thursday night, and the ninth day of the racks is done and about nine hundred measures of salt are standing on four hundred and forty racks in the sun. The date is on a board, on a hand and on two slates, and it is Monday the twentieth day of the Long Month, and there are four days of it.

---

They had all come for the same reason and not one of them would say the reason out loud, and the not-saying took most of the morning, and it was the loudest thing on that flat.

The board is at the bottom of the channel where it has been since the second day of the Long Month, and it is two feet by one and it has been repainted so many times that the grain has gone, and it carries the second of the Long Month in one chalk and the nineteenth in another, and the two dates are under a rail so that the rain does not take them, and above the rail somebody had cut a third set of marks with a knife because chalk goes.

**And the twenty-fourth, which is Friday, is not on the board, and about nine people learned that on Monday morning by standing in front of it and reading the two dates on it and doing the sum themselves.**

"Aye," said a woman of about thirty-four who keeps a stall by the wharf, who had come down with a barrow she did not need and had been at the board at nine feet for about a quarter of an hour. "**That is the arithmetic of it. Nine people have stood in front of that board since the seventh hour and about four of them can do that sum and the other five came to ask somebody to do it for them.**"

"And you did it for them."

"**I did it for three of them and I sent the other two away, and the reason I sent them away is that I am not going to be the woman at the board. A board on the flats is a thing anybody can walk up to, and about nine of them have walked up to it, and by the second hour of the afternoon it will be a thing anybody in four hundred miles can walk up to, and there is not one thing you or I can do about that which is worth the doing.**"

"You put the date on it," said a man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall.

"**I did not. He did,**" she said, and nodded at the man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall, who was the one who had it in pencil on the back of his own left hand where anybody on that flat could read it, and who had entered that he had done that on the Sunday and not on the Thursday. "**And I would have taken it off him on Monday morning if I could have got to him, and I am not going to pretend otherwise, and it is the only thing I have done this year that I am proud of and it is a thing I would undo.**"

---

**And then a boy of eight came out of a door at about the seventh hour with a length of rope in his hands, and the day is partly his, and the reason is that the woman who keeps the gate had told him something at about the sixth hour and had done it herself and had asked for it to be entered that she did it herself, and it is entered.**

She is about thirty-eight and she has a board she holds in her left hand and she is holding it now, and she has been keeping the channel at the bottom of that cut for nine years and she has been paid nothing for it for nine years, and on the seventh day of the Long Month she took a workway from a store up the road past this city, and the fourth line of that workway was read out to her in full and in daylight with a date on it before she said yes, and she said yes in nine sentences and put the board down on a table and did not pick it up again.

She told the boy at the sixth hour on Monday, at the bench by the door, in about four minutes, and here is the whole of what she told him, and she told him in that order and did not put it in a better order afterwards.

"There is going to be people in the room on Friday."

"For the water."

"**For the water.**"

"How much water."

"**All of it. All of it, from now to the end of the season.**"

The boy of eight considered this. He is eight. He had a length of rope in his hands and he had been doing something to it since Sunday and it was not a thing anybody had asked him to do.

"Whose water," he said.

"**Ours.**"

"**Whose ours.**"

"**The gate's. Ours while we keep it.**"

"Then somebody has to keep it," he said, "**and you are the one who keeps it, and I have been carrying the board.**"

"**You have been carrying the board.**"

"For how much."

She did not answer that, and about four adults on that flat, who were not there and would not have admitted to listening if they had been, would have told you that the reason was that **a quarter of a gate, and a day and a half of a Tuesday of a week of a month,** does not have a way of being said to a child of eight, and she was not going to invent one at the sixth hour in the morning.

"**Thursday night,**" she said. "**I am going to tell you the rest of it on Thursday night, and I am going to do that myself, and it is going to be late.**"

"All right."

"**And you are not going to be asked anything on Friday, and if anybody asks you anything, in any room, in any building, you are going to say that you are eight and you live here, and then you are going to come and find me. That is the whole of what you have to do and it is not a lot.**"

"Somebody might ask me something."

"**Then you come and find me.**"

---

**And the boy of eight went back to the rope, and the rest of that Monday is not about him, and nothing that happened on that flat for the next four days improved on him, and about nine adults arranged their whole morning around a thing a child was told to do and he has been asked twice what it is for and has told them both, both times, that he does not know.**

He got a blister on his left palm at about the ninth hour and did not stop, and about a woman of about forty-four who cannot get a rack above her shoulder told him to stop and he did not, and she told him to stop twice, and on the third time she said the thing that got him to stop, which was that if his hand went he could not carry the board and the board was the only thing he was good for.

"That is not a good reason," he said.

"**It is the only reason anybody has got on this flat, and I have watched three other people try to make him stop and I am the fourth.**"

---

**And at about the eleventh hour of the morning a man of about thirty-nine who trades on the Open Hand board came down the flats on foot, alone, the way he does, and he was asked four times in an hour who he was looking for and he answered the same way four times, and the answer is his and it has not changed since the first month of this flood.**

"**I am not looking for anybody. I am standing on a road. You would stop too.**"

He stood by the channel for about an hour and watched about thirty people clear silt and did not go into the water, and then he said one thing to a man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall and it is the only reason he came.

"**There are nine people on this flat who have decided this week that the store up the road past this city is right, and they have decided it on the channel, and not one of them has read a page, and I have asked four of them why and three of them said the same sentence and the sentence is about a drain. A channel that four hundred and forty racks drain is the settlement's problem and the racks are the households' income and both of those are true at once. I am not saying they are wrong. I am saying they arrived there on their own in nine days and I did not put it there.**"

"You are saying the settlement is winning."

"**I am saying the settlement has not started trying and I do not know what that is called and I have been on this flat for an hour and I have not found a word for it and I am going to go and find one.**"

---

**And in the city, eleven miles away, the same Monday went the way Mondays go, and a man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall said a number out loud in a market and about nine people heard it and about two hundred and forty who could hear it did not.**

A man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall is the person in this city who made the shape of a wage page, and he has a slate, and he writes on it when he is told not to, and on Monday he wrote two figures on it and did not read them out.

"**Ninety,**" he said, to nobody, in a market, on the twentieth day of the Long Month. The figure is his own day less the eighty-fifth day of this flood, and the first of March was the eighty-fifth day of this flood, and thirty-nine of forty men under the near gate turn up every morning and have not been paid any of the ninety. "**I have said that number in this market about four hundred times and I have stopped saying it, because the number does not do anything. It is the correct number and there is no instrument in four hundred miles or nine hundred that takes a number and turns it into a wage, and I wrote that sentence on a page in this city in the second month of this flood and it has not got better.**"

A man of about thirty-one with a hand that will not close was standing at the near gate when he said it, and he said the only thing anybody says about it that has ever been any use.

"**Then do not say it. Go and stand there in the morning and be one of the thirty-nine, and it will still be ninety, and they will still know that you know.**"

"I have been barred from every roster at my own request."

"**I know. I am not asking you to be on one. I am asking you to be a man standing in a place, which is free, and which I have not seen you do.**"

---

**And what there is to say about boards is fifty-three words long and it belongs to a woman of about thirty-four who keeps a stall by the wharf and she said it to a man of about thirty-four who keeps a stall on a flat at about the sixth hour of the evening with the light going off the water.**

"**A date is the only thing anybody in this world has that travels on its own.**"

"How is that."

"**A thing spreads because a person carries it and a person is findable, and a board is not a person, and eleven miles of this Reach knew that date by the second hour of the afternoon today, and nobody chose the eleven miles. Not the meeting-ground and not the store and not her.**" She put her hand flat on the board and left it there. "**And I am not going to say that is a good reason to take it down either, because a thing that is findable is not a thing that has been taken, and I have spent nine years making sure of exactly that, and I am not going to be the woman who takes a date off a board in a village on a Monday.**"

---

**A boy of eight is working a knot that is not for tying anything up. Nobody has asked him what it is for and nobody is going to.**
